## Translator's Note

### 1. Text base

The English text is prepared from the facsimile and OCR-Volltext of the first (and only) edition of Sehling's *Die evangelischen Kirchenordnungen des XVI. Jahrhunderts*, Band I, published by O. R. Reisland at Leipzig in 1902. The working source is the digital facsimile at the Heidelberg Academy of Sciences' DigiHadw platform (record `eko1`; DOI [10.11588/diglit.26586](https://doi.org/10.11588/diglit.26586)), which supplies both page images and OCR-derived plain text. Sehling's own pagination is preserved throughout the endnotes as `[S.N]` markers to allow cross-reference to the German critical edition.

Band I is the first volume of Sehling's landmark twenty-four-volume critical corpus of the sixteenth-century Lutheran territorial church orders (*Kirchenordnungen*). It bears the internal designation *Erste Abtheilung, Erste Hälfte* — "First Section, First Half" — because Sehling originally envisioned the whole edition as two divisions, the first covering the Saxon–Thuringian heartland of the Reformation and the second the outlying territories. The complementary "Zweite Hälfte" of the First Section is Band II (1904). The two-Abtheilung scheme was not extended by Sehling's successors, who instead numbered the later volumes serially.

### 2. What Sehling supplies

Sehling's 1902 apparatus is preserved intact and translated in full: his editorial preface (*Vorrede*), his introductions to each territorial subdivision, his source-critical notes on the transmission of each individual order, his dating discussions, and his indexes (chronological, personnel–geographic, and subject). Sehling's numbering of the church orders is retained; his footnotes are rendered as numbered endnotes at the close of each Part.

The sixteenth-century *Kirchenordnungen* themselves — the actual constitutional and liturgical documents issued by Luther, the Ernestine and Albertine electors and dukes, and the associated territorial councils — are translated as period legal instruments, in an English register that mirrors their public and public-liturgical character. Where the orders quote Scripture, we render the German Bible text as it stands in the order (which usually, but not always, reproduces one of the Luther Bible editions current at the time of promulgation), with a modern chapter-and-verse pointer in the endnote for readers who wish to consult a standard English Bible.

Liturgical Latin — Kyries, Introits, Collects, Post-communions, hymn incipits — is preserved verbatim in the body. English translations of stock liturgical formulas are given in the endnotes on first occurrence per document.

### 3. What the Scholastic Lutherans translation team has added

Only in endnotes:

- Modern-critical editions of texts the orders reference (WA for Luther's occasional writings; BSLK / Kolb–Wengert for confessional documents; standard editions for canon law and patristic citations).
- Modern chapter-and-verse pointers for scriptural quotations, where the order gives only a book name or an older versification.
- Biographical dates for persons named in the orders.
- Brief clarifying notes on obsolete technical vocabulary — territorial-legal terms (*Landesordnung*, *Konsistorium*, *Superintendent*, *Visitationsartikel*), currency and measure names, defunct place-names and jurisdictions.
- Cross-references to parallel material in other volumes of the Sehling corpus.

Nothing has been added to the body text. Sehling's own voice and the sixteenth-century voices he edited stand on their own.

### 4. Editorial conventions

Following the Scholastic Lutherans program conventions established in the Praetorius, Chytraeus, Selnecker, Kirchner, and Flacius volumes:

- **Territorial names** appear in German on first occurrence with an English gloss in parentheses (e.g. *Kurfürstentum Sachsen* — the Electorate of Saxony), and in English thereafter. Long-Englished names (Saxony, Thuringia, Franconia, Palatinate) are used in English throughout.
- **Personal names** follow Sehling's spellings, which are the historical German forms. Where the modern English convention differs (Melancthon for Melanchthon, Luther for Luther), we retain Sehling's form.
- **Roman numeral part / paragraph headings** within individual church orders are preserved and not renumbered.
- **[Square brackets]** in the body always mark editorial insertion, whether Sehling's or ours; the distinction is disambiguated in the endnote on first occurrence per document.
- **Greek and Hebrew** in the source (rare in this volume) are preserved in the original script with transliteration and a brief English gloss on first occurrence.
- **AI assistance:** the translation is prepared by Cherinet Demeke under the Scholastic Lutherans team name, assisted by Anthropic's Claude Opus model. Every rendering has been checked against the source. Where the OCR is corrupt or Sehling's editorial reading is contested, we say so in an endnote.

### 5. What is not yet in this working translation

This is a working English text, released tranche by tranche to the Scholastic Lutherans team's private repository. The following are not yet complete:

- The bulk of the body text (Luther's orders, Ernestine orders, Albertine orders, and Sehling's introductions to each) — see the tranche list at the head of the notes file.
- The three back-matter indexes (chronological, personnel–geographic, subject) — deferred until body-text translation is closed, since the index entries reference paginated body content.
- Cross-references to Bände VI/1 (Braunschweig-Lüneburg 1955) and XVI (Baden-Württemberg II 2004), where later Saxon and Thuringian material was back-filled — pending completion of those volumes.

### Note on the volume title

Sehling's German uses the older orthographic form ***Abtheilung*** (rather than the modern ***Abteilung***) and the older ***Ernestinischen*** and ***Albertinischen*** endings appropriate to the pre-1901 orthographic reform. We render these as their standard English equivalents (**Section**, **Ernestine**, **Albertine**) rather than perpetuating the archaism, but preserve Sehling's own spelling verbatim in the transcription block above.

The designation ***Erste Hälfte*** ("First Half") reflects Sehling's original two-part conception of the *Erste Abtheilung*; the "Zweite Hälfte" is Band II (1904), covering the geistliche Gebiete (the former ecclesiastical territories), the Harz and Henneberg counties, the Erzstift Magdeburg / Halberstadt, and Anhalt. The two-Abteilung scheme was quietly abandoned by Sehling's postwar successors, who continued the series with straightforward serial numbering (III, IV, V…) rather than a "Zweite Abtheilung" for the outlying regions.¹

## T1b · §I — Sehling's *Vorwort*, Section I

### *The legal order of the evangelical Church*

*Sehling's editorial preface (*Vorwort*, pp. V–XXI), Section I (pp. V–VIII, corresponding roughly to lines 66–312 of the extracted OCR). Sehling signed the preface at Erlangen on Christmas Eve, 24 December 1901.*

*Section I lays out the theological and historical premise of the whole 24-volume edition to come: the evangelical Church, though centred on the Gospel rather than on law, nevertheless requires a legal order — and the sixteenth-century* Kirchenordnungen *are that legal order in its formative period.*

---

**I.** The evangelical Church places less value upon law than the pre-Reformation Church did; it finds its centre of gravity in the Gospel. Yet the Reformers were never of the opinion that the Church could do without a firm order, without a law of its own. It was well known to them what weight Holy Scripture lays upon order within the congregation (1 Cor. 14:33, 40; Col. 2:5; 1 Cor. 1:10, 11, 34; 12:25; Tit. 1:5), and the Apostle's admonition, "Let all things be done decently and in order" (1 Cor. 14:40), stands not without reason as motto at the head of so many church orders. The visible Church requires a legal order. Without it the ***εὐταξία*** — as Melanchthon in his *Loci theologici* repeatedly puts it — is unthinkable.²

The formation of this legal order within the evangelical Church is bound up in the closest way with the development of the evangelical Church as such. The movement, at first directed only against particular points of the ruling system, gradually deepened. After some others had gone before, Luther himself at length came forward with reformatory orders. Yet nothing lay farther from the Reformer than to issue, as a lawgiver would, generally binding prescriptions:

> It is not my opinion that the whole German land must simply take over our Wittenberg order. Nor has it ever yet been the case that the collegiate churches, monasteries, and parishes were alike in all respects; rather, it would be a fine thing if within each individual lordship divine service were conducted after a single manner, and the surrounding little towns and villages held with the city as one — whether other lordships kept the same or something particular of their own, that should be free and unpunished.³

And to the people of Erfurt he wrote on 28 October 1525:

> Nor do I think it matters if the other churches will not concede to this: who is to compel the unwilling? We ourselves have already sketched out the form. Then, if it pleases, it may either be confirmed among us, or you may use your own.⁴

How Luther pictured the coming-into-being of ecclesiastical law he shows in a letter to Landgrave Philip of Hesse concerning the reforming plan of the Homburg Synod of 1527. Here he articulates the thought that the parish clergy — and at first only a few of them — should follow a concordant manner in individual matters, put it into effect, continue with it, win over other pastors to the same, extend their order to further points — "and then one could set it down in a little book." When Nikolaus Hausmann had composed his order for Dessau, Luther advised against its public publication, so as not to lend the order a statutory character; it should rather settle into the congregations gradually. ***Nullos magis odi***, he said on one occasion, ***quam eos, qui caeremonias liberas et innoxias exturbant et necessitatem ex libertate faciunt*** — "there are none I hate more than those who drive out free and harmless ceremonies and make necessity out of liberty."⁵ Similar utterances on Christian liberty appear repeatedly. In this Luther was thinking chiefly of the reshaping of cult, sacraments, and doctrine according to the new principles. Accordingly the confessional writings ascribe the authority to draw up ecclesiastical orders — ***ordinationes, caeremoniae, traditiones*** — to the episcopal (= pastoral) office, to the office of teaching (*Lehramt*).⁶

And in fact we find in the earliest period of the Reformation many orders drawn up by parish clergy as the first foundation of the new church life. They were on occasion observed also in other congregations and served as norm, without any properly law-making source having endowed them with statutory force. Passing over the works of an authority such as Luther, one may think, for example, of Münzer's orders for Allstedt, of Lang's Mass for Erfurt, of the order of the provosts Pessler and Pömer and of Döber's Mass for Nürnberg, of Kantz's Mass for Nördlingen, of Hieronymus Noppus's order for Regensburg, of Franz Kolb's order for Wertheim, and so forth. What circulation and standing did Veit Dietrich's *Agendbüchlein* for the parish clergy in the countryside come to enjoy! (Among the church orders that issued from the office of teaching I do not, as Sohm does, count the orders of the Prussian bishops of Samland and Pomesanien: these two reform mandates were indeed issued by the bishops on their own authority, but by virtue of their authority *as bishops*, as ***iudices ordinarii*** in the Catholic sense; and they lie chronologically before the transfer of the temporal government to the Grand Master Albrecht. The orders that issued in Prussia after that time are to be characterized as territorial-governmental orders.)⁷

Such orders issuing from the teaching office we find also in later times. If they were not expressly recognized by a law-making authority, they nevertheless acquired their legal force through actual custom. Particularly in the shaping of divine service and in the choice of their agendas, the parish clergy proceeded with the greatest freedom. As the Wittenberg Church Order of 1533 puts it: "For the ceremonies shall not be necessary laws, but shall stand in the parson's power, to deal with therein as shall serve to the best." So there prevailed — even where distinct territorial church orders existed — the greatest variety.⁸ Only more so, naturally, where strict laws were lacking or were not enforced. So it emerged at the Visitation in Saxony-Coburg in 1613/14 that with respect to the ceremonies almost every village kept a different order.

These private compositions must not be missing from a collection of church orders. But under the same head belong also *private opinions* issued by clergy, insofar as they were laid at the foundation of the reformation of a congregation and thereby, so to speak, formed the legal basis of its organization. (Thus, for example, the Articles of Lüneburg; but not, on the other hand, the *Opstel* of the Lüneburg preachers for East Frisia in 1534.)

If the confessional writings assign to the office of teaching the authority to draft the orders, and if such orders — especially in the earliest period — are indeed not few, still this source of ecclesiastical legal formation is early on completely overshadowed by *governmental* law-making (***obrigkeitliche Rechtssetzung***). The grounds need not be discussed here. They stand in the closest connection with the well-known history of the evangelical church constitution.

The libertarian development of things, as Luther originally envisioned as ideal, proved unworkable. When the "Instruction of the Visitors to the Parish Pastors in the Electorate of Saxony" observes concerning its own paragraphs, "And however much we cannot let these go forth as strict commands, so as not to set up new papal decretals, but rather as a history or narrative, and further as a testimony and confession of our faith" — nevertheless in the same breath it expresses the hope "that all pious, peaceable pastors, to whom the Gospel with earnest is pleasing and who have desire for it, will hold unanimously and evenly with us," and develops further the principle that those who "from the ground would make something particular of their own" must be separated as the chaff from the wheat, and this precisely with the help of the territorial ruler invoked as guardian of concord and peace. Wholly in opposition to the freedom in ceremonies demanded by Luther as ideal, the call for uniformity soon sounds from many sides, and numerous church orders contain provisions aiming at this — provisions whose very frequency at once proves their necessity.⁹

But if one was no longer to grant free rein to the libertarian shaping of things, to development out of itself; if one wished to create order by secular compulsion, and against the resistant even *had* to do so — then the cooperation of the temporal authority was indispensable. Luther himself already laid his first Mass reforms before the sovereign for approval. So then, called upon by the Reformers, princes and cities took the reforming into hand as a task assigned to them by God within their territory. The conditions of the time — the disintegration of ecclesiastical affairs, the sect-phenomenon, peasant unrest, and so on — themselves gave the temporal authorities cause not to remain inactive but to intervene decisively, as indeed the pre-Reformation territorial rulers had already done when the Church failed on its side. Moreover, the questions of the organization of the Church, which now — the legal forms of the old Church being no longer transferable — came to the fore, were not soluble by the way of individual pastors' orders.

The territorial rulers carry through the Reformation: they order the Visitations, give the requisite instructions, and, as the Visitations lead to lasting institutions, issue the necessary orders. The orders for the Church become *governmental*, i.e. territorial-princely or municipal. They extend to all branches of the outer and inner life of the Church and form the main content of a collection of the evangelical church orders.

To investigate the legal character of these orders — particularly on the basis of the theoretical views then held concerning the relation of state and Church — is not the place here. They are juridically in no relation distinct from the rest of territorial law. That the princes in issuing their orders let themselves be more or less influenced by their theologians — indeed that the theologians are frequently to be reckoned the actual authors of the orders — alters nothing of this. Such influence was not a legally constructible boundary, and beyond participation in the preparatory work for the laws (which, given the ultimate purpose of the legislation — the preservation of correct teaching and administration of the sacraments — was only too natural), the cooperation of the teaching estate did not extend.

A true boundary consisted often in the *estates* (*Stände*), and these have in fact frequently participated in the drafting. But this boundary too fell in the course of the sixteenth century. That in the republican-organized cities the whole community, through its representation, participated in the legal formation, lies on the surface. Interesting in this connection is the legal situation of the *landsässige* cities (those subject to a territorial ruler). These in many cases proceeded with legal formation without the consent of the territorial ruler, and not only those cities that enjoyed a certain independence (without being properly imperial cities), but also the wholly subjected ones. In those times of overturning of existing things, little was asked about competence; these orders found their validity through actual custom; and the theory teaches outright that the dependent cities acquired the right of legislation in ecclesiastical matters through immemorial prescription.¹⁰

In detail the proceedings in this ecclesiastical legal formation were very various. The territorial ruler issued and published the law directly, like the other territorial laws; or he commissioned third parties to issue ordinances in his name, e.g. the Visitation commissions — whose ordinances are in essence likewise princely ordinances. Or the territorial ruler commissioned officials to publish orders in their own name, e.g. Duke Albrecht of Prussia the two bishops (marriage-mandates). Or an ecclesiastical assembly (synod) resolved and the territorial ruler ratified the resolution. That synodal resolutions should be binding without princely ratification does not correspond to Lutheran but to Reformed legal practice. Here the legal formation practised by self-governing bodies (*Selbstverwaltungskörper*) enters alongside that issuing from the territorial (or municipal) authority.

More rarely are *treaties* the foundation of the new church polity, e.g. agreements between prince and city (as at Zerbst, Stolberg), or between council and clergy, as at Zwickau.

Also the evangelical bishop and the consistories occasionally made provisions of lasting character. The Celle orders were "compared" (harmonized) through a consultation of delegates from the consistories, and — without the territorial ruler having expressly ratified them — they were observed by the consistories, and equally freely altered again.

Yes, even the libertarian legal formation that had been Luther's ideal has (albeit likely only for ceremonies) maintained itself in independent regulations of the clergy right through the century.

Concerning questions of *competence* one did not quarrel in the sixteenth century; only about the *content*. If a regulation went against the intentions of the temporal authority, the latter already saw to its removal. One may therefore perhaps go so far as to posit a tacit ratification of the sovereign for such measures. On this all were agreed: that an order to be carried out with coercive power, or one extending over the whole land, could go forth only from the central state authority.

Whoever wishes to understand the legal condition of the evangelical Church must therefore not content himself with a knowledge of the laws that formally issued from the territorial ruler. Only the composite of all the norms flowing from such various sources yields a right picture of the ecclesiastical life of that period. We therefore take, for our collection, the concept of *church order* in the widest sense of the word, and understand under it all provisions determined for the regulation of ecclesiastical affairs (with the natural exception of those directives serving only transient individual purposes).

**⁷** On the exclusion of the Prussian bishops' mandates: Sehling here argues against Sohm's *Kirchenrecht* Vol. 1, p. 579, which had classed the reform mandates of the bishops of Samland and Pomesanien among orders issuing from the teaching office (*Lehramt*). Sehling's counter: these mandates were promulgated by the bishops precisely *qua* bishops — as ***iudices ordinarii*** in the Catholic canonistic sense — and predate the 1525 transfer of temporal government in Prussia to Albrecht of Hohenzollern-Ansbach as duke. They are therefore properly *episcopal* (Catholic-canonistic) acts, not *pastoral* (Lutheran-*Lehramt*) acts. — On Thomas Münzer's Allstedt orders (1523/24), Johannes Lang's Erfurt Mass (1525), Andreas Osiander (with Pessler, Pömer, and Döber) at Nürnberg (1524), Kaspar Kantz's Nördlingen Mass (1522 — the *first* German evangelical Mass), Hieronymus Noppus at Regensburg (1542), Franz Kolb at Wertheim (1528), and Veit Dietrich's *Agendbüchlein für die Pfarrherrn auf dem Lande* (Nürnberg, 1543): standard reference is now the individual entries in the *TRE* (*Theologische Realenzyklopädie*, ed. Krause–Müller, Berlin 1977–2004).
