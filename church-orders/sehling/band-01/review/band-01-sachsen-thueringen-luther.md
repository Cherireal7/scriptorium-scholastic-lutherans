## Translator's Note

### 1. Text base

The English text is prepared from the facsimile and OCR-Volltext of the first (and only) edition of Sehling's *Die evangelischen Kirchenordnungen des XVI. Jahrhunderts*, Band I, published by O. R. Reisland at Leipzig in 1902. The working source is the digital facsimile at the Heidelberg Academy of Sciences' DigiHadw platform (record `eko1`; DOI [10.11588/diglit.26586](https://doi.org/10.11588/diglit.26586)), which supplies both page images and OCR-derived plain text. Sehling's own pagination is preserved throughout the endnotes as `[S.N]` markers to allow cross-reference to the German critical edition.

Band I is the first volume of Sehling's landmark twenty-four-volume critical corpus of the sixteenth-century Lutheran territorial church orders (*Kirchenordnungen*). It bears the internal designation *Erste Abtheilung, Erste Hälfte* — "First Section, First Half" — because Sehling originally envisioned the whole edition as two divisions, the first covering the Saxon–Thuringian heartland of the Reformation and the second the outlying territories. The complementary "Zweite Hälfte" of the First Section is Band II (1904). The two-Abtheilung scheme was not extended by Sehling's successors, who instead numbered the later volumes serially.

### 2. What Sehling supplies

Sehling's 1902 apparatus is preserved intact and translated in full: his editorial preface (*Vorrede*), his introductions to each territorial subdivision, his source-critical notes on the transmission of each individual order, his dating discussions, and his indexes (chronological, personnel–geographic, and subject). Sehling's numbering of the church orders is retained; his footnotes are rendered as numbered endnotes at the close of each Part.

The sixteenth-century *Kirchenordnungen* themselves — the actual constitutional and liturgical documents issued by Luther, the Ernestine and Albertine electors and dukes, and the associated territorial councils — are translated as period legal instruments, in an English register that mirrors their public and public-liturgical character. Where the orders quote Scripture, we render the German Bible text as it stands in the order (which usually, but not always, reproduces one of the Luther Bible editions current at the time of promulgation), with a modern chapter-and-verse pointer in the endnote for readers who wish to consult a standard English Bible.

Liturgical Latin — Kyries, Introits, Collects, Post-communions, hymn incipits — is preserved verbatim in the body. English translations of stock liturgical formulas are given in the endnotes on first occurrence per document.

### 3. What the Scholastic Lutherans translation team has added

Only in endnotes:

- Modern-critical editions of texts the orders reference (WA for Luther's occasional writings; BSLK / Kolb–Wengert for confessional documents; standard editions for canon law and patristic citations).
- Modern chapter-and-verse pointers for scriptural quotations, where the order gives only a book name or an older versification.
- Biographical dates for persons named in the orders.
- Brief clarifying notes on obsolete technical vocabulary — territorial-legal terms (*Landesordnung*, *Konsistorium*, *Superintendent*, *Visitationsartikel*), currency and measure names, defunct place-names and jurisdictions.
- Cross-references to parallel material in other volumes of the Sehling corpus.

Nothing has been added to the body text. Sehling's own voice and the sixteenth-century voices he edited stand on their own.

### 4. Editorial conventions

Following the Scholastic Lutherans program conventions established in the Praetorius, Chytraeus, Selnecker, Kirchner, and Flacius volumes:

- **Territorial names** appear in German on first occurrence with an English gloss in parentheses (e.g. *Kurfürstentum Sachsen* — the Electorate of Saxony), and in English thereafter. Long-Englished names (Saxony, Thuringia, Franconia, Palatinate) are used in English throughout.
- **Personal names** follow Sehling's spellings, which are the historical German forms. Where the modern English convention differs (Melancthon for Melanchthon, Luther for Luther), we retain Sehling's form.
- **Roman numeral part / paragraph headings** within individual church orders are preserved and not renumbered.
- **[Square brackets]** in the body always mark editorial insertion, whether Sehling's or ours; the distinction is disambiguated in the endnote on first occurrence per document.
- **Greek and Hebrew** in the source (rare in this volume) are preserved in the original script with transliteration and a brief English gloss on first occurrence.
- **AI assistance:** the translation is prepared by Cherinet Demeke under the Scholastic Lutherans team name, assisted by Anthropic's Claude Opus model. Every rendering has been checked against the source. Where the OCR is corrupt or Sehling's editorial reading is contested, we say so in an endnote.

### 5. What is not yet in this working translation

This is a working English text, released tranche by tranche to the Scholastic Lutherans team's private repository. The following are not yet complete:

- The bulk of the body text (Luther's orders, Ernestine orders, Albertine orders, and Sehling's introductions to each) — see the tranche list at the head of the notes file.
- The three back-matter indexes (chronological, personnel–geographic, subject) — deferred until body-text translation is closed, since the index entries reference paginated body content.
- Cross-references to Bände VI/1 (Braunschweig-Lüneburg 1955) and XVI (Baden-Württemberg II 2004), where later Saxon and Thuringian material was back-filled — pending completion of those volumes.

### Note on the volume title

Sehling's German uses the older orthographic form ***Abtheilung*** (rather than the modern ***Abteilung***) and the older ***Ernestinischen*** and ***Albertinischen*** endings appropriate to the pre-1901 orthographic reform. We render these as their standard English equivalents (**Section**, **Ernestine**, **Albertine**) rather than perpetuating the archaism, but preserve Sehling's own spelling verbatim in the transcription block above.

The designation ***Erste Hälfte*** ("First Half") reflects Sehling's original two-part conception of the *Erste Abtheilung*; the "Zweite Hälfte" is Band II (1904), covering the geistliche Gebiete (the former ecclesiastical territories), the Harz and Henneberg counties, the Erzstift Magdeburg / Halberstadt, and Anhalt. The two-Abteilung scheme was quietly abandoned by Sehling's postwar successors, who continued the series with straightforward serial numbering (III, IV, V…) rather than a "Zweite Abtheilung" for the outlying regions.¹

## T1b · §I — Sehling's *Vorwort*, Section I

### *The legal order of the evangelical Church*

*Sehling's editorial preface (*Vorwort*, pp. V–XXI), Section I (pp. V–VIII, corresponding roughly to lines 66–312 of the extracted OCR). Sehling signed the preface at Erlangen on Christmas Eve, 24 December 1901.*

*Section I lays out the theological and historical premise of the whole 24-volume edition to come: the evangelical Church, though centred on the Gospel rather than on law, nevertheless requires a legal order — and the sixteenth-century* Kirchenordnungen *are that legal order in its formative period.*

---

**I.** The evangelical Church places less value upon law than the pre-Reformation Church did; it finds its centre of gravity in the Gospel. Yet the Reformers were never of the opinion that the Church could do without a firm order, without a law of its own. It was well known to them what weight Holy Scripture lays upon order within the congregation (1 Cor. 14:33, 40; Col. 2:5; 1 Cor. 1:10, 11, 34; 12:25; Tit. 1:5), and the Apostle's admonition, "Let all things be done decently and in order" (1 Cor. 14:40), stands not without reason as motto at the head of so many church orders. The visible Church requires a legal order. Without it the ***εὐταξία*** — as Melanchthon in his *Loci theologici* repeatedly puts it — is unthinkable.²

The formation of this legal order within the evangelical Church is bound up in the closest way with the development of the evangelical Church as such. The movement, at first directed only against particular points of the ruling system, gradually deepened. After some others had gone before, Luther himself at length came forward with reformatory orders. Yet nothing lay farther from the Reformer than to issue, as a lawgiver would, generally binding prescriptions:

> It is not my opinion that the whole German land must simply take over our Wittenberg order. Nor has it ever yet been the case that the collegiate churches, monasteries, and parishes were alike in all respects; rather, it would be a fine thing if within each individual lordship divine service were conducted after a single manner, and the surrounding little towns and villages held with the city as one — whether other lordships kept the same or something particular of their own, that should be free and unpunished.³

And to the people of Erfurt he wrote on 28 October 1525:

> Nor do I think it matters if the other churches will not concede to this: who is to compel the unwilling? We ourselves have already sketched out the form. Then, if it pleases, it may either be confirmed among us, or you may use your own.⁴

How Luther pictured the coming-into-being of ecclesiastical law he shows in a letter to Landgrave Philip of Hesse concerning the reforming plan of the Homburg Synod of 1527. Here he articulates the thought that the parish clergy — and at first only a few of them — should follow a concordant manner in individual matters, put it into effect, continue with it, win over other pastors to the same, extend their order to further points — "and then one could set it down in a little book." When Nikolaus Hausmann had composed his order for Dessau, Luther advised against its public publication, so as not to lend the order a statutory character; it should rather settle into the congregations gradually. ***Nullos magis odi***, he said on one occasion, ***quam eos, qui caeremonias liberas et innoxias exturbant et necessitatem ex libertate faciunt*** — "there are none I hate more than those who drive out free and harmless ceremonies and make necessity out of liberty."⁵ Similar utterances on Christian liberty appear repeatedly. In this Luther was thinking chiefly of the reshaping of cult, sacraments, and doctrine according to the new principles. Accordingly the confessional writings ascribe the authority to draw up ecclesiastical orders — ***ordinationes, caeremoniae, traditiones*** — to the episcopal (= pastoral) office, to the office of teaching (*Lehramt*).⁶

And in fact we find in the earliest period of the Reformation many orders drawn up by parish clergy as the first foundation of the new church life. They were on occasion observed also in other congregations and served as norm, without any properly law-making source having endowed them with statutory force. Passing over the works of an authority such as Luther, one may think, for example, of Münzer's orders for Allstedt, of Lang's Mass for Erfurt, of the order of the provosts Pessler and Pömer and of Döber's Mass for Nürnberg, of Kantz's Mass for Nördlingen, of Hieronymus Noppus's order for Regensburg, of Franz Kolb's order for Wertheim, and so forth. What circulation and standing did Veit Dietrich's *Agendbüchlein* for the parish clergy in the countryside come to enjoy! (Among the church orders that issued from the office of teaching I do not, as Sohm does, count the orders of the Prussian bishops of Samland and Pomesanien: these two reform mandates were indeed issued by the bishops on their own authority, but by virtue of their authority *as bishops*, as ***iudices ordinarii*** in the Catholic sense; and they lie chronologically before the transfer of the temporal government to the Grand Master Albrecht. The orders that issued in Prussia after that time are to be characterized as territorial-governmental orders.)⁷

Such orders issuing from the teaching office we find also in later times. If they were not expressly recognized by a law-making authority, they nevertheless acquired their legal force through actual custom. Particularly in the shaping of divine service and in the choice of their agendas, the parish clergy proceeded with the greatest freedom. As the Wittenberg Church Order of 1533 puts it: "For the ceremonies shall not be necessary laws, but shall stand in the parson's power, to deal with therein as shall serve to the best." So there prevailed — even where distinct territorial church orders existed — the greatest variety.⁸ Only more so, naturally, where strict laws were lacking or were not enforced. So it emerged at the Visitation in Saxony-Coburg in 1613/14 that with respect to the ceremonies almost every village kept a different order.

These private compositions must not be missing from a collection of church orders. But under the same head belong also *private opinions* issued by clergy, insofar as they were laid at the foundation of the reformation of a congregation and thereby, so to speak, formed the legal basis of its organization. (Thus, for example, the Articles of Lüneburg; but not, on the other hand, the *Opstel* of the Lüneburg preachers for East Frisia in 1534.)

If the confessional writings assign to the office of teaching the authority to draft the orders, and if such orders — especially in the earliest period — are indeed not few, still this source of ecclesiastical legal formation is early on completely overshadowed by *governmental* law-making (***obrigkeitliche Rechtssetzung***). The grounds need not be discussed here. They stand in the closest connection with the well-known history of the evangelical church constitution.

The libertarian development of things, as Luther originally envisioned as ideal, proved unworkable. When the "Instruction of the Visitors to the Parish Pastors in the Electorate of Saxony" observes concerning its own paragraphs, "And however much we cannot let these go forth as strict commands, so as not to set up new papal decretals, but rather as a history or narrative, and further as a testimony and confession of our faith" — nevertheless in the same breath it expresses the hope "that all pious, peaceable pastors, to whom the Gospel with earnest is pleasing and who have desire for it, will hold unanimously and evenly with us," and develops further the principle that those who "from the ground would make something particular of their own" must be separated as the chaff from the wheat, and this precisely with the help of the territorial ruler invoked as guardian of concord and peace. Wholly in opposition to the freedom in ceremonies demanded by Luther as ideal, the call for uniformity soon sounds from many sides, and numerous church orders contain provisions aiming at this — provisions whose very frequency at once proves their necessity.⁹

But if one was no longer to grant free rein to the libertarian shaping of things, to development out of itself; if one wished to create order by secular compulsion, and against the resistant even *had* to do so — then the cooperation of the temporal authority was indispensable. Luther himself already laid his first Mass reforms before the sovereign for approval. So then, called upon by the Reformers, princes and cities took the reforming into hand as a task assigned to them by God within their territory. The conditions of the time — the disintegration of ecclesiastical affairs, the sect-phenomenon, peasant unrest, and so on — themselves gave the temporal authorities cause not to remain inactive but to intervene decisively, as indeed the pre-Reformation territorial rulers had already done when the Church failed on its side. Moreover, the questions of the organization of the Church, which now — the legal forms of the old Church being no longer transferable — came to the fore, were not soluble by the way of individual pastors' orders.

The territorial rulers carry through the Reformation: they order the Visitations, give the requisite instructions, and, as the Visitations lead to lasting institutions, issue the necessary orders. The orders for the Church become *governmental*, i.e. territorial-princely or municipal. They extend to all branches of the outer and inner life of the Church and form the main content of a collection of the evangelical church orders.

To investigate the legal character of these orders — particularly on the basis of the theoretical views then held concerning the relation of state and Church — is not the place here. They are juridically in no relation distinct from the rest of territorial law. That the princes in issuing their orders let themselves be more or less influenced by their theologians — indeed that the theologians are frequently to be reckoned the actual authors of the orders — alters nothing of this. Such influence was not a legally constructible boundary, and beyond participation in the preparatory work for the laws (which, given the ultimate purpose of the legislation — the preservation of correct teaching and administration of the sacraments — was only too natural), the cooperation of the teaching estate did not extend.

A true boundary consisted often in the *estates* (*Stände*), and these have in fact frequently participated in the drafting. But this boundary too fell in the course of the sixteenth century. That in the republican-organized cities the whole community, through its representation, participated in the legal formation, lies on the surface. Interesting in this connection is the legal situation of the *landsässige* cities (those subject to a territorial ruler). These in many cases proceeded with legal formation without the consent of the territorial ruler, and not only those cities that enjoyed a certain independence (without being properly imperial cities), but also the wholly subjected ones. In those times of overturning of existing things, little was asked about competence; these orders found their validity through actual custom; and the theory teaches outright that the dependent cities acquired the right of legislation in ecclesiastical matters through immemorial prescription.¹⁰

In detail the proceedings in this ecclesiastical legal formation were very various. The territorial ruler issued and published the law directly, like the other territorial laws; or he commissioned third parties to issue ordinances in his name, e.g. the Visitation commissions — whose ordinances are in essence likewise princely ordinances. Or the territorial ruler commissioned officials to publish orders in their own name, e.g. Duke Albrecht of Prussia the two bishops (marriage-mandates). Or an ecclesiastical assembly (synod) resolved and the territorial ruler ratified the resolution. That synodal resolutions should be binding without princely ratification does not correspond to Lutheran but to Reformed legal practice. Here the legal formation practised by self-governing bodies (*Selbstverwaltungskörper*) enters alongside that issuing from the territorial (or municipal) authority.

More rarely are *treaties* the foundation of the new church polity, e.g. agreements between prince and city (as at Zerbst, Stolberg), or between council and clergy, as at Zwickau.

Also the evangelical bishop and the consistories occasionally made provisions of lasting character. The Celle orders were "compared" (harmonized) through a consultation of delegates from the consistories, and — without the territorial ruler having expressly ratified them — they were observed by the consistories, and equally freely altered again.

Yes, even the libertarian legal formation that had been Luther's ideal has (albeit likely only for ceremonies) maintained itself in independent regulations of the clergy right through the century.

Concerning questions of *competence* one did not quarrel in the sixteenth century; only about the *content*. If a regulation went against the intentions of the temporal authority, the latter already saw to its removal. One may therefore perhaps go so far as to posit a tacit ratification of the sovereign for such measures. On this all were agreed: that an order to be carried out with coercive power, or one extending over the whole land, could go forth only from the central state authority.

Whoever wishes to understand the legal condition of the evangelical Church must therefore not content himself with a knowledge of the laws that formally issued from the territorial ruler. Only the composite of all the norms flowing from such various sources yields a right picture of the ecclesiastical life of that period. We therefore take, for our collection, the concept of *church order* in the widest sense of the word, and understand under it all provisions determined for the regulation of ecclesiastical affairs (with the natural exception of those directives serving only transient individual purposes).

## Chapter I — Divine-Service Orders (*Gottesdienstordnungen*)

*Sehling's introduction to the chapter, p. 1.*

### §1. *Von ordenung gottis diensts in der gemeine* (1523) — On the Order of Divine Service in the Congregation

*Luther's earliest liturgical writing. Addressed initially to the congregation at Leisnig, then reissued as a model for other congregations. OCR lines 1410–1578.*

**Sehling's textual apparatus.** — Luther had promised the congregation at Leisnig, on 29 January 1523, an order "for singing and prayer and reading." As fulfillment of that promise, the present writing appeared shortly after Easter. I follow Kawerau (*Luthers Werke*, Weimar 1891, Vol. 12, p. 32) in assuming that it did not appear until after the reform of divine service in Luther's sense had been carried through at Wittenberg, and that it went out to the end of serving as a model for other congregations — not, as Kolde holds (*Luther* 2, 107; also *Gött. gel. Anz.* 1892, p. 575), that it was destined for the Wittenberg congregation itself, to announce to them the intended reforms.

On the printings, cf. Kawerau, who names ten 1523 printings and additionally printings in three collections of 1523 and 1526 respectively. In the collected editions of Luther's works it is found at: Jena (1558) 2, fol. 257b–259a; (1572) 2, fol. 235–236a; Altenburg 2, 332–333; Leipzig 22, 226–227; Walch 10, cols. 263–267; Erlangen 22, 151–156; Weimar 12, 35–37. It is further printed in Richter, *Kirchen-Ordnungen*, 1, 1; Daniel, *Codex liturgicus*, 2, 75–80.¹⁴

The following printing is after the Weimarer Ausgabe. (Variants and biblical citations are here, as in all following Luther texts, omitted; the spelling is also simplified.)¹⁵

---

**Luther's text (English rendering):**

> The divine service that goes on now everywhere has a fine Christian origin, just as does the office of preaching. But just as the office of preaching has been corrupted through the spiritual tyrants, so also has the divine service been corrupted through the hypocrites. As we now do not do away with the office of preaching, but rather desire to bring it back into its rightful standing, so it is not our intent to abolish the divine service, but rather to bring it back again into its right course.
>
> Three great abuses have crept into the divine service. The first, that God's word has been kept silent, and only reading and singing done in the churches — this is the worst abuse. The second, that where God's word has been silent, there have crept in alongside so many unchristian fables and lies, both in legends and in song and in sermon, that it is dreadful to behold. The third, that such divine service has been performed as a *work*, whereby one seeks to earn God's grace and salvation; there faith has perished, and every man has given himself to church-endowments, to the founding of masses, has wished to become priest, monk, and nun.
>
> Now to do away with these abuses, it is first to be known that the Christian congregation shall never come together unless there be preached and prayed God's word among them, though it be but very briefly. As Psalm 102 [Sehling: Psalm 101] says: "When the kings and the people come together to serve God, they shall proclaim God's name and his praise." And Paul in 1 Cor. 14 says that in the congregation there shall be prophesying, teaching, and admonition. Wherefore, where God's word is not preached, it is better that one neither sing nor read nor come together.¹⁶
>
> So it went among the Christians at the time of the apostles, and so it should still go: that daily, in the morning, one hour early — at four or five o'clock — one should come together and have there read [aloud], whether they be pupils or priests or whoever, just as one still now reads the lesson at matins. This shall two or three do, or one after the other, or one choir after the other, as best pleases.
>
> Afterwards the preacher, or whoever is charged with it, shall step forth and expound a portion of the same lesson, so that the others may all understand it, learn from it, and be admonished. The first work Paul calls "speaking with tongues" (1 Cor. 14); the second, "expounding" or "prophesying," and "speaking with sense and understanding." And where this does not happen, then the congregation gets no benefit from the lesson, as until now in the monasteries and collegiate churches has occurred, where they have merely puffed up the walls.
>
> This lesson, however, shall be from the Old Testament — namely, that one takes a book and reads a chapter or two, or half a chapter, till it is finished, then takes up another, and so on, till the whole Bible is read through. And where one does not understand it, one should pass over and honour God — that through daily exercise in Scripture, Christians become expert, fluent, and knowledgeable in Scripture. For thence in olden times came very fine Christians, virgins, and martyrs; and so it should yet also come to be.
>
> When now the lesson and exposition have gone on for half an hour or more, one shall thereafter in common thank, praise, and pray to God, and pray for the fruit of the Word, and so on. To this shall one use the psalms and some good responsories, antiphons — briefly, so that it all be carried out within an hour, or however long they will; for one must not overwhelm the souls, that they do not become tired and weary, as until now in the monasteries and collegiate churches they have burdened themselves with ass's labour.
>
> Likewise in the evening at six or five o'clock one should again come together. And here, too, out of the Old Testament one book after another should be taken up — namely the Prophets, just as in the morning Moses and the histories. But since the New Testament is also a book, I place the Old Testament for the morning and the New for the evening, or the other way about; and just so read, expound, praise, sing, and pray, as in the morning, also for an hour long. For it is all to be done for God's word, that the same may run its course and ever raise up and refresh the souls, so that they do not grow weary.
>
> If one would now hold such a gathering once again in the day, after meals, that stands in free choice.
>
> And even if such a daily divine service perhaps cannot be attended by the whole congregation, still the priests and the pupils, and above all those who are hoped to become good preachers and pastors, shall do it. And one should admonish them to do it freely, not from compulsion or unwillingness, not for pay temporal nor eternal, but only to honour God and to do good to their neighbour.
>
> On Sunday, however, such a gathering shall be for the whole congregation, over and above the daily gathering of the smaller company; and there, as until now customary, Mass and Vespers are to be sung — also in such manner that at both times one preach to the whole congregation, in the morning on the usual Gospel [pericope], in the evening on the Epistle; or it stands with the preacher whether he also take up a book for himself, or two, as seems to him most useful.
>
> If then anyone wishes to receive the sacrament, let it be given to him, as one can well arrange all of this among one another according to the fitness of time and person.
>
> The daily masses shall be abolished altogether; for what matters is the Word, and not the Mass. If, however, some outside of the Sunday desire the sacrament, then one holds Mass as devotion and time may allow, for here one cannot set law nor limit.
>
> The chants in the Sunday Masses and Vespers, let them stand — for they are exceedingly good and drawn from Scripture, though one may reduce or increase them. But the appointing of the daily chants and psalms morning and evening shall be the office of the parson and preacher, so that they arrange for every morning one psalm, one fine responsory or antiphon with a collect. In the evening likewise: publicly to read and sing after the lesson and its exposition. But the antiphons and responsories and collects, the legends of the saints and of the cross — let those still lie idle for a time, until they be swept clean; for there is dreadful filth in them.
>
> All the saints' feasts should be done away, or, where there was a good Christian legend, one might carry it on the Sunday, following the Gospel, as example. Yet the feasts of the Purification and of the Annunciation of Mary I let stand; and Assumption and Nativity we must for a time still let stand, even though the singing in them is not pure. John the Baptist's feast is likewise clean. The legends of the Apostles are none of them clean, except that of St. Paul; therefore one may transfer them to the Sundays, or, if it please, celebrate them particularly.
>
> More shall present itself with time, once one has begun. But the sum shall be this: that all things happen so that the Word may run its course, and it may not become again a roaring and droning, as until now has been. It is all better neglected than the Word. And nothing is more usefully driven than the Word. For that the same should run its course among the Christians, all Scripture shows, and Christ himself says (Luke 10): "One thing is needful" — namely, that Mary sit at Christ's feet and hear his word daily. That is the best part, which is chosen, and shall never be taken away. It is an eternal word; the other must all pass away, however much it may give Martha to do. God help us to this. Amen.¹⁷

### §2. *Formula missae et communionis pro ecclesia Wittembergensi* (1523)

*Luther's foundational Latin statement of his reform of the Mass, addressed as an open letter to Nikolaus Hausmann (c. 1478–1538), pastor at Zwickau (whom Luther habitually addressed as "Bishop of the Church at Zwickau"). Written November 1523. OCR lines 1579–2151.*

**Sehling's textual apparatus.** — Luther had intended "this cleansing of the transmitted Mass-form from later additions" at first for the congregation at Wittenberg, but then also for several friends who had approached him for such an order, such as Nikolaus Hausmann in Zwickau. Luther carried out the work in November 1523.

At Hausmann's request, Paulus Speratus translated the *Formula* for the laity into German. A further translation was issued by a Nürnberg printing house. Cf. Schmidt, *Nikolaus Hausmann*, 1860, pp. 27 ff., 31; Jacoby, *Liturgik* 1, 256 ff.; Gottschick, *Luthers Anschauungen*, pp. 68 ff.; Köstlin, *Luther*, 1, 561 ff.; Kawerau in *Weimarer Ausgabe* 12, 199 ff.; Smend, *op. cit.*, p. 6.

On the printings — three Latin editions, six editions of the Speratus translation, four editions of the Nürnberg translation — cf. Kawerau in *Weimarer Ausgabe* 12, 203 ff. Reprintings in the collected editions of Luther's works: Wittenberg (1546) 2, 412b ff.; Jena (1566) 2, 556a ff.; *Frankofurt. Opp. var. arg.* 7, 17; Weimar 12, 205 ff. Further reprints in: Richter, *K.O.* 1, 2 ff.; Daniel, *Codex liturg.* 2, 80 ff.; Hering, *Hülfsbuch zur Einführung in das Studium der Liturgik*, pp. 125 ff. The Speratus translation is in the collected editions: Wittenberg (1561) 7, fol. 362 ff.; Jena 3 (1560), fol. 269 ff.; Altenburg 3, 460 ff.; Leipzig 22, 232 ff.; Walch 10, 27, 44. Here the printing follows the Weimarer Ausgabe 12, 205 ff.¹⁸

---

**Luther's text (English rendering; salutation preserved in Latin):**

> **VENERABILI IN CHRISTO D. NICOLAO HAVSMANN, EPISCOPO CYGNEAE ECCLESIAE IN CHRISTO SANCTO. — MART. LVTHER.**
>
> *To the venerable in Christ Master Nikolaus Hausmann, Bishop of the Church at Zwickau, holy in Christ. — Martin Luther.*
>
> Wishes grace and peace in Christ. — Hitherto I have laboured by pamphlets and sermons among the people, that I might first draw hearts away from impious opinions about ceremonies, judging that I did a Christian and useful thing if I caused the abomination which Satan through the man of sin had set up in the holy place to be worn down without violence of hand. Accordingly I attempted nothing by force or command, nor did I change old things for new, ever hesitating and fearful — on the one hand for the sake of weak souls in faith, from whom one so ancient and inveterate a mode of worshipping God could not suddenly be removed, nor so recent and unaccustomed a mode be introduced; on the other hand and most of all for the sake of those light and fastidious spirits who, like unclean swine, without faith and without mind, rush in and rejoice only in novelty, and, as soon as the novelty ceases to be novel, are disgusted. As with such a class of men in all other matters nothing is more troublesome, so in sacred matters they are most troublesome and intolerable — though, that I may burst with rage, I am compelled to bear them, unless I wish to remove the Gospel itself from the public.
>
> But since there is now hope that the hearts of many have been enlightened and strengthened by God's grace, and the very matter itself demands that at last scandals be removed from the kingdom of Christ, something must be ventured in the name of Christ. It is just that we take counsel even for the few, lest, while we perpetually dread the levity and abuse of the others, we take counsel for none at all, and, while we wish to guard against future scandals of these people, we confirm all their abominations. Therefore concerning some pious formula of celebrating (as they say) and communicating, as you have so often requested, most excellent Nikolaus, we shall deal; and we shall deal in such manner that not by the word of doctrine alone we govern hearts, but by public administration we bring it into practical work — prejudging none at all, so that another form may be embraced and followed. Rather, we entreat by Christ from the heart, that if anything better be revealed to them, they order us first to be silent, so that by common labour we may aid the common cause.
>
> First, therefore, we profess that it neither has been, nor is, in our mind to abolish the whole worship of God utterly, but to purge that which is in use — polluted with the vilest additions — and to show the pious use. For this we cannot deny, that the Mass and the communion of bread and wine is a rite divinely instituted by Christ, which was observed under Christ himself and afterwards under the apostles most simply and most piously, without any additions. But with the succession of times it was so increased by so many human inventions that beyond the name nothing of the Mass and communion has passed down to our own ages.
>
> The additions of the earliest Fathers, who are read to have prayed one or another psalm before the blessing of bread and wine, in low voice, were praiseworthy — such as Athanasius and Cyprian are thought to have done. Then those who added the *Kyrie eleison* also please me; for under Basil the Great we read that the *Kyrie eleison* was in public use of the whole people. Now the reading of the epistles and gospels was even necessary and is — except that it is a vice for it to be read in a language which the common people do not understand. After that, when the singing began, the psalms were changed into the introit; then was added that angelic hymn *Gloria in excelsis, et in terra pax*; likewise graduals and alleluia and the Nicene Creed, Sanctus, Agnus Dei, communion. All of which are of such a kind that they cannot be reproved, especially those which are sung *de tempore*, that is, on the Sundays — which days alone attest their pristine purity, apart from the canon.
>
> But when now the license began of adding and changing as anyone pleased — and there was joined to this the tyranny of gain and of priestly ambition — then began those altars and standards of Baal and of all gods to be set up in the temple of the Lord, by our impious kings, that is, by the bishops and pastors. Here the impious Ahaz removed the bronze altar and set up another sought from Damascus — I speak, however, of that lacerated and abominable Canon, gathered as from many bilges into a sink: there the Mass began to be made a sacrifice; there were added offertories and mercenary collects; there sequences and prose were inserted between the Sanctus and the Gloria in excelsis. Then the Mass began to be a priestly monopoly, exhausting the wealth of the whole world, feeding the rich, the idle, the powerful and voluptuous and unclean celibates throughout the whole earth as an overflowing final desolation. Hence Masses for the dead, for journeys, for goods; and who could number these titles alone by which the Mass has been made into a sacrifice?
>
> Nor today does this Canon cease to be augmented, adopting for various feasts various actions, various *communicantes* — to say nothing of memorials of the living and the dead not yet increased to their term. For to speak of the external additions of vestments, vessels, candles, palls, then of organs and of the whole of music, of images — what shall I say? There is scarcely anything in the whole realm of the crafts which did not have its business and its gain to a great extent, and was fed from the Mass.
>
> Let these things pass, then — and let them still pass, as the Gospel reveals such abominations, until they be utterly abolished. We in the meantime shall test all things; we shall hold what is good. But in this book we omit to say that the Mass [is not] a sacrifice or good work, which we have abundantly taught elsewhere. Let us grasp it as sacrament, or testament, or blessing (in Latin), *eucharistia* in Greek, or the Lord's table, or the Lord's Supper, or the memory of the Lord, or communion — or by whatever pious name pleases, provided only it not be polluted with the title of sacrifice or of work — and let us show the rite by which it has seemed to us to use it.¹⁹
>
> **First**, we approve and retain the introits for Sundays and for the feasts of Christ — namely, of Easter, of Pentecost, of Nativity — although we would prefer the psalms whence they were taken, as of old; but for now we shall indulge the received usage. If, however, any wish to approve the introits of apostles, of the Virgin, and of other saints (when they are taken from psalms or other scriptures), we do not condemn them. We at Wittenberg shall seek to sabbatize on the Lord's days and the feasts of the Lord alone, judging all the saints' feasts to be entirely abrogated, or, if anything worthy is in them, to be mingled in the Sunday sermons. The feasts of the Purification and of the Annunciation we hold as feasts of Christ, as also the Epiphany and the Circumcision. In place of the feast of St. Stephen and John the Evangelist, the office of the Nativity pleases. Let the feasts of the Holy Cross be anathema. Let others do, according to their own conscience or the weakness of others, as the Spirit shall suggest.
>
> **Secondly**, the *Kyrie eleison*, as it has hitherto been celebrated with various melodies for various seasons, we embrace, together with the following angelic hymn *Gloria in excelsis* — though this shall stand at the discretion of the bishop, how often he wishes it to be omitted.
>
> **Thirdly**, the following prayer or collect, provided it be pious (as most are, which are had on the Lord's days), let it continue in its rite — but that one only. After this, the reading of the Epistle. Yet it is not now the time to innovate here, since none impious is read — although, since it is rare that those parts from the Pauline Epistles are read in which faith is taught (but rather chiefly the moral and hortatory ones, so that the arranger of the Epistles seems to have been remarkably ignorant and a superstitious weigher of works), the office required for the most part to arrange those in which faith in Christ is taught. Certainly the same for the Gospels.
>
> **Fourthly**, the gradual of two verses, together with the alleluia, or one of the two — let it be sung at the bishop's judgment. Further, the Lenten graduals and the like, which exceed two verses, let anyone who wishes sing at home. In the church we do not wish the spirit of the faithful to be extinguished by tedium. But neither is it fitting to display Lent itself, or Holy Week, or Good Friday with more painful rites than other days, lest we seem, with a half-Mass and one part of the sacrament, still to wish to mock and laugh at Christ. For the alleluia is the perpetual voice of the Church, just as the memory of his passion and victory is perpetual.
>
> **Fifthly**, we admit no sequences or proses, unless it please the bishop [to admit] that brief one at the Nativity of Christ, *Grates nunc omnes*. Nor are there almost any that savour of the Spirit, save those on the Holy Spirit: *Sancti spiritus* and *Veni sancte spiritus* — which either after the meal, or under vespers, or under the Mass (if it please the bishop), it is permitted to sing.
>
> **Sixthly**, the reading of the Gospel follows. Here we neither forbid candles nor censings, nor do we demand them. Let this be free.
>
> **Seventhly**, the Nicene Creed, wont to be sung, does not displease; yet this too the bishop has in his own hand. The same we judge concerning the vernacular sermon — that it makes no difference whether it be done here after the Creed, or before the introit of the Mass; although there is another consideration why it is more fitly done before the Mass: because the Gospel is the voice crying in the wilderness and calling unbelievers to faith, while the Mass itself is the use of the Gospel and the communion of the Lord's table, which is only for the faithful and it was fitting for it to be done apart. But nevertheless this consideration does not bind us who are free — especially because all things that are done up to the Creed in the Mass are ours and free, not exacted by God, and therefore do not necessarily pertain to the Mass.
>
> **Eighthly**, then follows that whole *abomination*, to serve which whatever went before in the Mass was compelled — whence it is called the offertory. And from this point almost all things sound and smell of oblation. In the midst of which those words of life and salvation are so placed as once the ark of the Lord was in the temple of idols next to Dagon. And there is no Israelite there who can either approach or bring back the ark, until it, having smitten its enemies with hindmost parts, ennobled itself with eternal reproach and compelled itself to be dismissed — which is a parable of the pressing time. Therefore, with all those things repudiated which sound of oblation, together with the entire Canon, let us retain those which are pure and holy, and so let us begin our Mass.²⁰
>
> **I.** Under the Creed, or after the Canon, the bread and wine are prepared for blessing according to the accustomed rite — except that I have not yet settled with myself whether water is to be mixed with the wine, though I incline to this: that pure wine rather be prepared, without admixture of water, because the signification distresses me which Isaiah 1 places: "Thy wine (he says) is mixed with water." For pure wine beautifully figures the purity of evangelical doctrine. Next, because there was poured out for us only the unmixed blood of Christ, of which we there make memory. So that dream of theirs shall not stand, who say that here is figured our union with Christ: we do not here make memory of this union; nor were we united before his outpouring — otherwise our own blood at once will be celebrated as poured out for us with the blood of Christ. Yet against liberty I will not introduce a superstitious law. Christ will not much regard these things, nor is the matter worthy of contention. The Roman and Greek Church has fought this foolish battle enough, as also many others. That some adduce that from Christ's side flowed water with blood proves nothing; for that water signifies something other than what they wish to be signified by this mixed water. Nor was that water mixed with the blood — a figure moreover proves nothing, and an example is not established. Therefore let this human invention be treated freely.
>
> **II.** With the bread and wine prepared, one proceeds thus: "*Dominus vobiscum*." Response: "*Et cum spiritu tuo.*" "*Sursum corda.*" Response: "*Habeamus ad Dominum.*" "*Gratias agamus Domino Deo nostro.*" Response: "*Dignum et justum est. Vere dignum et justum est, aequum et salutare, nos tibi semper et ubique gratias agere, Domine sancte, Pater omnipotens, aeterne Deus, per Christum Dominum nostrum.*"
>
> **III.** Then: "*Qui pridie quam pateretur*, accepit panem, gratias agens fregit deditque discipulis suis dicens: Accipite, comedite, hoc est corpus meum, quod pro vobis datur. Similiter et calicem, postquam caenavit, dicens: Hic calix est novi testamenti in meo sanguine, qui pro vobis et pro multis effundetur in remissionem peccatorum. Haec quotiescunque feceritis, in mei memoriam faciatis.*"
>
> I would wish these words of Christ to be recited with a modest pause interposed after the preface, in that tone of voice in which the Lord's Prayer is otherwise chanted in the Canon, so that they may be heard by those standing about — although in all these things there is liberty for pious minds, either to recite the words silently or openly.
>
> **IV.** The blessing being finished, let the choir sing *Sanctus*, and under the chanting of *Benedictus* let the bread and cup be elevated, by the rite hitherto observed, or on account of the weak who might perhaps be offended by so sudden a change in this more distinctive rite in the Mass, especially where by vernacular sermons they have been taught what is signified by that elevation.²¹
>
> **V.** After these, let the Lord's Prayer be read. Thus: "*Oremus. Preceptis salutaribus moniti* etc.," omitting the following prayer "*Libera nos, quesumus*," together with all the signs which are wont to be made over the host and with the host over the chalice; neither shall the host be broken nor mingled with the chalice. But at once after the Lord's Prayer let it be said: "*Pax Domini* etc." — which is a certain public absolution of the sins of the communicants, a plainly evangelical voice announcing the remission of sins, that one and most worthy preparation for the Lord's table, if it be apprehended by faith — no otherwise than as brought forth from the mouth of Christ. Whence I would wish it to be announced with face turned to the people, as bishops are wont to do — which is the sole vestige of the ancient bishops in our bishops.
>
> **VI.** Then let him communicate both himself and the people, during which time let *Agnus Dei* be sung. And if he shall wish before receiving to pray that prayer "*Domine Iesu Christe, fili Dei vivi, qui ex voluntate Patris* etc.," he will not pray ill, changing only the singular number to the plural, "*nostris*" and "*nos*" for "*meis*" and "*me*." Likewise also that other: "*Corpus Domini* etc. *custodiat animam meam, vel tuam, in vitam aeternam.*" And: "*Sanguis Domini nostri custodiat animam tuam in vitam aeternam.*"
>
> **VII.** If it please to sing the communion, let it be sung. But in place of the *complenda* or last collect, because they nearly all sound of sacrifice, let that prayer be read in the same tone: "*Quod ore sumpsimus, Domine.*" That one also can be read: "*Corpus tuum, Domine, quod sumpsimus* etc.," changing the number to the plural, "*Qui vivis et regnas* etc." "*Dominus vobiscum* etc." In place of "*Ite missa est*" let it be said: "*Benedicamus Domino*," adjoining (where and when it pleases) *alleluia* in its melodies, or let them be borrowed from the *Benedicamus* of vespers.
>
> **VIII.** Let the accustomed benediction be given, or let that of Numbers 6 be taken, which the Lord himself set forth, saying: "*Benedicat nos Dominus et custodiat nos, ostendat nobis faciem suam et misereatur nostri; convertat Dominus faciem suam ad nos et det nobis pacem.*" Or that of Psalm 96 [67 in the Hebrew numbering]: "*Benedicat nos Deus, Deus noster; benedicat nos Deus et metuant eum omnes fines terrae. Amen.*" — Such, I believe, Christ also used, when ascending into heaven he blessed his disciples.²²
>
> **[On the manner of communicating.]** And here also let it be free for the bishop, in what order he will use either species — either to receive or to administer. For he will be able to bless both, namely the bread and the wine, continuously, before he shall have taken the bread; or between the blessing of the bread and of the wine at once himself and as many as have wished, communicate in the bread; and then bless the wine, and finally give to all to drink. Which rite Christ seems to have used, as the words of the Gospel sound, where he commanded them to eat the bread before he blessed the chalice; then he expressly says: "*Similiter et calicem, postquam caenavit*" ["Likewise also the cup, after supper"] — so that you may perceive the cup was blessed for the first time after the eating. But this new rite will not suffer those things which we have hitherto said after the blessing to be done, unless they too be changed.
>
> Thus we think about the Mass. In which all things must be so guarded lest we make a law out of liberty, or compel to sin those who shall either do it otherwise, or shall have omitted certain things — provided they permit the words of blessing to remain integral, and act here by faith. For these ought to be the rites of Christians, that is, of children of freedom, who spontaneously and from the heart observe these things, to be changed as often and in whatever manner they wish. Therefore there is no cause why anyone should either seek or establish any necessary form as a law in this matter, by which he might ensnare and vex consciences. Wherefore even among the ancient fathers and the primitive Church we read no complete example of this rite, except in the Roman Church. Nor should it have been observed even if they had sanctioned anything as law in this matter, because these matters neither can nor ought to be bound by laws. Further, if even different [Christians] use different rite, let no one judge or condemn the other, but each abound in his own sense, and let us know and taste the same, even if we do different things and each one's rite pleases the other — lest with diversity of rites diverse opinions and sects follow, as it happened under the Roman Church.
>
> For external rites, though we can no more do without them than without food and drink, still do not commend us to God, just as neither food commends us to God. But faith and love commend us to God. Wherefore let that of Paul rule here: "The kingdom of God is not food and drink, but righteousness, peace, and joy in the Holy Spirit." So neither is any rite the kingdom of God — but faith within you, and so on.
>
> As for vestments — we pass over them. But concerning these, as concerning other rites, we judge: let us permit their free use, provided pomp and luxury be absent. For neither do you please God more if you have blessed in vestments, nor do you please him less if you have blessed without vestments. For vestments do not commend us to God. But neither would I wish them to be consecrated or blessed, as though they were to be something sacred above other vestments — save with that general blessing by which every good creature of God is taught to be sanctified through word and prayer. Otherwise it is mere superstition and impiety introduced through the pontiffs of abomination, as also other things.
>
> **On the communion of the people.**
>
> These things we have said about the Mass and the office of the minister or bishop. Now we shall speak about the rite of communicating the people, for which above all this Supper of the Lord was instituted, and by that name is called. For as it is exceedingly absurd for a minister of the Word so to be deranged as to pronounce the Word in public ministry where there is no hearer — and, alone among rocks and trees, or under the open sky, to shout — so also it is most perverse if the ministers prepare and adorn the public Supper of the Lord where there are no guests to eat and drink, and they themselves alone (who ought to minister to others), at an empty table and hall, eat and drink. Wherefore, if we wish to embrace Christ's institution truly, no private Mass ought to be left in the church, unless either weakness or necessity be tolerated here for a time.
>
> Here also the rite is to be observed which is observed in Baptism. Namely, that first it be signified to the bishop who the future communicants are, and let them themselves petition to be communicated of the Lord's Supper, so that he may become acquainted with both their names and their life. Then let him not admit petitioners, unless they shall have rendered account of their faith, and, being interrogated, shall have answered whether they understand what the Lord's Supper is, what it confers, what use they will make of it — namely, whether they can recite the words of blessing from memory and expound them: whether they come for this reason, that vexed by conscience of sin or by fear of death, or by other evil of temptation of the flesh, the world, the devil, they hunger and thirst for the word and sign of grace and salvation from the Lord himself through the ministry of the Bishop, by which they may be consoled and strengthened — such as Christ by inestimable love has given and instituted in this Supper, when he said: "*Accipite et comedite* etc."
>
> I hold, however, this examination or interrogation to be sufficient if it be done once in the year with him who petitions to communicate. Nay, one who petitions can be so intelligent that either once in his whole life, or absolutely never, he need be interrogated. For by this rite we wish to guard against this, that the worthy and unworthy rush together to the Lord's Supper, as hitherto we have seen happen under the Roman Church, where nothing else was inquired but to be communicated — of faith, of consolation, and of the whole use and fruit of the Supper, no mention or thought was had. Nay, the very words of blessing, namely the very Bread of Life, they hid — with immense zeal, indeed with the utmost fury acting thus, so that communicants might work a good work by their worthiness, and not that they might nourish and strengthen faith through Christ's goodness. But we wish those who cannot answer according to what has been said to be entirely excluded and estranged from the communion of this Supper, as lacking the wedding garment.
>
> Then when the bishop shall have seen them understand all these things, he shall also observe this: whether by life and morals they approve that faith and understanding. For Satan too understands all these things and can speak them; that is: if he shall have seen anyone a fornicator, adulterer, drunkard, gambler, usurer, slanderer, or infamous by any other manifest crime, let him utterly exclude him from this Supper, unless by manifest argument he shall have testified that he has changed his life. For to those who sometimes fall and return and lament their fall, this Supper ought not merely not to be denied — nay, we ought to know it has been instituted especially for their sakes, that they may be refreshed and strengthened. "For in many things we all offend." And we bear one another's burdens, since we also mutually burden one another. For I speak of those contemners who shamelessly and without fear sin, and none the less boast great things of the Gospel.
>
> Further, where the Mass is celebrated, it is fitting that the communicants stand apart in one place and one company. For to this end the altar was invented, to this end the choir was invented — not that with God anything matters, to have stood here or there, or that any faith accrue from this, but because it is needful that they be openly seen and known both by those who communicate and by those who do not communicate, so that their life too may afterwards be better seen and proved and made manifest. For the communion of this Supper is part of the confession by which before God, angels, and men they profess themselves to be Christians. Therefore care must be taken lest they, as it were, secretly take away the Supper, and afterwards, mingled among others, it be ignored whether they live well or ill. Although here also I would not fix a law, but only demonstrate what is honourable and fitting to be freely performed by free Christians.
>
> Concerning private confession before communion, I hold as I have hitherto taught: that it is neither necessary nor to be exacted — yet useful and not to be contemned — since the Lord has not necessarily exacted or established as law even the Supper itself, but has permitted each freely, saying: "As often as ye do these things, etc." So we judge concerning preparation for this Supper: that it be free to compose oneself by fasting and prayer. Certainly sober one ought to be present, and diligent — that although you fast very little or pray little, nevertheless you belch not, nor grow torpid with a distended belly. For sobriety I mean not that superstitious one of the papists, but such as [prevents] crapula. For the best preparation is (as I said) the soul agitated by sins, death, temptations, hungering and thirsting for the medicine and strength. But whatever there is of these matters, it pertains to the bishop to teach the people.
>
> The last remaining question is whether both species (as they say) ought to be administered to the people. Here I say thus: since the Gospel has now for two whole years been inculcated among us, weakness has been indulged and given enough. Henceforth we are to act according to that saying of Paul: "He that is ignorant, let him be ignorant." Nor does it matter if those receive neither species again, who for so long a time have not known the Gospel, lest perhaps perpetual toleration of infirmity nourish obstinacy and prescribe against the Gospel. Wherefore, simply, according to the institution of Christ, let both species be both petitioned and administered. Those who shall not wish this, let them be left to themselves, and let nothing be administered to them. For we prescribe this form of the Mass to those to whom the Gospel has been announced and in some measure known. But to those who have not yet heard nor could have known it, in this matter nothing can yet be counselled.
>
> Nor ought anyone hold us back because they boast of a council in which it may be sanctioned that this is again permitted. We have Christ's law and can heed neither councils nor hear them in these things which are manifestly of the Gospel. Nay, we say further: If by any chance a council should decree and permit it, then least of all should we wish to use both species — nay, then first in contempt of both the council and its decree we should wish either only one species, or neither, and by no means both — and utterly to hold them anathema who by the authority of such council or decree should use both species. Do you marvel and seek the cause? Hear.
>
> If you know that bread and wine were instituted by Christ, namely that both are to be received by all — as the Gospels and Paul most clearly testify, so that even the adversaries themselves are compelled to confess this — yet do not dare to believe him and trust in him, so as to receive; but do dare to receive if men in their council decree it: Do you not then prefer men to Christ? Do you not exalt men of sin above God, who is called and worshipped? Do you not trust more in men's words than in God's words? Nay, you utterly distrust God's words and believe in men's words alone. But what an abomination and denial of the highest God is this? What idolatry can then be equal to your so religious obedience toward a council of men? Ought you not rather die a thousand times? Ought you not rather receive one species or none, than in such obedience so sacrilegious and in apostasy of faith receive?
>
> Let them therefore cease to boast of their councils. But let them first do this: let them make restitution for the sacrilege against divine glory; let them confess that with Satan as their master they have forbidden one species, have raised themselves above God, have condemned his word, and have destroyed so many peoples through so many ages; and let them do penance for this tyranny of unspeakable cruelty and impiety. And let them sanction that we have acted rightly, when even without — nay, against — their dogmas we have both taught and received both species, and have not awaited their council. And let them give thanks that we have refused to follow their perdition and abomination. After they shall have done these things, willingly and readily we shall adore and embrace their council and decree. Meanwhile, as long as they do not do this — but continue to demand that we await their authority — we hear nothing, but proceed both to teach and to do what is contrary to them, and this the more because we know it most displeases them. For by this diabolical demand what do they demand, unless that we exalt them above God, their words above God's words, and set for ourselves the monstrous ghosts of their apparitions as idols in place of God? — when we should wish the whole world to be subject to God and made obligated.
>
> I would wish that hymns also should be to us as many as possible in the vernacular, which the people would sing under the Mass — either alongside the graduals, likewise alongside the Sanctus and Agnus Dei. For who doubts that these were once the voices of the whole people, which now the choir alone sings or answers to the bishop blessing? These hymns can indeed be so arranged by the bishop that either together after the Latin chants, or by turns of days now in Latin, now in the vernacular, they be sung, until the whole Mass be made vernacular. But poets are lacking to us, or are not yet known, who might compose pious and spiritual songs (as Paul calls them) worthy to be frequented in the Church of God. In the meantime it pleases that that one be sung after the communion: "*Gott sei gelobet und gebenedeiet, der uns selber hat gespeiset*" etc., omitting that little clause "*Und das heilige sacramente, an unserm letzten ende, aus des geweieten priesters hende*," which was added by some worshipper of St. Barbara, who esteeming the sacrament of little worth throughout his whole life, hoped by this good work in death to enter life without faith. For both metre and reason of music prove that clause superfluous. Besides this, that one is worthy: "*Nu bitten wir den heiligen geist*." Likewise: "*Ein kindelin so lobelich*." For you would not find many which savour anything of gravity of spirit. These things I say, that if any German poets there be, they may be goaded to hammer out for us poems of piety.²³
>
> Here also (as I have said elsewhere) it must be done that this chant not be only speaking with the tongue, or rather only as a sound of pipe or cithara without sense. Therefore daily readings are to be instituted — one in the morning from the New or Old Testament, another in the evening from the other testament, with vernacular explanation. That this rite is ancient the thing itself proves, and the word "*homilia*" in matins, and "*capitulum*" in vespers and other hours — namely, that Christians, whenever they had come together, read something and expounded it in the vernacular, in the manner which Paul in 1 Cor. 14 describes. Afterwards, with the succession of a worse time, when prophets and interpreters were failing, that voice alone was left after the lessons and chapters, "*Deo gratias.*" Then in place of the interpretation, the lessons, psalms, and hymns, and other things were multiplied to this tedious prolixity. Although the hymns and "*Te Deum laudamus*" attest the same as "*Deo gratias*" — namely, that after the interpretations and homilies they praised God and gave thanks for the revealed truth of God's discourses. Such also I would wish our vernacular hymns to be.
>
> This much I have had, most excellent Nikolaus, to write to you about the rites and ceremonies of our Church at Wittenberg — partly now instituted and shortly (Christ willing) to be completed — of which, if it pleases you and others, let it be permitted to imitate the example. If not, we shall willingly give place to your anointing, prepared to receive from you and any others whatever is more suitable. Nor let it deter you or any others that in our Wittenberg there still remains that sacrilegious Topheth, which of the wicked and abandoned money of the princes of Saxony is — I mean the church of All Saints. For there is so much antidote among us, by the merciful God through the abundant word of God, that this plague, languishing in its corner, is pestilent to none but itself. Finally, there are scarcely three or four hogs and bellies in that very house of perdition who worship that money; to all others, and to the whole people alike, it is a signal nausea and abomination. Nor is it permitted by force or command to rage against them, since as you know it befits Christians to fight only with the strength of the sword of the Spirit. For so also I bridle the people daily; otherwise, already long ago that house of All Saints — nay, house of all devils — would go under another name in the world. But I have not exercised the power of the Spirit which God has given us against it, bearing with longsuffering that reproach — if perhaps God should give them repentance — content meanwhile that our house, which is more truly the house of all saints, here reigns and stands as the tower of Lebanon against the house of all devils. Thus we torture Satan by the Word, though he feign a smile. But Christ shall grant that his hope deceive him, and in the sight of all he be cast down. Pray for me, holy man of God. Grace with you and with all yours. AMEN.²⁴

**¹⁸** Modern reference for §2 (*Formula missae*): WA 12, 205–220; LW 53:15–40, "An Order of Mass and Communion for the Church at Wittenberg" (trans. Paul Zeller Strodach, rev. Ulrich S. Leupold). Nikolaus Hausmann (c. 1478/79 – 1538) was pastor at Zwickau from 1521 until his death in 1538; Luther consistently addressed him as *episcopus Cygneae ecclesiae* ("Bishop of the Church at Zwickau"), a title reflecting Luther's early theology of the pastoral office as episcopal *ex officio*. Zwickau (Latin *Cygnaea* / *Zwiccavia*) was a significant Saxon Reformation city. Paulus Speratus (Paul Sprett, 1484–1551), later Bishop of Pomesania in East Prussia, hymnist ("*Es ist das Heil uns kommen her*") and Luther's collaborator, translated the *Formula* for lay use in early 1524.

**¹⁹** The polemical typology Luther deploys here — Ahaz importing the pagan altar from Damascus (2 Kings 16:10–16), the Baal-standards set up in the Lord's temple (2 Kings 21:3–7; 23:4–7) — assimilates the Roman canon of the Mass to Old Testament acts of idolatrous cultic corruption. The "*man of sin*" (2 Thess. 2:3) is Luther's standard designation for the papal antichrist; the *abomination* (Matt. 24:15 / Dan. 12:11) similarly. That the *Formula* refers the reader to Luther's other works for the fuller argument against the Mass-as-sacrifice — supra "*abunde docuimus*" — points to *De captivitate Babylonica* (1520; WA 6, 497–573; LW 36:11–126) especially.

**²⁰** The image of the Ark of the Covenant in the temple of Dagon (1 Sam. 5:1–5) is Luther's polemical typology for the words of institution set within the Roman canon: the Ark (= Christ's own words of promise) smites the surrounding idolatrous rite until it is dismissed. The *Octavo* section is the polemical hinge of the whole *Formula*: everything before is preservation and modest reform; everything from *Octavo* on is the surgical excision of the Roman canon (the eucharistic prayer proper) as itself the "abomination."

**²¹** The retention of the elevation of the host at Wittenberg — resisted by Karlstadt and later the Sacramentarians — became a flashpoint. Luther retained it until 1542; his final abolition of the practice at Wittenberg on 12 August 1542 marked a shift, though he explicitly declined to make its removal binding on other congregations. See WA 38, 240–256.

**²²** The reduction of the eucharistic prayer to (i) the accustomed preface, (ii) the *verba* (Words of Institution) recited audibly with a modest tonal marking, and (iii) the *Sanctus* + *Benedictus* during the elevation — while cutting the whole Roman canon between the *Sanctus* and the *Pater noster* — is the concrete liturgical innovation for which the *Formula* is remembered. In effect Luther's Mass proceeds directly from Preface → Verba/Elevation → Pater noster → Pax Domini → Agnus Dei / Distribution, dropping the Canon entirely. This structural surgery underlies virtually every subsequent Lutheran territorial *Kirchenordnung* on the Sunday Mass, though details of what is retained or restored vary widely — most notably as between Nürnberg-Brandenburg 1533 (much fuller retention of medieval material) and Wittenberg 1533 / Braunschweig 1528 (Bugenhagen's northern German scheme, closer to the *Formula*).

**²³** The three vernacular hymns Luther recommends here are the first canonical corpus of Lutheran congregational song: (a) "*Gott sei gelobet und gebenedeiet*" — Luther's own reworking (c. 1524) of the medieval Corpus Christi sequence; (b) "*Nun bitten wir den heiligen Geist*" — Luther's expansion (c. 1524) of a pre-Reformation German Leise on the Holy Spirit; (c) "*Ein Kindelein so lobelich*" — a pre-Reformation Christmas carol. Luther's own "*Vom Himmel hoch da komm ich her*" and "*Ein feste Burg*" both post-date the *Formula*. The *Deutsche Messe* of 1526 (to be translated in tranche T2c) will greatly expand this hymnic material.

**²⁴** The "*Topheth*" at Wittenberg = the All Saints' Foundation (*Allerheiligenstift*), the collegiate church attached to the Castle Church (*Schlosskirche*) where Luther had posted the 95 Theses in 1517. The chapter of All Saints continued to celebrate the traditional (Roman) Mass privately after Luther's reforms, funded by the accumulated pre-Reformation endowments (the "*wicked and abandoned money of the princes of Saxony*") — a fact Luther found scandalous but tolerated on grounds of Christian liberty (only the sword of the Spirit, not the temporal sword, should act) until the chapter was finally dissolved. The reference to "three or four hogs and bellies" is Luther's characteristic polemical wit against the remaining canons. Cf. WA Br 3, no. 685; and Reinhold Jauernig, "Das Allerheiligenstift zu Wittenberg," in *Luther-Jahrbuch* 6 (1924), 24–108.
