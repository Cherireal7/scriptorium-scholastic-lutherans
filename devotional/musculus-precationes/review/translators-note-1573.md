# Translator’s Note

## 1. The book

Andreas Musculus (1514–1581), professor of theology at Frankfurt on the Oder and later one of the authors of the Formula of Concord, gathered this book of Latin prayers for the use of students, pastors and households. Its matter is not his own. As the title says, the prayers are taken *ex veteribus orthodoxis doctoribus* — out of the ancient teachers of the Church, above all the books of meditations, soliloquies and the manual that went under Augustine’s name, with Bernard, Jerome, Cyprian, Ambrose, Gregory and others; *ex Ecclesiae hymnis et canticis* — out of the hymns, collects, sequences and canticles of the Church’s services; and *ex Psalmis Davidis* — out of the Psalter. Musculus arranged them under eighteen heads, from the invocation of the Holy Trinity to the blessing of the table, so that a reader could find a form of prayer for the chief articles of the faith and for the chief needs of a Christian life: repentance, the holy Supper, newness of life, the cross, enemies, the devil, thanksgiving, sickness and death, morning and evening.

The collection was printed many times from the 1550s on, at Frankfurt on the Oder, Wittenberg, Erfurt and Leipzig, and was revised and enlarged by its compiler (see section 5 below). The edition translated here calls itself *nunc recens recognitae et auctae*, “now newly revised and enlarged”. Its dedicatory preface, addressed to four of Musculus’ colleagues in the University of Frankfurt, is dated at Frankfurt on the Oder on the eve of Mary Magdalene, 1570.

## 2. The text-base

This working English translation follows the Latin edition printed at Leipzig by Andreas Schneider with the types of Vögelin in 1573 (VD16 M 7192; 22 unnumbered leaves, 262 numbered pages, 1 leaf, in octavo). The copy used is that of the Bayerische Staatsbibliothek, Munich, shelf-mark Asc. 5554 s, in the library’s public-domain digital copy bsb00019110.

The translation was made from the page images of that copy, page by page. The library’s machine-read text was used as an aid only; where it and the image differ, the image was followed. The printer’s abbreviations have been expanded silently. Handwritten entries of former owners on the flyleaves of the Munich copy are not part of the book and are not translated.

The scan of this copy lacks one opening of the calendar, the two pages for May. They are supplied from the library’s second scan of the same edition (bsb10998571).

## 3. What is translated

Everything the printer set is here: the title page, the calendar with its month verses and saints’ days, the short notes on the year, the dedicatory preface, the eighteen sections of prayers, and the index. Running heads, signature marks and catchwords are left out.

## 4. Editorial conventions

**Page numbers.** The small red numbers in square brackets in the text mark the place where each page of the 1573 print begins, so that the English can be laid beside the Latin.

**Headings.** Each main section carries its English title with the Latin title beneath it. Within a section, each prayer stands under the name the book gives it — the Father or the book it is taken from (“Augustine, Meditations, chapter 12”), or “The Church” for hymns, collects and canticles of the services, or the number of the Psalm.

**Scripture.** Psalms and other passages of Scripture are conformed to the King James Version (1611), which is in the public domain. The book numbers the Psalms as the Hebrew Bible and the King James Version do, not as the Vulgate does, and its Latin Psalter is not the Vulgate but a newer version made closer to the Hebrew. Where that Latin plainly says something other than the King James Version, the Latin is translated. The pieces headed “gathered out of the Psalms” are chains of single verses from many Psalms; they are given verse by verse as printed, without references.

**GOD and LORD.** Inside a prayer, the print often sets *Deus* and *Dominus* in small capitals; these are given as GOD and LORD. The capitals of the first words of a prayer are the printer’s ornament and are not kept. In the Psalms the King James Version’s LORD is used in most places; this has not yet been made even through the whole book.

**Register.** The prayers are rendered in the traditional English of prayer — *thou*, *thee*, *thy* — so that the patristic prayers, the collects and the Psalms stand in one voice, as they do in the Latin.

**Hymns and verse.** Hymns, sequences and the verses of the calendar are translated line for line in plain prose lines, without rhyme or metre. The Latin first line is given in italics above each hymn.

**Attributions.** The attributions are those of the 1573 print. Many of the prayers that the sixteenth century read under the names of Augustine, Bernard or Jerome are now known to be the work of later, mostly medieval, writers; the book’s own ascriptions are kept as printed, without correction. Where a prayer has no name over it in the print, none has been added.

**Misprints.** Where the print is plainly wrong and the right reading is not in doubt, the right reading is translated and the printed one is given in a note. Where the print differs from the usual text of its source but makes sense, the print is followed.

**Uncertain readings.** A small red query in square brackets marks a word that could not be read with certainty on the page image. Notes, where there are any, stand at the end of the section they belong to.

## 5. The edition of 1561

The book was printed many times, and its compiler enlarged it more than once. The Frankfurt on the Oder edition of 1561, which its own dedication calls the third, was laid beside the Leipzig edition of 1573. All that the 1561 edition prints among its prayers stands also in the 1573 edition, in the same order; the later edition adds a calendar, a new preface, an index, and ninety-four further pieces at the ends of ten sections. A reader who has the 1561 edition before him will therefore find every one of its prayers in this volume. The first appendix gives the title page and dedication of 1561; the second lists what the 1573 edition adds and shows where each section begins in both.

## 6. Status

This is a working edition. Every page of the translation has been read a second time against the page images of the 1573 print. The shared text of the 1561 and 1573 editions has been compared in samples, not word for word; the other printings (among them Frankfurt on the Oder 1559, Wittenberg 1562, Leipzig 1571 and 1580) have not been compared; and the sources of the single prayers have not been traced beyond what the book itself names.
