In this table thou shalt find the Sunday letter thus: assign the first letter, which is g, to the year 1570; the next, which is f, assign to the year 1571. And so go forward in order, until thou come to the letter upon which the number of that year falleth; that will be the Sunday letter. If it be single, the year will be a common year; if double, a leap year. And then the former, that is, the upper, will serve until the 24th of February; but the lower will be fitted to the remaining part of the year. Now it is to be known that, when 28 years are ended, one must return to the beginning, with the years then running.

## The Golden Number
*Aureus numerus*

XIII. XIIII. XV. XVI. XVII. XVIII. XIX. I. II. III. IIII. V. VI. VII. VIII. IX. X. XI. XII.

In like manner as hath been said of the Sunday letter, thou shalt discover the golden number in the series of numbers written above. For the first number, which is XIII, assign to the year 1570; the next, which is XIIII, assign to the year 1571; and so forward, until thou come to thine own year. When 19 years are ended, go back again to the beginning.

Now the Calendar following serveth our region, so far as concerneth the rising and setting of the sun; for it is fitted to the elevation of 52 degrees.[^1]

# Of the Year and its Parts
*De Anno et partibus eius*

{img.42} The year hath twelve months, fifty-two weeks and one day; and three hundred and sixty-five days and six hours.

| Sixty-five, three hundreds, and six hours the year containeth;
| these give the leap year in every fourth year.

## Of how many days each month consisteth

| April hath thrice ten, June, and September, November;
| the rest one more; February twenty-eight.
| But if it be leap year, one is added over and above;
| let *Mat, Mat* be said, but the feast is not changed.

## Of the Leap Year

That year is called a leap year (*bissextilis*) in which one day is intercalated, that is, inserted, before the feast of Matthias, on the 6th of the Kalends of March.

| The sixth of the Kalends of March hath held the leap day;
| on the latter day the feast of Matthias is kept.

## Of the Number of the Kalends, Nones and Ides in each Month

| {img.43} The first day of every month is called the Kalends;
| six Nones have May, October, July and March,
| but the rest four; each will give eight Ides;
| thereafter say that all the other days are Kalends,
| which, counting backward, thou shalt name from the month following.

## Of the Four Seasons of the Year

| Clement giveth winter, Peter in his Chair giveth spring,
| Urban bringeth the summer heat, Symphorian bringeth autumn.

### Manilius

| Summer riseth from the Twins, autumn from the Virgin;
| winter beginneth with the Archer, spring with the Fishes.

## Of the Equinoxes of the Year and the Solstices

| At Lambert and at Gregory the night is made equal to the day;
| Vitus and Lucy are the two solstices.

## The Fasts of the Four Ember Seasons of the Year

| {img.44} After the Cross, after the Ashes, after the Spirit, and after Lucy,
| let the Wednesday next following be thy Ember day.

## Of the Immovable Feasts

| Six there are unto the Purification, twice six unto Philip,
| as many unto James, nine there are unto Michael,
| six unto Martin, six unto the Nativity of Christ;
| add eight days, and the whole year shall be filled up.

## The Qualities of the Four Parts of the Year

| Summer is dry and hot, autumn dry and cold;
| winter is cold and moist, spring is warm and moist.[^8]

## Of the Weather

| A clear day of Paul betokeneth good seasons of the year;
| if there be winds, they betoken battles for the people;
| {img.45} if there be mists, all manner of beasts perish;
| if snow or rain, it betokeneth times of dearth.

## Of the Presages of the Moon

| A pale moon raineth; a ruddy one bloweth; a white one maketh fair weather.

### Basil

If the moon on the third day after the conjunction shine with a thin and pure light, it promiseth settled fair weather; but if it be dark, it is believed to portend rains:

| But if she spread a maiden blush over her face,
| there will be wind; golden Phoebe ever reddeneth with wind.[^6]
