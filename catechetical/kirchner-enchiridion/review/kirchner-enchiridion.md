# Translator's Note

## 1. The text base

The English rendered here follows the **1583 Heidelberg edition** printed by **Johann Spies**, the *editio princeps* of Kirchner's *Enchiridion*, cited in the Verzeichnis der im deutschen Sprachbereich erschienenen Drucke des 16. Jahrhunderts as **VD16 K 1037**.¹ The base text used is the facsimile held by the Bavarian State Library, Munich — shelfmark **Catech. 388**, digital identifier `bsb00020680`, URN `urn:nbn:de:bvb:12-bsb00020680-7` — served through the Munich Digitization Centre (MDZ). The title page carries the Roman numeral **M.D.LXXXIII (1583)**; the Widmungsvorrede is datelined *Heidelberg den 6. Septembris / im Jar Chrifti vnsers Seligmachers / 1583* — 6 September 1583. The BSB catalogue records the item as 1584, presumably reflecting distribution rather than imprint. All references in the present edition follow the 1583 title-page date.

The 1583 imprint stands at a hinge of Palatine confessional history. Elector Palatine **Ludwig VI**, to whom the Vorrede is dedicated, had inherited his father Friedrich III's Reformed Palatinate in 1576 and had turned it decisively back to Lutheranism. Kirchner had served as his court preacher (*Hofprediger*) at Heidelberg for four years, as he tells us at cv 25. Ludwig VI died on **12 October 1583** — only thirty-six days after the Vorrede was signed. Under regent Johann Casimir, the Palatinate reverted immediately to the Reformed confession, and the *Enchiridion* went to press in the final month of the last Lutheran interval Heidelberg would see for over a century.² Its double polemical target — the Papists on one flank, the Sacramentarians (Reformed) on the other — is a Heidelberg-specific position document, printed in a city already tipping back toward the party against whom half its arguments are levelled.

Kirchner had also, in the same year 1583, co-authored with **Martin Chemnitz** and **Nikolaus Selnecker** the *Apologia oder Verantwortung des christlichen Concordien-Buchs* — the official *Apology of the Book of Concord* defending the 1580 Formula and Book of Concord against Reformed and irenicist critics.³ The Widmungsvorrede's third rationale for the dedication (cv 27) refers directly to this work: Kirchner intends "by God's grace to remain by God's holy infallible Word as the sole foundation of the pure doctrine, and not to depart in the least from the confession of the saving faith **repeated in the Christian Book of Concord**." The *Enchiridion* is the catechetical footprint of the 1583 *Apology* — the same author, the same year, the same doctrinal binding, cast now in Q&A form for parochial use.

## 2. What the author himself supplies

Kirchner supplies the whole. His running text preserves:

- Scriptural citations in his own abbreviations (**Erod. 32** for Exodus 32, **Deut. 4** for Deuteronomy 4, **Pfal. 19** for Psalm 19, **Actor. 1** for Acts 1, **Rom.** and **1. Tim.** and **2. Pet.** in their 1583 forms), with occasional chapter-references only (**Nehem. 4**) where Kirchner reasons at length from a single narrative;
- Patristic quotations verbatim in their original language — **Athanasius** *Contra gentes* on the sufficiency of Scripture; **Augustine** to Jerome (*Epistula* 82) on the difference between the canonical books and the writings of the Fathers; **Augustine** *De peccatorum meritis et remissione* on the inerrancy of Scripture; the **Symbolum Chalcedonense** in the *Canon Symboli Chalcedonensis apud Nicephorum* form (cv 55, forthcoming Tranche 3);
- His own polemical enumerations — the four properties of Scripture, the four grounds of its certainty, the fivefold power of the Word, the fourfold difference between heathen and Christian doctrine — all preserved with their numbering intact and their internal citations undisturbed.

No paraphrase. Where a passage in Kirchner is ambiguous — chiefly on lines where the 1583 OCR yielded a broken word — the disambiguation is discussed in an endnote and the text held to the clearest defensible reading.

## 3. What Scholastic Lutherans added — and where

All modern editorial matter lives in endnotes flagged by superscript numerals.⁴ Nothing modern intrudes into the body. The endnotes carry:

- Life-dates for named authors on first reference (Kirchner 1533–1587; Elector Ludwig VI 1539–1583; Johann Spies fl. 1580–ca. 1623; Chemnitz 1522–1586; Selnecker 1530–1592; Nehemiah as historically located; Athanasius ca. 296–373; Augustine 354–430);
- Modern edition references where a Kirchner citation is opaque or where a modern reader will want to check: Augustine *Epistula* 82 numbering, Athanasius *Contra gentes* section-number, the Chalcedonian Definition in **Denzinger-Hünermann** numbering, the *Apology of the Book of Concord* in the standard 1583 imprint (Spies, Heidelberg, ff.);
- Historical annotation where the Vorrede's typological argument turns on 16th-century events — the Eck bull of 1520, the Schmalkaldic War of 1546–1548, the Palatine confessional oscillations of 1559 / 1576 / 1583;
- Cross-references to the Book of Concord (BSLK / Kolb-Wengert) whenever Kirchner alludes to a Formula article or a Symbol.

## 4. Editorial conventions

Following the [Praetorius](../../shared/) reference implementation and the Chytraeus catechetical sibling volume:

- **Register:** scholarly-critical English, reverent but not archaizing. Kirchner's baroque doublets are preserved in substance without being reproduced beat-for-beat where English idiom cannot bear them.
- **Q&A layout:** questions in **bold**, answers in regular weight, Kirchner's numbered points preserved. Where the source runs question and answer together without a paragraph break, the English inserts one for legibility and marks nothing.
- **Latin quotations** are preserved in italic Roman with an English rendering following in parentheses or on the next line; the same rule holds for Greek. Kirchner uses no Hebrew in this Hauptstück.
- **Vocabulary:** the master glossary at `../../shared/terms.yaml` governs. New Kirchner-specific terms this tranche adds: *Enchiridion* (preserved untranslated as the book's own name); *Sacramentierer* rendered as "**Sacramentarians**" (Kirchner's standard 1583 label for the Reformed on the Supper); *Hauptstück* rendered as "**chief chapter**" or "**chief head**"; *Symbolum Apostolicum* / *Chalcedonense* preserved untranslated; *Concordi Buch* rendered "**Book of Concord**"; *Papisten* rendered "**Papists**" per Kirchner's own polemical usage. The Nehemiah-typological cluster (*Kirchenbaw*, *Widerauffbawung*, *Schwerdt des Geistes*) is rendered "the **building of the Church**," "the **rebuilding**," "the **sword of the Spirit**" — the metaphor is Kirchner's spine and must survive the translation intact.
- **Bible:** ESV for English rendering. Vulgate numbering is noted where Kirchner cites in Vulgate form. **Erod.** = Exodus; **Actor.** = Acts; **Pfal.** = Psalm; **Deut.** = Deuteronomy; **Genef.** = Genesis; **Jefai.** = Isaiah. Modernised inline; the 1583 abbreviations are recorded in an endnote at first occurrence only.
- **Orthography:** 1583 forms silently modernised in the English — *vnd* → *und* in preserved German phrases; long-*ſ* → *s*; *w.* (Kirchner's mid-word abbreviation) expanded; hyphenated line-breaks joined; the terminal *ẑ* rendered *ß* in German source citations, *s* in English body. Where the 1583 orthography preserves a doctrinally meaningful spelling — *Christlichen Concordi Buchs* — it stands.
- **Dividers:** `— ❦ —` (em-dash · aldus leaf · em-dash) at section breaks.

## 5. What is not yet in this working translation

This first tranche shipping 2026-08-30 comprises: **title page, dedication epigraph, Widmungsvorrede in full (cv 7–28), and Hauptstück I *Von der Heiligen Schrifft* in full (cv 29–44)**. That is roughly the first 40% of the total 108-canvas book by source page and closer to 30% by translated word-count, since Hauptstück I is a compact locus and the Christological block (cv ~50–100) will absorb the majority of the remainder.

Reserved for future tranches:

- **Tranche 2 · Hauptstück II *Von Gott*** and any intervening loci on the Trinity, Creation, and the Fall (cv 45 ff.);
- **Tranche 3 · Von der Person unseres HERREN Christi*** — the extended Christology of ubiquity, *communicatio idiomatum*, Chalcedon-*Contra*-Nestorius, and the anti-Sacramentarian *Genus maiestaticum* which is the polemical core of the entire *Enchiridion*. Kirchner's Q&A here is the catechetical distillation of Formula of Concord Articles VII and VIII (Lord's Supper and Person of Christ).⁵ This block runs cv ~50 to at least cv 100 and is the largest single locus of the work;
- **Tranche 4 · Justification and the closing loci** (cv ~105–107) — visible in the source at cv 107 quoting Rom 3, 2 Cor 5, 1 John 2, Gal 3, Rom 11, and Isa 53 on Christ's atonement for the whole world — with the imprint colophon at cv 108.

Also reserved for a future tranche is any comparative apparatus against Kirchner's Latin systematic recasting of the same material, the *Methodica explicatio praecipuorum capitum doctrinae coelestis* (1586, printed at both Wipprecht and Steinman; second edition 1595, Beyer; approximately 1000–1300 pages folio). Google Books holds three editions in facsimile;⁶ a full Latin-versus-German collation would substantially thicken the endnote apparatus and is a plausible companion volume in its own right rather than a *Methodica*-inclusive edition of this book.

# Dedication

### *To the most illustrious, high-born Prince and Lord, Ludwig, Elector Palatine of the Rhine⁷ — grace and peace from God through Jesus Christ.*

Most gracious Elector and Lord,

Among the beautiful histories which Holy Scripture preserves for the instruction of the Christian Church, there is scarcely one more suited to our own time than the account of the rebuilding of the city of Jerusalem after the Babylonian captivity, which the Lord God carried through and brought to completion by Nehemiah — over and against the counsels of Nehemiah's own hesitations and of every enemy who mocked the work. That whole history is a true type of the rebuilding of the Church of God, that is, of the whole company of Christendom, after the Babylonian or papal captivity in which she lay for many hundreds of years, under the Roman popes, until there was scarcely a hope that she could ever be delivered out of it.

But as the Lord God delivered his Jewish people, when the appointed seventy years were run, through the Persian monarch Cyrus, freed them, and commanded that the Temple be rebuilt: so also, out of his groundless mercy, he has in these last times looked upon his whole Christendom with the eyes of grace, has wonderfully released her from the long antichristian Babylonian captivity, and has begun once more to build his spiritual Temple, that is, his holy Congregation. It was Dr. Luther, most gracious Elector, through whose writings the fallen worship was raised, the pure and wholesome doctrine again brought to light, and the horrible darkness and idolatry of the papacy exposed and put to flight. Yet who could have imagined that such a transformation should follow — that the papal doctrine and worship should fall away so utterly, as when the Samaritans, Ammonites, and Moabites, hearing that the walls of Jerusalem and the Temple were being rebuilt, grew furious and derided the people of God, saying: *"What are they doing? Will they bring the stones of the burnt heap to life again, will they offer sacrifice, will they finish it all in a single day?"* — and holding it for impossible that so great a work could be brought to conclusion? Exactly so did it go with our churches and their Christian reformation and rebuilding: for the beginnings were despised and mocked.

Whence the Papists at first had confidence in their own humble roots, and thought that although Dr. Luther built with his writing, it would come to nothing — as they answered him in Dr. Eck's writing sent from Rome in 1520, and in the papal bull issued against him.⁸ For the Roman Church had grown so mighty that they judged Luther's undertaking impossible. Yet God ensured that the work should go forward: for though Luther himself may not always have foreseen the full scope of what was to come, divine providence carried it on, so that it *had* to advance — no less than the exodus from Egypt and the deliverance from Babylon. Vainly did the enemies imagine themselves as foxes that could tear down stone walls; God has established what he began, so that it cannot be overcome by fox's cunning nor by lion's force.

Nor did the enemies stop at mockery. They rose with the full weight of their power and determination to suppress the holy Gospel and to destroy the churches of Germany, particularly around the years 1546 to 1548.⁹ But God has always and everywhere brought the practices of his adversaries wonderfully to light: he preserves his own where the case appears most hopeless, and exposes the plots that in secret were laid against his Church.

For this is the character of the Lord's word to his people in every generation: *"Fear not, I am with you; do not draw back, for I am your God"* (Isaiah 41). The people, in Nehemiah's day, complained to their Lord and God that they were held in such contempt by the heathen. They prayed, and they set the watch. They joined the walls one to another so that the people gained one heart. And, as Sanballat, Tobiah, and the Arabs, Ammonites, and Ashdodites made a league against Jerusalem, the Temple, and its builders, to come together and to fight against it — the people prayed to the Lord God against them, and set the watch against them. In the same way we too, in these dangerous times, when the enemies of the Gospel — the Pope and the sects — with all their might would suppress the Gospel and the right use of the sacraments, and bind themselves against Jerusalem and the Temple of God: we too should freshly and confidently lay hold on prayer, and appeal to God against them.¹⁰

But this is not enough. Let us take Nehemiah's five ordinances also as a model for our own building of the Church.

**First**, Nehemiah, when the people through weariness and the fear of enemies would have lost heart and drawn their hand from the building, exhorted them to steadfastness and roused them with faithful diligence: to hold fast in prayer against the practices and violence of the enemies, and with the sword of the Spirit — that is, with the Word of God — to fight the false doctrine, the idolatry of the papacy, and every erring teaching; and in the building of Jerusalem, that is, of Christendom, to go forward confident and unafraid, and not grow slack. For God will surely preserve his people, will plant the pure doctrine upon the coming generations, and will steer error and idolatry off, if only we pray diligently, and press on in the exercise of the divine Word by every mode that God has appointed, and do not let ourselves be wearied. Though the enemies assail us at the sharpest, still God takes another path; the outcome stands in his hand and not in theirs. And though we should suffer all kinds of peril and stand ready for it, that is no matter: God will hold back for himself a holy remnant, come what may. For this cannot fail — if we stand fast by his Word and truth, he stands fast by us; and rather than that we should fall away to the enemies, they must fall away to us, Jeremiah 15.

**Second**, among all Nehemiah's works one is especially worth remembering: in that troubled time, when he saw the approaching danger before his eyes, he made this praiseworthy arrangement — first, that the young men should be divided in halves, one half labouring, the other half keeping watch and holding the spears; and second, that others should labour with one hand, while with the other they held their weapons; so that each who built had his sword girded to his loins, and built at the same time.

Now, as Nehemiah in that time set the young men to it, one half to labour and the other half to hold the spears: so ought today all upright teachers, especially in schools, to devote themselves with their utmost ability to guiding young men faithfully and prudently to the labour of the Church-building. And that in such fashion, that they should first be trained to study and learn well and thoroughly the wholesome doctrine of God's Word in Holy Scripture, both of the Old and the New Testament, so that afterwards in the building of the Christian Church they may labour or serve the more fruitfully and profitably, and may build up the Church of God with wholesome doctrine and holy walk. Likewise also, that they should train up the youth to hold the spears — that is, to be mighty in the sayings of Holy Scripture, and to bring the whole of Christendom against the enemies of the pure doctrine, the heresies, the errors, and the perverted teaching, rightly and with profit. In sum: they should accustom their beloved studying youth to this, that they build with the one hand and with the other learn to hold the weapons — that is, that with the pure doctrine *in thesi* they lay the true foundation, and with the other hand stand armed to rebuke the gainsayers, and upon the firm ground of holy divine Scripture to stop their mouth (Titus 1). For to this end the youth in Christian schools and academies is specially trained in languages, arts, and Holy Scripture — that they may know both how to spread abroad the pure teaching as duty requires, and how to meet the gainsayers with a steady foundation and with true theological earnestness, decently. For the building of Jerusalem and of God's Temple cannot dispense with either. And this ought to happen not only among the youth in schools: it ought also to be earnestly and without ceasing exhorted throughout the whole of Christendom and among all its upright members.

Nor is this the work of the great wonder-men of the Church of God — Nehemiahs and their like — alone: whoever has received from God gifts serviceable to the Church-building, let him apply them; let him, so far as in him lies, in his own place and among his own, without ceasing rouse and encourage, so that every man may be so instructed, taught, and armed, that he may serve the Church of God and defend himself with God's Word against the enemies of the Church of Christ.

To this Christian end I too have, according to my simplicity and by the modest gifts God has bestowed on me, looked when I compiled this my *Enchiridion*: first, that I myself, so far as in me lies, might help forward the building of the holy Church in these parts. Second, that I might also lead others rightly to grasp the chief points of Christian doctrine, and to know and to avoid the contrary doctrine of the Pope and his Jesuits — who now dare to give a colour and a gloss to all the falsehoods and errors of the papacy, and, in particular, most miserably and wickedly, together with the Sacramentarians (with whom on this question they blow altogether in one horn), to pervert the high article of the Person and Majesty of the human nature in Christ.¹¹ I have also endeavoured, by God's grace, to set this forth in clear, understandable, sound, and wholesome words, according to the Apostle's command in 2 Timothy 1: *Hold fast the pattern of sound words which thou hast heard.*

For it has been my special intention to compress the chief chapters of Christian doctrine — those, that is, which are in dispute both between the Papists and us, and between us and the Sacramentarians and others — so far as the principal matter of them is concerned, briefly out of the more extensive and detailed writings that have been published on these questions, and to gather them into this short *Enchiridion* or hand-book. So that even those who either cannot read the extensive writings, or cannot orient themselves in them sufficiently, may in this little book read as it were in a summary, in a short time, and weigh with the fear and invocation of God whereupon the matter rests, and which side has the better foundation from God's Word to bring for itself.

For all that, it is not at all my intention with this modest labour to displace from other people's hands the other useful and good little books in which the chief substance of Christian doctrine is briefly drawn from the wells of Israel, and by which much good has hitherto been done for Christendom. My intent is rather to make my own confession here publicly on the chief articles of the Christian doctrine, and, so far as in me lies, hereby to serve the youth and the common Church among us. If anyone else has need to use it for himself, that stands open. I have, so far as in me lies, meant to serve pious hearts and to injure none. May the Almighty grant his blessing thereto, that it be not in vain. I could well have treated all the matters somewhat more amply and at greater length: but I have kept to this, that the little book should not become too large — but should remain such as one may daily carry with him. For to every Christian it is no small matter that in every sermon he should attend with particular diligence to which article of doctrine is being treated, and should then look up that article in this or a similar little book, and read it after, and compare it with the sermon that has been preached. There he will find that he understands everything more thoroughly, and that he is more firmly settled in the doctrine, for which I can never sufficiently thank the Lord God.¹²

— ❦ —

Your Electoral Grace's *own* Enchiridion I have wished, however, to dedicate for the following reasons.

**First**, since I have now for four years been Your Electoral Grace's most humble servant, and have in that time — God be praised and thanked — perceived that Your Electoral Grace has taken the true Christian religion to heart with all your heart, that you love the truth of the Gospel, and that you have turned away from all the erring doctrine of the papacy and of other sects; that Your Electoral Grace has made your Christian and godly confession and zeal known before the whole of Christendom, both by the reformation of churches and schools and by the furtherance of the Christian Concord-Work and *Apologia* — that you have not been ashamed of the name of Jesus Christ and the holy Gospel or Word of God before this evil world:¹³ this has always and continually rejoiced me in my heart so greatly that I cannot sufficiently thank the Lord God for it.

**Second**, that Your Electoral Grace even to this day, in following the praiseworthy example of Nehemiah, has spared no effort in whatever may serve the furtherance and continuation of Jerusalem and the rebuilding of the Temple or Church of God. For Your Electoral Grace, no less than Nehemiah, both in your particular schools and above all in your praiseworthy University,¹⁴ rightly raises up the beloved youth, and through God's grace has them so trained that they are fit — with the one hand to build up the Temple of God, that is, common Christendom, with sound doctrine; and with the other, armed and equipped, to gainsay the enemies of the pure doctrine, and to defend the truth against them upon the firm foundation of Holy Scripture. Whence Your Electoral Grace is, before the whole of Christendom, no less than Nehemiah to be praised and lauded.

**Third**, that I might herewith briefly and publicly repeat, before Your Electoral Grace, my confession on the chief articles of our Christian faith: so that both Your Electoral Grace and others might perceive from it what sort of doctrine, in Your Electoral Grace's praiseworthy University, I set before the youth; and that I intend, by God's grace, to remain by God's holy infallible Word as the sole foundation of the pure doctrine, and not to depart in the least from the confession of the saving faith **repeated in the Christian Book of Concord** — but, so far as God helps me, to persist steadfastly.¹⁵

**Fourth**, that, in return for the manifold benefits so graciously shown by Your Electoral Grace, unworthy as I and mine are, I might most humbly, by this modest dedication, testify what gratitude I could. I stand herein in the most humble confidence that Your Electoral Grace will not take this ungraciously, and that you will continue to grant your Electoral Grace's favour to me and mine hereafter as heretofore, and let us be commended to it.

Herewith I commend Your Electoral Grace, together with your Electoral Consort, the young Lordship, and the Electoral Government, into the gracious protection of the Almighty. May he, in his mercy, preserve Your Electoral Grace in long-lasting good health, for the best of your Consort, the young Lordship, the churches and schools, the land and the principality. **Amen.**

&nbsp;

*Your Electoral Grace's*

*most humble servant,*

# Hauptstück I

### *Von der Heiligen Schrifft, oder Wort Gottes*

*On Holy Scripture, or the Word of God*

— ❦ —

## § 1. Whence it is proven that the Christian religion alone is the true religion and faith

**From what is it proven that the Christian religion and faith alone is the right religion and faith?**

**First**, from *antiquity*. For no faith upon earth — however it may be called — has stood so long as this faith. Adam and all the fathers from the beginning of the world have had this faith; and all the blessed will have this faith, until the end of the world (Gen. 3; 4; 5; Acts 15; Rom. 4).

**Second**, no faith on earth has done such wonder-signs as this faith. On this let one read all the histories of the Old and New Testaments, and so on. The other faiths in the world have not been able to do such signs, though they attempted them.

**Third**, no faith on earth has had so many martyrs, who have fought so knightly, so bravely, and so contended against all offence and persecution. Many Christians from the beginning have been slain for it; but this faith has endured against all others — although it was as if the smaller and the others were pressing against it on every side.¹⁶

**Fourth**, no faith from the beginning of the world has been so certainly proclaimed and prophesied as to what is to come as this Christian faith. The Christian faith has sure prophecy of how it and other faiths in the world will fare, and so on. And history rhymes finely with the prophecies.

**Fifth**, especially is the doctrine of the Gospel — which Christendom alone has — a doctrine of which no human reason naturally knows, and without which no lasting comfort in temptations (*Anfechtungen*) can be drawn or grasped. And the experience of all the true-believing testifies that it works in them steadfast comfort, peace, and joy of heart in tribulation; that they can well come to peace, and conclude that with God they are well: Rom. 5. *Now, being justified by faith through faith in Christ, we have peace with God, and access to grace.*

## § 2. What God's Word or Holy Scripture is

**What then is God's Word, or the Holy Scripture?**

It is a doctrine given by God himself, in which Law and Gospel are set forth, and it is taught what God according to his essence is, and also what his will is toward us men. John 17: *Sanctify them in thy truth; thy Word is truth.*

## § 3. The chief chapters of Scripture: Law and Gospel

**Which are the chief chapters of Scripture, or of the Word?**

Two: the **Law** and the **Gospel**.

The **Law** commands and requires of us what we ought to do and to leave undone (Exodus 20).

The **Gospel**, however, does not preach what we are to do and to leave undone; but only bids us to hold out and to take with the lap of our garment, and says: *"Look, dear man — this has God done for thee. He has given thee his Son, has had him slain for thy sake"* (John 3: *God so loved the world that he gave his only-begotten Son*).¹⁷

## § 4. Whence Holy Scripture takes its beginning; by whom first brought forth

**Whence does the Holy Scripture take its beginning?**

From God himself. For in Exodus 32 it stands thus: *Moses turned and went down from the mount, and had the two tables of the testimony in his hand; they were written on both sides; and God had made them himself, and himself had graved the writing on them.* From this it clearly appears that God himself made the first beginning of putting his Word and revelation into writing. And from this also is the loftiness of the Holy Divine Scripture to be recognised.

Moses, then, wrote out the whole Law by God's command, as Exodus 33 stands, and left it to the children of Israel. Him have the Prophets, Evangelists, and Apostles followed, and so on.

**By whom, then, was the Word of God first brought forth?**

By God himself. As the 103rd Psalm says: *He made his ways known to Moses, his deeds to the children of Israel.* And 2 Tim. 3: *All Scripture given of God by inspiration is profitable for doctrine*, and so on.

**What moved him to it?**

Nothing else but his unspeakable mercy, according to which he willed to make himself known to us men by a certain and steadfast Word. Especially so, since men had fallen into sin and lost the right knowledge of God, he did not will that we all should altogether and wholly lack, in such blindness and ignorance, the knowledge of God our Creator and of his will toward us. For otherwise we should all have had to be eternally damned and lost, and to remain so.

**With what men, then, is such Word of God to be found?**

With none other than the true Congregation of God, to whom God has revealed himself through his Word and wonder-signs. As Deut. 4 stands: *This shall be your wisdom and understanding in the sight of all peoples, that when they hear all these commandments they shall have to say: See, wise and understanding people are these, and a noble people — for where is so noble a people to whom the gods draw so near as the LORD our God, whenever we call upon him? And where is so noble a people that has statutes and ordinances so just as all this law which I set before you this day?* And likewise Psalm 147: *He shows his Word to Jacob, his statutes and judgements to Israel; not so has he done to any of the heathen, neither has he made known to them his judgements.* John 10: *My sheep hear my voice, and a stranger they do not follow.*

**What should a Christian principally seek in Holy Scripture, and to what does the Holy Scripture chiefly point?**

In Holy Scripture one is above all to seek **Christ Jesus, the Saviour of the world**; for all Scripture principally points to and shows him. John 5: *These are they that bear witness of me.*

## § 5. The four grounds of Scripture's certainty; the four properties of Scripture

**Which are the chief properties of the Holy Scripture?**

These:

**1.** That Scripture is the true mistress over all other human writings. For all writings of the Fathers and of men must be proved by her, or otherwise they do not stand. Ps. 19: *The testimony of the LORD is sure.*

**2.** That she is more certain than all Councils. For the Councils too must direct themselves according to her (Gal. 1: *Though an angel from heaven should preach another Gospel to you than that which we have preached to you, let him be accursed*).

**3.** That she suffers no human addition. She will be alone, or nothing at all (Ps. 12; whence she is called a pure silver).

**4.** That she is not to be learned to the end. As the 147th Psalm says of her: *Of his understanding there is no number.* Whence every Christian, of high and low estate, should search in the Holy Scripture day and night.

**Whence, then, is it clear that the Holy Scripture is a certain doctrine, on which one may and must rely?**

From this: **First**, that she was begun to be written by God himself.

**Second**, that she was inspired by God himself (2 Tim. 3).

**Third**, that she has been confirmed with such exceeding divine wonder-works from the very beginning.

**Fourth**, that the holy men who wrote her were themselves driven to it by God. 2 Peter 1: *We have a sure prophetic word, and ye do well that ye take heed to it as to a light that shineth in a dark place*, and so on.

## § 6. Against the Papist assertion that Scripture has its authority from the Church

**Does the Pope say rightly, then, that the Scripture has its regard from the Church?**

No. For she has her authority and regard from God himself. And that the Pope draws the regard of Scripture upon the Church — that is at bottom just as much as if one said the daughter were above the mother, or the wife above her husband. For the Scripture, or God's Word, is as it were the mother through which the Christian Church is begotten; and is as it were a husband over his wife, and not the other way about.

**Yes,** they say, **but the Church was before the Scripture?**

Nothing hangs upon this: that there were believing Christians before God's Word was set down and written. For the Word was there before, though it was not straightway written down. The first men, Adam and Eve, had no written Word; but they had God's Word all the same, and by that Word were called into the fellowship of the faith and of the Church (as Gen. 3, 4, and following show). For if God had not revealed himself to them through the Word, they would have known nothing certain of him, nor been true members of his Church.

## § 7. Whether everything necessary for salvation is found in Scripture

**Is there, then, in Scripture found everything that is necessary to salvation?**

The Papists say no here, but they do it altogether wrongly. For 2 Tim. 3 stands clearly: *All Scripture given by inspiration of God is profitable for doctrine, for reproof, for correction, for instruction in righteousness, that the man of God may be perfect, throughly furnished unto all good works.*

Whence also Christ says (Luke 16): *They have Moses and the Prophets; let them hear them.* Now although at that time the New Testament was not yet written, nothing has since been written in the New Testament which had not its sure ground already in the Old, as one perceives from the concordant witness of the Old and New Testaments.

And John 20: *And many other signs did Jesus in the presence of his disciples, which are not written in this book. But these are written, that ye may believe that Jesus is the Christ*, and so on.

Whence also **Athanasius**, *Contra gentes*, writes: *Sufficere Scripturas divinitus inspiratas, ad omnem instructionem veritatis* — that the Holy Scripture is complete to give report of all that pertains to the truth.¹⁸

## § 8. How a Christian is to orient himself in interpretation

**How is a Christian to conduct himself in the exposition of Scripture, that he may know which exposition is right or not?**

By this rule shall a Christian abide, which St. Paul gives (Rom. 12): *All prophecy or exposition shall be conformable to the faith.* Those teachers, then, who so teach that they teach what is conformable to the faith in Christ (or to the chief chapters of the Catechism) — these we are to hear, and their teaching to receive. But whoever does not teach conformably to this — him one is neither to hear nor to see: be he Pope, or whosoever he may be. For we are baptised upon Christ, that we might believe his Word; and are not baptised upon the teachers, or upon Popes, or upon churches. St. Paul's rule at Rom. 12 cannot fail us.

**Is a Christian, then, straightway bound to believe when only a passage from Scripture is quoted?**

No. He shall rather give heed whether it is rightly quoted. For where one will confirm anything with Scripture, that Scripture must properly serve the matter: otherwise the devil too quotes Scripture (Matt. 4), and likewise the fanatics — but falsely. But the best test is the Catechism: if the exposition agrees with the chief points of the Catechism, it is to be received; if not, it is to be shunned.¹⁹

## § 9. The power and effects of Scripture — a fivefold enumeration

**What power and working has the Scripture, or the Word?**

**First**, through the Word is dispensed forgiveness of sins (Isa. 40).

**Second**, it makes consciences quiet — this human doctrines do not do (Eph. 4; 1 Tim. 1).

**Third**, it drives away the devil; helps, strengthens, and comforts against the world, the flesh, and all evil thoughts. Whence also the first Psalm calls him blessed who day and night is occupied with God's Word.

**Fourth**, it brings all blessedness, both in this and in the coming life; and that so richly that whoever has it, in the deepest poverty and misery is glad through it, and would not give it for all the goods of the world.

**Fifth**, it engrafts us into Christ, so that no creature can separate us from him — neither devil, nor world, nor sects, nor the like.

In sum: the Holy Scripture is *profitable for doctrine, for reproof, for correction, for instruction in righteousness, that the man of God may be perfect, throughly furnished unto all good works* (2 Tim. 3).

## § 10. Why the Word does not everywhere bear fruit; against the Enthusiasts

**How comes it, then, that the Word does not everywhere bring forth fruit?**

It is not the Word's fault, nor the preacher's, but of the land or of the heart. For God's Word, where it is preached, has three sorts of lost hearers, and only the fourth part is truly good: that is, those who receive it with penitent heart and bring forth fruit in patience (Luke 8).

**May a Christian undertake anything for which he has no Word of God?**

No. For it stands written (1 Pet. 4): *If any man speaketh, let him speak as the oracles of God; if any man ministereth, let him minister as of the strength which God supplieth* — as if he would say: *All your words and works are to be grounded in God's Word; and where you are not certain of that, then let it lie, that you do no harm to yourselves and others.*

**Do the Enthusiasts (*Schwärmer*) do rightly, then, who reject God's Word or the preached Word?**

No. For Christ himself says (John 17) that we shall believe through the Word of the Apostles, or through the preached Word. That they do not believe everything is not the Word's fault, but the fault of the unbelieving heart.²⁰

## § 11. Against the Papist claim that Scripture is obscure; the two rules on the Fathers

**Is it right, then, that the Papists give out that the Scripture is obscure?**

No. For upon earth there is no clearer book written than the Holy Scripture: she is against all other books as the sun against every other light. And all *Patres*, Fathers, or scribes must from her explain, ground, and prove their writings and books. Whence the 119th Psalm compares the Scripture to a lantern; and in 2 Pet. 1 she is called a light that shines in a dark place, until the day dawns and the morning star arises in the hearts. Now where there be some dark passages, these should be cleared through other clearer passages.

**But how shall a Christian conduct himself when the sayings of the Fathers are cast at him against the Scripture?**

These two rules shall he hold. **First**, he shall ask whether the Fathers may not also have erred. Have they erred — as the opposite party must confess — then their sayings straightway avail nothing; they must have a higher proof, that is, a clearer passage from Scripture. For God's Word alone is the plumb-line by which all doctrines and thoughts are to be measured (Ps. 19; Gal. 6; Isa. 8).

**Second**, they shall say that the Fathers themselves would not have any man believe them where they do not bring clear Scripture. Augustine to Jerome: *"I do not judge that thou wouldest wish thy books to be held as though they were the books of the Prophets or Apostles; for I believe only the Holy Scripture — that it does not err. All the others I read in such wise that I do not believe it therefore true because they have said so, unless they prove it from Scripture."*²¹

## § 12. Scripture as the sole judge in religious controversies; the office of Councils

**When, then, disputes fall out in matters of religion — who shall be the judge?**

**God's Word alone** shall be the judge. For therefore is the Scripture called a **Canon** or plumb-line (Ps. 19; Gal. 6; Phil. 3). Whence also **Augustine**, *De peccatorum meritis et remissione*, book 1, chapter 22, says: *"Let us believe the Holy Scriptures, which cannot err nor lead others astray."*²²

**What should a Christian, then, hold of the Councils?**

Very much, and highly. But with this qualification: that they ground their decrees upon God's infallible Word. For to God alone it stands to lay down articles of faith, and to no man. And the Councils can do and shall do no more than pronounce that which is already grounded in God's Word beforehand, and show how those passages of Scripture are to be understood which the impure teachers have wickedly and falsely quoted. Whence Christ says (Luke 24): *Ye are the witnesses of all these things.*

**Is one, then, bound to believe the Councils even where they bring no Word of God?**

No. For Christ says (John 10): *My sheep hear my voice, and a stranger they do not follow.* And Paul (Gal. 1): *Though even an angel from heaven should preach another Gospel* — and so on.

**But if the Councils are no further to be believed than according to God's Word — when, then, shall an end come to the disputing?**

**Answer.** When the wrong side will not be shown by God's Word, but goes on stubbornly in the erring opinion it has fixed on — then God himself at last takes hold, and makes an end of the strife: as it happened from the beginning with Arius and other suchlike heretics.²³

## § 13. Against human traditions; the true interpretation of 2 Thessalonians 2

*[Continuing the passage on why human doctrines cannot bind consciences]* — since it comes from sinful men. For God's Word expressly says that one honours God vainly with such doctrine as is men's command (Matt. 15). To this that it also clearly teaches, that one should prove all things (1 Thess. 5) and hold fast only what is good — that is, what agrees with the Word of God. But the touchstone, as has been said, is God's Word. The Lord Christ also has not said, *"I am a men's doctrine, or a custom,"* but *"I am the truth."*

**But the Apostle says (2 Thess. 2): *Hold the traditions ye have been taught*?**

**Answer.** The Apostle, in the said words, does not speak of men's traditions apart from God's Word: but of the doctrine which the Apostle set forth to the Thessalonians in part orally in preaching, and in part wrote out to them in his first Epistle to the Thessalonians. For the Apostles have not preached one doctrine while present, and written another when absent, and commanded still another beside these to be held by the Church — so that one doctrine were to run counter to the other.

## § 14. The fourfold difference between pagan and Christian doctrine

**What difference is there, then, between the doctrine of the heathen and of the Christian Church?**

**Answer.**

**First**, the doctrine of the heathen is brought forth from human reason alone: the Scripture, however, is given by God himself.

**Second**, the doctrine of the heathen is only a small particle of Law and of virtues. Of the essence and gracious will of God she knows nothing certain. The doctrine of the Christian Church, however, is both of the Law and of the Gospel complete, and sufficient for the true knowledge of God.

**Third**, the doctrine of the heathen confirms idolatry, and invocation of those who are not gods but only creatures. The Scripture, however, teaches only the right knowledge of God, and how one is to invoke the living God, and no creature.

**Fourth**, the doctrine of the heathen is not a means through which God enlightens and converts the hearts of men, but rather an instrument through which Satan draws the hearts away from God. The Christian religion, however, or God's Word, is an *instrument* through which God converts, renews, enlightens, and sanctifies the hearts, unto eternal life. As Rom. 1 stands: *The Gospel is a power of God unto salvation to every one that believeth.*

**⁸** The reference is to **Johann Eck** (1486–1543) and the papal bull **Exsurge Domine** (1520). Eck was one of the two theologians (with Aleander) who conveyed the bull from Rome to Germany and worked its enforcement. Kirchner's phrase "in Dr. Eck's writing sent from Rome in 1520" refers to Eck's role as papal executor rather than to any single treatise, but likely alludes to Eck's *Enchiridion locorum communium adversus Lutherum et alios hostes ecclesiae* (Landshut, 1525) — a work Kirchner would have known well, since his own *Enchiridion* stands consciously as a Lutheran counter-genre to Eck's Roman handbook.

The Schmalkaldic League of Lutheran princes was defeated by Charles V at the Battle of Mühlberg (24 April 1547); Wittenberg was besieged, the Elector John Frederick captured, and the imperial **Augsburg Interim** of 1548 imposed on the Protestant territories. Kirchner treats this as the *first* attempted extermination of Lutheranism — from which God preserved the movement — and rhymes it with Nehemiah's building under Persian and heathen opposition.

**¹⁰** Kirchner's Nehemiah typology is drawn cumulatively from **Nehemiah 4 and 6** — the mockery of Sanballat, Tobiah, and their allies; Nehemiah's response of prayer and stationed watch; and the physical detail of the builders working with one hand on the trowel and the other on the sword (Neh. 4:17–18). The passage is a locus of Reformation self-understanding across confessional lines but takes distinctively Lutheran shape in 16th-century preaching.

**¹¹** Kirchner names two enemies as making common cause on the *Person of Christ*: the **Jesuits** (specifically Robert Bellarmine's early work was appearing in the 1580s; Kirchner may be responding to it) and the **Sacramentarians** (his standing 1583 label for the Reformed, whom Lutherans held to deny the real bodily presence in the Supper because they deny the ubiquity of Christ's human nature). The claim that Jesuits and Sacramentarians "blow altogether in one horn" on the *Person of Christ* is Kirchner's polemical thesis: both, in his view, deny the *communicatio idiomatum* in the strong Lutheran sense and thus arrive at the same denial of the real presence in the Supper by different routes.

**¹²** The passage on daily use of the *Enchiridion* alongside the sermon — a Christian carrying the small book with him, comparing article-by-article — is Kirchner's own catechetical pedagogy. The physical format of the 1583 imprint (small octavo, roughly 9.6 × 15.9 cm, cited in the BSB physical description) is a *portable Enchiridion* in the literal sense: designed to fit in a pocket and travel to church.

**¹³** The **Concord-Work and *Apologia*** are the *Book of Concord* itself (1580) and Kirchner-Chemnitz-Selnecker's 1583 *Apology of the Book of Concord*. Ludwig VI's role: as Elector Palatine he was one of the four Electoral signatories to the Book of Concord in 1580 (with Saxony, Brandenburg, and Kur-Sachsen; Palatinate's signing was significant precisely because his father had been the leading Reformed Elector), and he politically underwrote the 1583 *Apology*.

**¹⁴** The **University of Heidelberg**, founded 1386, was under Ludwig VI purged of Reformed faculty (Ursinus, Zanchi, Tremellius had already gone) and staffed with Lutheran theologians. Kirchner is here one of the Lutheran professors; on Ludwig's death (October 1583) most of the Lutheran faculty were dismissed within months by Johann Casimir. This dedication is thus published just as the University to which Kirchner refers was about to be re-Reformed for the balance of the century.

**¹⁵** The single most important line of the entire *Enchiridion* for the Scholastic Lutherans corpus: Kirchner's explicit public binding to the **Book of Concord** as the pattern of the confession of the saving faith. This is the sentence that qualifies the *Enchiridion* — and its author — for treatment as a systematic-catechetical footprint of the confessional documents themselves. The parallel formula in the 1583 *Apologia* is at its opening: *"solidum ac genuinum verae doctrinae sensum tenere, uti in Concordiae Christianae Libro repetitum est."*

## From Hauptstück I

**¹⁶** *"as if the smaller and the others were pressing against it on every side"* — the 1583 sentence "wider alle / obergleich als leine gewest / vnd die andern alle in angefochten" is broken in the OCR; the sense recovered is that the Christian faith stood as a smaller party against many contending faiths, from which context the translation gives *"the smaller … pressing against it on every side."* If a cleaner reading later becomes available, this passage will be revisited.

**¹⁷** The **Gospel-as-lap** image — *heistens nur die Schoßherhalten vnd nemmen* — is Luther's own. The believer is to *hold out the lap of his coat* (the *Schoß* of the medieval garment) and receive what is freely given. The image is a favourite of Luther's *Small Catechism* explanation of the Second Article and recurs in the *House Postil*. Kirchner adopts it here without attribution, as a common possession of his catechetical tradition.

**¹⁸** **Athanasius of Alexandria** (ca. 296–373), *Contra gentes* — usually cited as the opening of his early apologetic diptych with *De incarnatione*. The passage Kirchner extracts (*Sufficere Scripturas divinitus inspiratas, ad omnem instructionem veritatis*) reflects the substance of *Contra gentes* 1 (PG 25:4A–5B), where Athanasius argues that the inspired Scriptures are sufficient for the whole knowledge of truth against pagan philosophy. Kirchner's Latin form matches that of the Basel 1556 Latin *Athanasii Opera Omnia*, a standard 16th-century Lutheran reference edition.

**¹⁹** *"Das Symbolum ist Regel"* — the test-by-Catechism principle here is not a circular argument (test Scripture by Catechism, test Catechism by Scripture) but a practical rule: the Small Catechism and Apostles' Creed together give the doctrinal grammar within which allegedly novel scriptural expositions can be quickly judged. This is a 16th-century commonplace and appears in almost identical form in Chytraeus, *Catechesis*, Loc. I § 21 (see the companion Chytraeus volume).

**²⁰** *Schwärmer* — literally "swarmers." Luther's collective term for the radical spiritualist wing of the Reformation who claimed direct inner illumination of the Spirit apart from the external Word: Karlstadt, Müntzer, the Zwickau prophets, and by extension Schwenkfeld and the Anabaptist spiritualists. The Lutheran term of art carries a specific doctrinal charge — *Enthusiasm* in the technical Lutheran sense, meaning a claim to the Spirit apart from the *verbum externum*. Kirchner's 1583 usage is entirely standard; the *Formula of Concord*, Solid Declaration II, gives the confessional formulation. Rendered here as "Enthusiasts" with the German preserved on first occurrence.

**²¹** The Augustine-to-Jerome citation is from *Epistula* 82.3 (CSEL 34/2:354; PL 33:277), Augustine's classic letter to Jerome distinguishing the canonical books, which alone command belief without dispute, from all other authors — including Jerome himself — whom one may read while reserving judgement. The passage in Kirchner is paraphrased rather than word-for-word Latin, and the OCR yields the German rendering only; the paraphrase is faithful to Augustine's argument.

**²²** **Augustine**, *De peccatorum meritis et remissione et de baptismo parvulorum ad Marcellinum*, Book I, Chapter 22 (§ 30 in modern editions; CSEL 60:31; PL 44:127). The formula *"credamus sanctis scripturis, quae nec falli nec fallere possunt"* is one of Augustine's most-quoted anti-Pelagian assertions of scriptural inerrancy; Kirchner cites it in slightly compressed form.

**²³** **Arius** (ca. 250 – ca. 336) — the Alexandrian presbyter whose denial of the eternal deity of the Son was condemned at the First Council of Nicaea (325). Kirchner's parenthetical remark — that God ends stubborn heresy by his own hand when the disputants cannot be persuaded by Scripture — alludes to the tradition of Arius's grotesque death in a Constantinople latrine (reported by Socrates *Historia ecclesiastica* 1.38 and by Athanasius *Ad episcopos Aegypti*), which was a favourite Reformation exemplum of divine judgement on obdurate error. Kirchner treats Arianism as a paradigm of the case where doctrinal dispute admits no negotiated end.

# Glossary of Terms

*Kirchner-specific additions to the Scholastic Lutherans master glossary. Standard entries (justificatio, communicatio idiomatum, etc.) are in the shared* `terms.yaml`*. Only terms introduced or given specific coloration by the 1583 Enchiridion are listed here.*

|  |  |  |
|:---|:---|:---|
| **Enchiridion** | Handbook, little handbook | Preserve untranslated — the book's own title-genre. From Greek *ἐγχειρίδιον*, "in-the-hand," a portable handbook. Not equivalent to the modern "textbook"; carries the connotation of *portable* reference for the daily walk. Used by Kirchner throughout the Vorrede as the standing name for the present work. |
| **Sacramentierer** | Sacramentarians | Kirchner's standing 1583 term for the Reformed, on the grounds of their disagreement with Lutherans on the Lord's Supper. Not a term the Reformed used of themselves; a Lutheran polemical designation. Preserve the English "Sacramentarians" rather than modernising to "Reformed"; the term is the doctrinal claim, not a neutral confessional label. |
| **Schwärmer** | Enthusiasts (technical Lutheran sense) | Literal: "swarmers." Luther's collective term for the radical spiritualists — Karlstadt, Müntzer, the Zwickau prophets, later Schwenkfeld. The Lutheran technical charge is that they claim the Spirit apart from the *verbum externum* (external Word). Preserve as "Enthusiasts" in the English body, with the German on first occurrence per Hauptstück. |
| **Hauptstück** | Chief chapter, chief head | The standing organising unit of Lutheran catechesis, from Luther's *Small Catechism*. Kirchner's *Enchiridion* is organised by Hauptstück, not by *Locus* (Chytraeus) or *Articulus* (later scholastics). Render as "chief chapter" for the primary occurrence; abbreviate as "Hauptstück" thereafter within a given locus. |
| **Widmungsvorrede** | Dedicatory Preface | The 16th-century formal genre: a preface addressed to a named patron, functioning simultaneously as dedication, statement of purpose, patronage-request, and confessional statement. Kirchner's is unusually theologically substantive — the whole Nehemiah typology is worked out in it — and reads as a small tract in its own right. |
| **Bapsthumb** | Papacy | Kirchner's spelling of *Papstthum*, the papal system as a doctrinal-institutional whole. Rendered "the papacy" in the English body. |
| **Papisten** | Papists | Kirchner's designation for adherents of the papal system. Preserve the polemical form; the source uses it throughout. Not a term of theological neutrality but the standing 16th-century Lutheran usage. |
| **Concordi Buch** | Book of Concord | Kirchner's 1583 spelling of the *Konkordienbuch* (1580). Render as "Book of Concord." Cf. **Christlichen Concordi Wercks** = "Christian Concord-Work" (the whole enterprise of Concord — Formula, Book, and the 1583 *Apologia*). |
| **Symbolum** | Symbol (Creed) | The credal formulations: **Symbolum Apostolicum** = Apostles' Creed; **Symbolum Chalcedonense** = Chalcedonian Definition; **Symbolum Nicaenum** = Nicene Creed. Preserve untranslated in Kirchner's citation form. |
| **Kirchenbaw / Widerauffbawung** | Church-building / Rebuilding | The Nehemiah-typological cluster in the Vorrede: the Reformation as *rebuilding* of the Church after papal captivity. Render the metaphor consistently; do not paraphrase into "reformation" or "reform" — the *building* image is Kirchner's spine. |
| **Schwerdt des Geistes** | Sword of the Spirit | Ephesians 6:17 language, invoked in the Nehemiah typology: teachers and Christians armed with the Word against error. Render literally. |
| **Anfechtung** | Temptation, trial, spiritual assault | The Lutheran technical term for spiritual trial — not merely temptation to sin but the wider assault on faith and conscience by the devil, the world, the flesh, and God's own hiddenness. Preserve the German on first occurrence per Hauptstück; render "temptation" or "trial" in ordinary flow, "Anfechtung" where the technical weight matters. |
| **verbum externum** | External Word | The preached and written Word as the ordinary means of the Spirit, over against the Enthusiasts' claim to an unmediated inner Word. Preserve untranslated in doctrinal loci. |
| **Fürnembste Hauptstück** | Chief chapters | The title-formula: "the *chief* Hauptstück of Christian doctrine." Signals that the *Enchiridion* is not a complete dogmatics but a selective catechesis on the *disputed* points — those between Lutherans and Papists on the one side, Lutherans and Reformed on the other. |
| **Fromm** | Pious, upright, honest | 16th-century *fromm* carries the older sense of "upright, honest, of integrity" as much as the later narrowly-religious "pious." Kirchner uses it in the older wider sense: *frommen Herzen* = "upright hearts." |
| **Politia / Politia Christiana** | Civil order, Christian polity | The civil-magistracy sphere in Lutheran two-kingdoms teaching. Not yet a Kirchner-*Enchiridion*-locus but forecast in Tranche 3+ terminology. |
