# Translator's Note

## 1. The text base

The English rendered here follows the **1594 Leipzig edition** printed *in officina Voegeliana* (the shop of the Voegelin family), the last edition Chytraeus himself revised before his death in 1600.¹ The title page describes itself as *postremò nunc ab ipso Autore recognita, & multis in locis aucta* — "now at last revised by the Author himself, and augmented in many places." The base text used is the Internet Archive facsimile of the copy held under item identifier `Chytraeus_Catechism`, filename `Chytraeus_Catechism_1594.pdf`, cross-checked paragraph by paragraph against the OCR (`Chytraeus_Catechism_1594_djvu.txt`).

Two paratexts embedded in the 1594 printing are earlier in date than the edition itself:

- The address **PIO ET CANDIDO LECTORI** ("To the Pious and Candid Reader") is dated **Die Lucae. Anno [15]75** — 18 October 1575 — the year Aurifaber first began printing the Catechism from the manuscript.²
- The **EPISTOLA DEDICATORIA** to Bernhard von Menzingen is dated *postridie Ἐπιφανείας Filii Dei, inchoantium annum 1568* — 7 January 1568 — from the "Academia urbis Rosarum," i.e. the University of Rostock (*urbs rosarum*, the "city of roses," being Rostock's traditional epithet from its arms).³

Both are preserved unchanged in every subsequent edition and are here translated in their proper 1568/1575 voice. The body proper reflects the augmentations of the 1594 revision.

## 2. What the author himself supplies

Chytraeus's Catechism is composed on two typographic registers, and both are preserved:

- **Large-type Q&A** — the questions and answers proper, which children were expected to memorise. These are rendered here in normal body text.
- **Small-type commentary** — extended definitions, biblical proof-texts, scholastic amplifications, and cross-references, which Chytraeus added *"cætera vero, quæ statim mandari memoriæ non est necesse, exilioribus literis excudi à typographo petiuimus"* ("but the rest, which need not straightway be committed to memory, we asked the printer to render in the smaller letters"). These are rendered here in indented smaller passages.

All scripture citations, all patristic and classical allusions, the enumeration of Luther's three instruments (*oratio · meditatio · tentatio*) from the 1539 preface to Volume I of the German Wittenberg edition, the Aristotle *Ethica* reference, the *Pythagoras* aphorism, the Greek gnomic hexameters, the biographical dedication to the young Menzingen and to Chytraeus's own tenure "in hac Balthici littoris Sarepta" (Rostock, called *Sarepta* on Baltic shores) — all of these are Chytraeus's own and are preserved.

## 3. What the Scholastic Lutherans have added — and where

*Nothing in the body text is our own.* All editorial additions are relegated to the numbered endnotes at the end of this volume. These endnotes supply, and only supply:

- Standard modern citations for scripture (ESV numbering, with Vulgate reference given in parentheses on first occurrence in each section)
- Standard modern citations for Luther (WA / LW), Melanchthon (CR / MSA), and any patristic sources (PL / PG / GCS / SC / NPNF)
- Biographical dates on first named mention
- Modern place-names for German-humanist toponyms (Rostock for *urbs Rosarum*, Strasbourg for *Argentoratum*, Massilia for Marseille, etc.)
- Brief technical notes where the Latin carries a scholastic distinction the English cannot preserve

## 4. Editorial conventions

- **Register:** scholarly / academic-critical English. Reverent but not archaising.
- **Greek** is preserved in polytonic script, with parenthetical transliteration and English gloss on first substantive occurrence — e.g. **κατηχέω** (*katēcheō*, "I sound down into the ear," "instruct by voice").
- **Hebrew** — none appears in the Prolegomena.
- **Latin technical terms** are preserved in italic on first occurrence in each section, glossed, and rendered in English thereafter — *unless* the term is itself the subject of definition, in which case the Latin form is retained (thus *catechesis*, *theologia*, *religio* remain visible throughout the Prolegomena because they are what the Prolegomena is about).
- **Scripture:** ESV for the English rendering. Vulgate numbering, where Chytraeus follows it, is noted in parentheses on first citation per section; Chytraeus's own citation form is otherwise preserved.
- **Long-*s*** (ſ) is silently modernised to round *s*. Sixteenth-century *v/u* and *i/j* are normalised to standard modern Latin orthography in citation. The German ligature ß is preserved in original citations, resolved as "ss" in English body text.
- **Rubrication, typeface, layout** — locked via `shared/reference-custom.docx` and identical to the Praetorius trilogy.

## 5. What is not yet in this working translation

- **Locus I — X.** The Prolegomena translated here is the section preceding the Ten Loci proper. Locus I *De Vera Dei Agnitione* through Locus X *De Immortalitate Animae* are the substance of the Catechism and will follow in subsequent working sessions.
- **Marginal glosses.** The 1594 print carries running marginal notes in the OCR text that appear to be reader-added rather than Chytraeus's own. These are not yet transcribed; they will be catalogued when the full Locus set is translated.
- **The 1588 recension.** The Internet Archive item pairs the 1594 edition with an earlier 1588 recension. Textual collation between the two — to identify precisely which passages the 1594 title-page description *multis in locis aucta* refers to — is a task deferred to a future edition.

## 7. On AI assistance

Translation from the Latin was drafted with the assistance of a large language model, then verified paragraph-by-paragraph against the source facsimile. Every named source, every citation, and every scholastic term was cross-checked; no citation or attribution in the English body text is present that is not present in the Latin source. Where the source is corrupt in the OCR (chiefly in the small-type commentary passages, where 16th-century italic Antiqua occasionally defeats the scanner), the passage was read directly from the PDF facsimile and, where doubt remained, marked with an editorial endnote rather than silently patched. This is the working method already proven across the Praetorius *Syntagma musicum* trilogy (Volumes I–III, completed August 2026), and it is the method by which every one of the twenty-two works of the Scholastic Lutherans corpus will be produced.

# To the Pious and Candid Reader

*[Chytraeus's address to the reader, dated 18 October 1575, preserved unchanged in the 1594 revision]*

I daily beseech with fervent prayers the Son of God, our Teacher and Saviour, our Lord Jesus Christ, that He would, by His Holy Spirit, so instruct and govern all who teach and learn in the Schools, and me among them, that we may learn and teach things salutary to that tender age which is the seed-plot of Church and Commonwealth.⁴

Now while I was employed in the studies of this Sarepta on the shores of the Baltic⁵ I delivered to my auditors, among other subjects, the elements of the Christian *catechesis* — chiefly to be learnt out of the *Loci Theologici*.⁶ These, twenty years ago, without my knowledge or intent, were given over to be printed by my late teacher and colleague, D. Johann Aurifaber, and began to be published in typeset form.⁷ When I understood that they had since been used in many schools, worked over and augmented, I revised them once more this month with my colleagues brought in to consult.

And, lest their bulk should retard the learners, we asked the printer to set out in larger type only the few things which we judged useful to be laid before boys for memorising at the outset; but the rest, which need not straightway be committed to memory, we asked him to render in the smaller letters. And although I recognise that this little book is thin still, and puerile, and stands in need of manifold polishing — nevertheless I have decreed to alter nothing in it henceforth. I pray God to bless the studies of the pious youth, and to preserve for us the deposit of true doctrine pure and unspoilt among false teachers.

*"The evening now comes: abide, O Christ, with us; suffer not the light to be extinguished."*⁸

On the day of St Luke, in the year 1575.

# Dedicatory Epistle

*to the most illustrious young man, distinguished in the nobility of his lineage and in his virtue, Master Bernhard von Menzingen, son of the late Peter von Menzingen, knight of the golden spur and Maecenas, most deserving of his kin*

*by David Chytraeus*

———  ✦  ———

The purest and best part of the human race, and the chief seed-plot and flower of the Church of God, is that tender age which — holding fast the gift of the Holy Spirit received in Baptism — studiously learns the true doctrine of God and of our Redeemer Jesus Christ, and with chaste mind, simplicity, and pure faith invokes God; and with alert and glad heart gives thanks for the benefits received from Him; and, discharging in its studies and in the other departments of its life the offices commanded of it, reverently obeys God and its parents.⁹

To God, therefore, the studies of the earliest age are especially pleasing: invocation, obedience, and praises — however infirm and stammering the voice with which they celebrate God. To such an age Christ Himself grants glorious dignity: not only is it approved by Him, and dear and pleasing to God, but also its praises perfect His own glory; and grafted into Christ, it comes forth as a citizen of the heavenly kingdom.

For *of such is the kingdom of heaven*, saith Christ (Matt 19:14). And: *It is not the will of your Father in heaven that one of these little ones should perish*.¹⁰ And: *Except ye be converted, and become as little children, ye shall not enter into the kingdom of heaven*.¹¹ And again: *Out of the mouth of babes and sucklings hast thou perfected praise*.¹²

These ample and glorious encomia of tender age let all parents and teachers write up on their bedchambers and studies. Let them ponder what treasure has been committed to them in the teaching and governance of youth, and let them know that in this they merit well of the Church of God and render acceptable service to God — as often as, in their homes and schools, they faithfully instruct their infants and boys in Jesus Christ our Redeemer, and, together with the first arts and letters, drench their minds — indeed, together with the very milk of the nurse — with the sap of the true knowledge of God and of piety and of all the other virtues that are pleasing to God. For into the tender minds of boys must be instilled whatever we wish to take root and cling fast in all the rest of life.

Now the first and greatest care of all who are men created by God and redeemed by His Son must be this: rightly to acknowledge God the Creator and our Redeemer Jesus Christ, and with true fear and faith and with all the offices of piety commanded by Him to worship Him religiously. For although not all of us who are engaged in study can profess theology *ex professo* — since this life of men needs many other arts also — nevertheless all who wish to be called men understand that some time is to be granted to learning the true doctrine of God (which by His immense goodness God has delivered to the Church in fixed writings) as the norm of true knowledge and invocation of Himself and of all the counsels and actions of life.

For since the *cognitio et invocatio* of God — of the Son Himself, our Lord Jesus Christ — pertains to all men and to all ages, it is certain that not old men only, distinguished by prudence and erudition, but the tender and boyish age also must be accustomed to true piety and to the knowledge of God, and to the reading and study of the sacred books and of the doctrine delivered in them — particularly in Christian schools and families — as those most weighty divine oracles command the studies of the first age to have doctrine handed down by God: *From a child thou hast known the holy Scriptures, which are able to make thee wise unto salvation through faith which is in Christ Jesus* (2 Tim 3:15). Item: *These words which I command thee this day shall be in thine heart, and thou shalt whet them* — thus the Hebrew fount has it — *upon thy children*, that is, thou shalt propound them diligently and accurately polished and illustrated, and shalt continually rehearse them, and shalt inculcate them, and severely exact from thy children the diligence of learning (Deut 6:6–7). Well-known too is the precept of Paul: *Bring them up in the discipline and doctrine of the Lord* (Eph 6:4), which commands not only that the manners of the first age be governed honestly and diligently, and the wits be cultivated with letters and with the knowledge of the best arts, but also that the doctrine of God in our Lord Jesus Christ, delivered in the sacred books, be laid before them for reading and learning, and that it be assiduously inculcated and impressed. And Ps 78:5–7: *He commanded our fathers to make them known to their children: that the generation to come might know them, even the children which should be born; who should arise and declare them to their children: that they might set their hope in God, and not forget the works of God, but keep his commandments.* Ps 119:9: *Wherewithal shall a young man cleanse his way? by taking heed thereto according to thy word.*

These most severe divine mandates enjoin upon young men themselves the diligent study and keeping of the sacred letters and of the word of God; and they command parents, and especially teachers who succeed in the place of parents, to instil the seeds of the true doctrine of God into their children by teaching and discipline. These considerations impelled me also — while I was in this Sarepta on the shores of the Baltic (whither by a certain fate I was carried) — to lay before the studious youth, according to the reckoning of my office, some sixteen years ago the *summa* of the doctrine divinely delivered, distributed into a few *loci*, and illustrated with added definitions and divisions; and, as it were as a kind of prelude, to attempt to hand down the abridgement (*ἐπιτομή*) of the *Loci Theologici* of Philip [Melanchthon], which I then professed.

That method of doctrine concerning God, revised by me in the past year, and augmented with many definitions and other useful questions, I dedicate to you, dearest Bernhard: partly that I might show by some token — such as it is — a grateful memory of your most distinguished father, and of the ample and quite paternal benefits which I received from him, when he most kindly and liberally supplied the costs of my studies at several Academies; partly that I might congratulate you upon your entry into the studies of doctrine and virtue, with the great hope and expectation of us all. And although I know that these same things are being said to you daily by the most learned and best of your teachers, yet I also should be an exhorter from my station, that you would enlarge the eminent nobility of your family with the ornaments of eminent erudition and virtue, and would adjoin to the studies of the other arts and languages the true doctrine of God and of our Redeemer Jesus Christ.

For since the proximate and immediate ends of study are wisdom, or the cognition of things, and the faculty of speaking or writing rightly and perspicuously about the things proposed, — the first and highest degree of wisdom and virtue is the *agnitio Dei*, or true piety, as religious antiquity most weightily said:

**Πᾶσι θνητοῖσιν σοφίας ἀρετή τε τελεία,**  
**οἵτε γὰρ ἡ σοφία, καὶ ἀρετή, ψυχῆς κόσμος.**¹³

(*"For all mortals, wisdom and virtue are the perfection: for wisdom and virtue are the ornament of the soul."*)

And as men excel other living creatures by reason and by speech, so man surpasses man by these very endowments cultivated and polished by doctrine.

Especially therefore does it befit those born into noble and heroic families — whom God has willed to preside over some multitude of men, and to direct the counsels of public government — to be furnished and adorned above the rest with the doctrine of the liberal arts, with the knowledge of divine and human laws, and above all with the acknowledgement of God, with true righteousness, and with piety, that just as by rank and order, or by the splendour and wealth of their family, so also by wisdom, piety, justice, and doctrine, they may excel the rest of mankind. For the foundation of true nobility is not merely the dignity and wealth of ancestors bequeathed to their descendants, but virtue, wisdom, justice, fortitude, doctrine, and eminent eloquence — such as serve the glory of God, the fatherland, the Church, and the Commonwealth, and merit well of the rest of the human race. And although — since by divine appointment a distinction of orders has been made — it is consonant that eminent inclinations to virtue and more ardent and vehement motions of soul be propagated in heroic families, yet even these must be roused and governed and polished by doctrine and by the reading of the best writings and by an honest discipline and by honourable examples.

*Laws* and *Rules* of the counsels and actions of an honourable life — nearly the same among all peoples — you have set out in many other writings on the institution of life and of laudable manners, and in this little book of Catechesis too you have briefly expounded the *Definitiones* of all virtues and of the offices owed to God and to other men and to ourselves, distributed according to the precepts of the Decalogue, by which as by norms not only our deeds and words but also our counsels and thoughts must be directed.

The example of a pious and just man — of a prudent statesman, of a Hero adorned with the concourse of all the virtues pleasing to God, of an industrious and diligent *paterfamilias* — has been set before you in your most illustrious father. To this if you also add the honour of letters and of Latin eloquence, you shall be to your descendants the beginning of a new and ampler nobility, and an example of more illustrious virtue.

*Doctrine* — the nurse of wisdom and the rectifier of the other virtues — and *Discipline* — which by admonitions and by daily exercises, by examples, by rewards, by punishments, and by removing the occasions of delinquency accustoms tender souls to piety toward God and to honourable government of manners — I know have been to you most excellent and most laudable, since you have already been living for some years in the most celebrated Academy of Strasbourg,¹⁴ which — as Athens of old was called by the Greeks the mistress (*μάγιστρα*) of doctrine and virtue for all Greece, and in Roman times Massilia was the guardian of honest and severe discipline and the most praised nurse of good arts¹⁵ — is today in Germany the equal both of Massilia in severity of discipline and of manners, and of Athens in the light of all doctrines and especially in the sanity of speech, prudence, purity, and elegance. But both of these it surpasses by this illustrious and highest ornament: that it is the domicile and temple of the true God, and the faithful guardian and vindicator of the true and uncorrupt doctrine of the Son of God, our Lord and Redeemer Jesus Christ.

Fare well, well and happily.

From the Academy of the *urbs Rosarum*,¹⁶ on the day after the Epiphany of the Son of God, at the beginning of the year 1568 — which is the year 5530 from the beginning of the world.¹⁷ May the Son of God, given to us, grant that this year be happy and prosperous for you and for your most noble mother and for the whole Church and Commonwealth: this from the whole of my heart I pray.

# Chapter 1

## *Prolegomena — What the Catechism Is, and How and Why It Is to Be Learned*

———  ✦  ———

### § 1. What Catechesis is

***What is Catechesis?***

*Catechesis* is the *summa* of Christian doctrine comprised in a fixed method.¹⁸

———  ✦  ———

### § 2. What Theology, or Christian Doctrine, is

***What is Theology, or Christian Doctrine?***

*Theologia* is the doctrine concerning the essence and will of God, and concerning the benefits shown to the human race for Christ the Mediator's sake, and concerning the worships in turn owed to Him; a doctrine made manifest in the Word of God delivered through the Prophets and the Apostles, and confirmed by illustrious testimonies of miracles; through which God is truly efficacious, and imparts to those who believe the true knowledge of Himself, the remission of sins, and eternal life.¹⁹

———  ✦  ———

### § 3. The two chief heads of Christian doctrine

***What are the chief heads (*capita*) of Christian doctrine?***

Two.

*Lex Domini*, that is, the precepts of the *Decalogus*; and *Evangelium*, that is, the promise of the grace of the Lord and of eternal life, to be granted freely for Christ's sake.

> From these two fountains, as it were from the measuring-rod and rule of the whole *sacra scriptura* rightly to be understood, the remaining *loci* of theological doctrine are born and flow forth. To these two first heads of our children's Catechism — namely, the Decalogue and the Symbol of the Faith — all the dogmas and histories contained in the sacred Scripture can be referred. Yet, that the series and *summa* of the doctrine may be distributed for the pupil's ease into more members, and more easily be surveyed and impressed on the mind of adolescents, we shall, without superstition, set down **TEN LOCI** of Christian doctrine as the principal ones; to which all things that the studious read or hear in the Word of God, in the Fathers, and in any other Christian writings, sermons, disputations, or theological treatises, they may refer — arranging them, as it were, in fixed classes, or, as members of one body, aptly fitting them together and comprehending them in their proper order.²⁰

———  ✦  ———

### § 4. The ten principal loci of Christian doctrine

The **principal *loci* of Christian doctrine are ten**:

I. *De Deo* — Of God, or of the true knowledge and invocation of the one true God: Father, Son, and Holy Spirit.

II. *De Homine ad imaginem Dei creato* — Of Man created in the image of God.

III. *De Lege Dei* — Of the Law of God.

IV. *De Peccato* — Of Sin.

V. *De Evangelio* — Of the Gospel, which announces the remission of sins for Christ's sake; and Of the Son of God the Mediator.

VI. *De Nova Obedientia seu Bonis Operibus* — Of the New Obedience, or of Good Works.

VII. *De Poenitentia et Absolutione* — Of Repentance and Absolution.

VIII. *De Sacramentis, Baptismo, et Coena Domini* — Of the Sacraments, Baptism, and the Lord's Supper.

IX. *De Ecclesia et de Cruce ac consolationibus Ecclesiae* — Of the Church and of the Cross and of the Consolations of the Church.

X. *De Immortalitate Animae, Resurrectione Corporum, Extremo Iudicio, et Vita Aeterna* — Of the Immortality of the Soul, the Resurrection of Bodies, the Last Judgement, and Eternal Life.

———  ✦  ———

### § 5. What the Christian Religion is

***What is the Christian Religion?***

The **Christian Religion** is: to believe in Christ, and to worship God according to His Word.

*Religio Christiana* is the *praxis* of theology — that is: to acknowledge one true God, the Father, the Son, and the Holy Spirit, according to the doctrine made manifest by Him Himself; and truly to fear His wrath against sin; and to believe that for Christ the Mediator's sake sins are remitted to us and God is made propitious; and to seal this faith by the Sacraments instituted by Christ; and, this faith shining forth, to invoke Him and to worship Him by the good works which He has commanded in His Word to be worshipped by; and this on account of the honour of God and the salvation of the neighbour.²¹

———  ✦  ———

### § 6. The difference between the Christian Religion and the pagan

***What is the difference between the Christian Religion and the pagan?***

> The *religiones ethnicae* — the religions of the pagans — do not have a certain word of God on which they are founded, do not have Christ, do not have the true knowledge of the one God: instead they hold a plurality of gods and idols, and they undertake certain ceremonies and works of a sacred appearance, invented by human choice, by which they suppose they may expiate the sins of men and render the gods pleased and propitious, and, as it were, purchase good things from them.²²
>
> And although even Cicero, Plato, Seneca, and the like most wise of men, from the light of nature and from the works of the world, acknowledge that God exists — an eternal mind, the creator of things — and lay down many honest precepts concerning the offices of the virtues and concerning the external manners of men, yet these same men, since they are not governed by the Word of God, retain, against their own judgement, a monstrous multitude of idolatrous worships of gods, and equally call the Creator of the world, good angels, demons, devils, and dead heroes "gods," and enjoin that these idols, as by law and custom received in any given place, are to be worshipped — just as Pythagoras, in his brief summary of religion, prescribes.²³
>
> There is nothing to these men of the Son of God our Lord Jesus Christ, and of the gracious remission of sins and of eternal life freely to be given to believers, for the sake of the Son the Mediator — they are wholly ignorant.²⁴ There is no true religion, and no Church pleasing to God, where there is no true knowledge of Christ (John 14:6: *No man cometh unto the Father but by me*; John 3:36: *He that believeth not the Son shall not see life, but the wrath of God abideth on him*).²⁵
>
> Moreover, among the pagans the doctrine of the Law of God and of the virtues also is mutilated and maimed and depraved in many ways — as Cicero *De Officiis* and Aristotle in the *Ethics*,²⁶ instituting *ex professo* an explication of the virtues, make no mention at all of God, or of the primary virtues: studies toward God, fear of God, invocation of God, and giving of thanks, and the like — of which nevertheless some notice does shine forth in the minds of all men by nature, as the sentences even of Plato and Homer show. So too the second table of the Decalogue and the notice of the sixth precept, and the whole doctrine of the inner uncleanness of the heart which the Law argues, and of the perfect obedience of heart which the Law requires — among the pagans it is plainly obscured and corrupted, and almost wholly effaced.²⁷

———  ✦  ———

### § 7. The cause of certainty in Christian doctrine

***What is the cause of certainty in Christian doctrine?***

The chief and principal cause of certainty is the *authoritas et patefactio divina* — the divine authority and self-revelation — comprised in the books of the Prophets and the Apostles: that is, *DIXIT DOMINUS*, "the Lord hath said" — He who is truth itself and knows not to lie.

> Whoever, then, do not assent to the doctrine of these witnesses of divine truth, follow the spirit of falsehood — Satan the father of lies. It is therefore most certain that the doctrine concerning God delivered in the sacred writings is certain, true, divine, and the only doctrine that makes known to men the true will of God.²⁸

The subordinate *rationes certitudinis* are these:

> I. The *miracula divinae omnipotentiae* — as, for example, the raisings of dead men from the tomb — miracles which are proper only to God, and by which alone the Christian doctrine has been confirmed.
>
> II. The *universalis experientia omnium piorum* — the universal experience of all the pious, in the daily invocation and in the exercises of repentance.
>
> III. The *antiquissima et perpetua* — the most ancient and continuous — predictions concerning matters of the greatest moment: concerning the will of God, concerning the coming of Christ, concerning the sequence and changes of empires, all of which have so come to pass, and have been foretold in the sacred Scriptures.
>
> IV. The very *genus doctrinae* — making plain things hidden and unknown to human reason.
>
> V. The *miraculosa conservatio Ecclesiae*.²⁹
>
> VI. The *odium Diaboli* against this doctrine.
>
> VII. The *series doctorum et instauratorum* of the doctrine of the Gospel, from the very beginning of the Church onward.
>
> VIII. The *series annorum mundi continua*, which nowhere else is preserved.
>
> IX. The whole notice of the Law of God.
>
> X. The doctrine concerning marriage and chastity — which even in the *vita Christiana religione* remains unshaken.
>
> XI. The *persecutiones sanctorum et persecutorum poenae* — the persecutions of the saints and the punishments of persecutors.³⁰

Testimonies of Scripture: 1 Thess 2:13, *Ye received the word of God not as the word of men, but, as it is in truth, the word of God, which effectually worketh also in you that believe*. 2 Pet 1:21, *The prophecy came not by the will of man: but holy men of God spake as they were moved by the Holy Ghost*.

———  ✦  ———

### § 8. The best method of study in theology

***What is the best method of studying theology?***

There are three chief instruments (which **Luther**, in the preface to the first Tome of the German [Wittenberg Edition], from **Psalm 119**, recalls) by which the true and salutary knowledge of the Christian doctrine can be procured:³¹

**I. *Precatio* — burning and daily prayer, that God Himself may teach us, and govern and assist our studies by His Holy Spirit** — as David prays: *Deal with thy servant according to thy mercy, and teach me thy statutes* (Ps 119:124–125). *I am thy servant: give me understanding, that I may know thy testimonies* (Ps 119:125). *Lord, open thou mine eyes, that I may behold wondrous things out of thy Law* (Ps 119:18). *Teach me, O Lord, the way of thy statutes* (Ps 119:33). *Guide me in thy truth and teach me* (Ps 25:5). *For there are no studies happy and salutary, unless minds are governed by God.* — Irenaeus: *No man can learn about God, save with God for the teacher.*³²

**II. *Studium seu meditatio et diligentia in discendo*** — study, meditation, and diligence in learning.

**III. *Experientia seu praxis*** — experience or practice: which is the exercise of doctrine committed to memory, and of doctrine understood by teaching; the exercise thereof in daily invocation, and in the exercises of repentance; in the sorrows born of the sense of God's wrath and of punishments; in the studies and labours of our vocation — in seeking and awaiting the aid of God; likewise in bearing the holy Cross, and in manifold tentations, in which one is transferred to God — in which humility, hope, patience, and all other virtues, in whose cultivation the *religio Christiana* chiefly is, are exercised, grow, and are polished, and the true efficacy of the divine Word is known by experience.³³

Theological study, however — that is, the labours of learning — are to be governed by these rules:

> **1.** The first care must be to make the ***textus Bibliorum*** most familiar to us, from which alone, as from an august and pure fount, God wishes the doctrine concerning the true knowledge of Himself and our eternal salvation to be drawn. And in this one way and no other — through the reading and pondering of the text of the divine Word — God is efficacious and kindles in hearts the true knowledge of Himself, righteousness, and eternal life. Therefore every day at morning after they have risen, and at evening when they go to bed, let students read one chapter in the Bibles, and let them go through them in order, and let them retain in memory the chief sayings and histories distributed into the parts of the Catechism, or into the loci which contain the *summa* of Christian doctrine.³⁴
>
> **2.** The ***summa Doctrinae Christianae***, containing the principal articles set forth by a pious and erudite method, is to be embraced by the mind — into which all things which are read, heard, or learnt in theology are dexterously to be referred. This the studious learn most conveniently from the *Examen*, or from the *Loci Theologici*, by assiduous and attentive reading and re-reading.³⁵
>
> **3.** The knowledge of the ***linguae Ebraea et Graeca*** is necessary, that we may draw the true doctrine concerning God from the fountains, and that we may converse without an interpreter with the Son of God, the Prophets, and the Apostles.
>
> **4.** *Dialectica methodus* is necessary in learning and teaching the heavenly doctrine, and in refuting corruptions of the truth.
>
> **5.** The exercises of *disputationum et declamationum* must be undertaken, which confirm and sharpen the judgement about true opinions, and give birth to a proper and perspicuous facility of speech for the exposition of dogmas or oracles.
>
> **6.** The commentaries of *aliorum eruditorum et Patrum* — of other learned men and of the Fathers — are to be read, that by the studies and consensus of the Catholic Church we may confirm our faith.³⁶
>
> **7.** The *historia totius Ecclesiae* is to be known — especially of the ancient conflicts and judgements of the primitive Church, well acquainted with the Apostolic times — from the most ancient Fathers and writers of ecclesiastical history, and from the *acta conciliorum*, from the canons of the pontifical law, from the *Magister Sententiarum*, and so on.³⁷
>
> All *Philosophia*, the arts of speech, *Ethica* and *Politica*, *Arithmetica*, *Physica*, *Astronomia*, *Geographia*, and the knowledge of the other arts taught in schools, are of great use in explaining doctrine and the sacred books, and are moreover a singular ornament and safeguard of the Church.

———  ✦  ———

### § 9. For what causes it is necessary that adolescents learn letters and especially catechism

***For what causes is it necessary that adolescents pursue the studies of letters and especially of catechism?***

Men have been created for this: that they may know God the Creator and His Son our Redeemer Jesus Christ, invoke Him and worship Him, according to the doctrine made manifest by Him and set out in the writings of the Prophets and the Apostles.³⁸

Therefore, especially by the most severe *mandata Dei* let boys be moved to the pursuit of learning — the mandates by which God commands the doctrine delivered by Him to be heard, learnt, and propagated:

Matt 17:5: The eternal Father commands from heaven: *Hear ye the Son. HEAR YE HIM* (*HUNC AUDITE*).³⁹ It is therefore necessary that the sermons or the doctrine of Christ, described in the Evangelists, Prophets, and Apostles, be read, known, and perspicuously and clearly propounded to others for knowing.

1 Tim 4:13: *Attend to reading and doctrine*.

Col 3:16: *Let the word of Christ dwell in you richly in all wisdom*.

John 14:15,23: *Christ commands His word to be kept and preserved*, and He adds the most ample promise: that those who keep His doctrine, houses and temples of the whole Godhead shall be.

2 Tim 3:15: *From a child thou hast known the holy Scriptures, which are able to make thee wise unto salvation*. — And *all Scripture is given by inspiration of God, and is profitable for doctrine* (2 Tim 3:16).

The *finis* to which we have been created — and in learning and teaching to us proposed — is:

**First**, that we may rightly acknowledge God, and celebrate His glory (1 Cor 10:31; Matt 5:16; 1 Pet 4:11).

**Second**, that we may profit the Church of God, assisting the conservation of the Evangelical Ministry and the government of the Church.

**Third**, that we may serve the Commonwealth — which is the hospital of the Church of God — in counsels, studies, embassies, businesses, and all things that pertain to the common tranquillity and salvation. Titus 3:14: *And let ours learn to be forward in good works, for necessary uses, that they be not unfruitful*.

**Fourth**, that we may also have an honest place in the common life, food, and the necessaries for sustaining an honest life. *No labourer is worthy of his hire otherwise.*⁴⁰

But the *principal end* must always be before us: namely, the true acknowledgement and celebration of God, according to the doctrine made manifest by God Himself through Christ. Therefore the first care of all of us must be this: that we learn the *Catechesis* of the doctrine of Christ. Matt 6:33: **Seek ye first the Kingdom of God.**

———  ✦  ———

### § 10. The word *catechesis* — its meaning and etymology

***What is called Catechesis, and whence the appellation?***

*Catechesis* in general signifies a form of instruction, in which the beginnings of some doctrine are delivered by the living voice to the auditors, distributed methodically into fixed loci, and the auditors are compelled to give back and recite in turn what has been delivered to them and what they have learnt.⁴¹

The word comes from **κατηχέω** (*katēcheō*), which signifies to hear, to receive sound from another, and to require the same thing back by teaching.⁴²

***For what causes is Catechesis — that is, this form of instruction, which takes place through examination — used in the Church?***

### § 11. The uses of examination

***What are the uses of examinations in general?***

Examinations augment in the learners diligence, intention of mind, and the care of learning rightly and integrally the things proposed — when they know that the things they have heard are, at the examination, to be given back by them.

Then, in the Church it is necessary that every man render a *Confessio suae fidei* — a confession of his faith. Therefore in the primitive Church there were instituted *Examina*, in which those under catechesis, before they were received into the Church by Baptism, were accustomed to testify or confess that they embraced the true doctrine concerning Christ.⁴³ Hence the examination in the ceremonies of Baptism took its rise. It pertains also to the fidelity of pastors and teachers in the Church to know what the learners understand and profess.

> Furthermore, examinations contain the whole course of studies within fixed goals, that they may not wander and stray; augment the necessary and useful things to be learnt; take away the unnecessary and useless. They further extirpate errors previously conceived in the mind. They sharpen the wits, and form the judgement of the young. They cherish and augment the faculty of speaking, and the extemporaneous mode of teaching. They make each one able to give back what he has perceived, and to give ready answers to questions proposed to him. They transfer doctrine from an empty and airy persuasion to the concreteness of wisdom. They accustom to a certain communication of speech and humanity in the whole of life. They form pronunciation or action, in moderating the voice and hearing and in avoiding gestures unbecoming. Finally — since the doctrine of salvation is, and the counsels of good things and the good arts should be — in *doctores* and in *auditores*, as the poet's verse says:
>
> **Ὅσοι ἔμπροσθεν διδασκάλοις εἴπονται, ἀγαθῆς εἰκόνες οὐρανίας**⁴⁴
>
> *("As many as follow their teachers well, are images of a heavenly good")*
>
> — so in schools, where we shall hear our Teacher the Son of God, and shall converse with our teachers, and shall in turn learn many good things from our companions.

These uses I have recited, since I see that adolescents, either by shame or by pride, flee examinations; whereas I would rouse in some good minds a love of examinations — as much in the other arts as above all in the Christian doctrine.

# Glossary of Terms

*Terms preserved in Latin (or Greek) in the body of the translation, with their English gloss.*

———  ✦  ———

**agnitio Dei** — Acknowledgement / knowledge of God. Not mere information (*notitia*) about God, but the reverent recognition of God as God, which issues in worship. Standard technical distinction across Lutheran orthodoxy.

**analogia fidei** — Analogy of Faith. Not used explicitly in the Prolegomena but implicit in Chytraeus's insistence that *sacra scriptura* is rightly interpreted by referring all passages to the *summa doctrinae Christianae*.

**auditores** — Auditors, hearers. The catechumens as *hearers* of the catechetical instruction.

**authoritas divina** — Divine authority. In the Lutheran doctrine of Scripture, the formal cause of the certitude of Christian doctrine — *DIXIT DOMINUS*.

**capita** (of doctrine) — Heads, chief divisions. In catechetical usage, the *duo capita* are Law and Gospel; expanded pedagogically into the *decem loci*.

**catechesis** — The oral, methodical instruction in the elements of Christian doctrine, with examination. From Greek **κατηχέω** (*katēcheō*), "to sound into the ear," "to instruct by voice." Chytraeus's title-word; preserved untranslated throughout this volume as the subject of definition.

**cognitio et invocatio Dei** — Knowledge and invocation of God. The dual formula summarising the *practical* aim of theology.

**Decalogus** — The Decalogue, the Ten Commandments. Preserved in Latin as the standard catechetical designation.

**Dialectica** — Dialectic. In the Melanchthonian curriculum this is not sophistical disputation but the methodical art of definition, division, argument, and refutation — a preparatory discipline for theology.

**doctrina Christiana** — Christian Doctrine. Chytraeus treats this as coextensive with *theologia*.

**doctrina de Deo** — The doctrine concerning God.

**doctrina Evangelica** — The Evangelical (Gospel) doctrine. In Chytraeus's usage, always Christ-centred and Word-centred.

**Evangelium / Evangelion** — Gospel. In its material sense: the promise of grace and eternal life for Christ's sake, freely given.

**Examen** — Examination. Both (i) the act of examining a learner, and (ii) the specific reference to Chemnitz's *Examen Concilii Tridentini*.

**experientia (in Luther's three-instrument scheme)** — Experience or *praxis*. Not empirical or empiricist experience, but the experience of faith under *tentatio* — the "touchstone" of the Word's true efficacy.

**fides in Christum** — Faith in Christ. The *actus fidei* by which the sinner grasps the promise.

**finis (proximus / principalis)** — Proximate / principal end. Chytraeus distinguishes the proximate ends of study (wisdom, expressive ability) from the principal end (the *agnitio et celebratio Dei*).

**genus doctrinae** — Kind, or genre, of doctrine. In Chytraeus's argument at § 7.IV: the very *sort* of teaching that Scripture is — making manifest what is hidden to reason — is an argument for its divinity.

**illustria testimonia miraculorum** — Illustrious testimonies of miracles. In Chytraeus's definition of theology: one of the seals of the doctrine's divinity.

**Lex Dei** — The Law of God. In the *duo capita* division, the counterpart to the Gospel. Under both Tables of the Decalogue.

**Loci** (*locus, loci*) — Locus, loci. The systematic *places* or *headings* under which theology is disposed. Preserved in Latin, since the term is (i) technical, (ii) the subject of division in Chapter 1, and (iii) foundational to Melanchthonian and post-Melanchthonian Lutheran method.

**Loci Communes / Loci Theologici** — Melanchthon's *Loci Communes rerum theologicarum* (1521, definitive edition 1559). Chytraeus's stated model for the ordering of Christian doctrine.

**Loci Praecipui** — The *chief* loci, i.e. the ten Chytraeus enumerates.

**Magister Sententiarum** — The Master of the Sentences: Peter Lombard, whose *Sententiae* (c. 1150) were the standard textbook of Latin theology into the 16th century.

**mandata Dei** — The commandments of God.

**meditatio (in Luther's three-instrument scheme)** — Meditation. Sustained, prayerful attention to the text of Scripture.

**miracula divinae omnipotentiae** — Miracles of the divine omnipotence. Especially the *resuscitationes hominum mortuorum* (raisings of the dead), which Chytraeus argues are proper to God alone.

**oratio (in Luther's three-instrument scheme)** — Prayer. The first and indispensable instrument of theology.

**patefactio divina** — Divine self-revelation. Chytraeus's preferred term (over *revelatio*) for the divine act of making Himself known in the written Word.

**paterfamilias** — Head of the household, father of a family. In the Roman-humanist ethical vocabulary Chytraeus borrows, a title of civic and pious dignity.

**praxis theologiae** — The practice of theology. Chytraeus identifies *religio Christiana* with theology-in-practice — cognition issuing in worship.

**precatio ardens et quotidiana** — Burning and daily prayer. The first of Luther's three instruments, as Chytraeus formulates it.

**Prophetae et Apostoli** — The Prophets and the Apostles. The double authorship of the biblical canon, standard Lutheran formulation.

**rationes certitudinis** — Grounds of certainty. Chytraeus's eleven-point enumeration in § 7 of the subordinate arguments for the certitude of Christian doctrine.

**religio Christiana** — The Christian Religion. Not a genus of which paganism is another species, but the *only true worship of God*, defined by Christ, the Word, faith, and the Sacraments.

**religio ethnica / religiones ethnicae** — Pagan religion(s). The category of external-cultic religion opposed to the *religio Christiana*.

**Sacramenta a Christo instituta** — The Sacraments instituted by Christ. Lutheran orthodoxy admits two (Baptism, Lord's Supper); the whole catechesis will treat both in Locus VIII.

**sacra scriptura** — Sacred Scripture. Preserved in Latin on formal occurrence.

**Sarepta** — Type-name for Rostock in Chytraeus's usage. See note 5.

**summa doctrinae Christianae** — The *summa* of Christian doctrine. A technical Melanchthonian phrase — the systematic epitome of the whole. Distinct from the mere *summa* as compendium.

**Symbolum (fidei)** — The Symbol of the Faith, i.e. the Apostles' (or Nicene) Creed. Preserved in Latin — as the technical designation of the confessional formula.

**tentatio (in Luther's three-instrument scheme)** — Temptation, tribulation. The *touchstone* (Luther: *lapis Lydius*) which teaches the theologian by experience.

**textus Bibliorum** — The text of the Bibles. Chytraeus's preferred term for the primary source of theology.

**theologia** — Theology. In Chytraeus's opening definition, the doctrine concerning God's essence, will, benefits (through Christ), and the worships owed to Him — made manifest in Scripture and efficacious for the salvation of believers.

**urbs Rosarum** — The City of Roses. Rostock. See note 3.

**vera Dei agnitio** — The true knowledge of God. The material subject of Locus I.

**verbum Dei** — The Word of God.
