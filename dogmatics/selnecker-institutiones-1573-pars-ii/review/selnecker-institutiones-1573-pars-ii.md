# Translator's Note

*This working translation of the Second Part of Selnecker's* Institutio Christianae Religionis *is at its kick-off state. The paragraphs below record the intended text base, the editorial conventions binding on the eventual body prose, and — with full disclosure — what remains to be verified once the facsimile has been sighted paragraph by paragraph.*

## 1. The text base

The English rendered here follows the **1579** edition of Nikolaus Selnecker's ***Institutionis Christianae Religionis Pars Secunda*** — the second of three parts of Selnecker's mature systematic-dogmatic Loci-work. Pars I (Prolegomena · Doctrine of God · Creation · Angels · Anthropology · Sin · Law · Cross · Consolation) was published as a companion working volume by this team earlier in 2026. **Pars III** (probably *De Novissimis*, and closing controversies) is deferred to a future volume once its digital source is located and confirmed — the Google-Books identifier for the 1580 Jena Tröster Pars III has not yet been ascertained at the time of this kick-off.

The working text-base for the present volume is the digitised **Bayerische Staatsbibliothek München** copy (StaBibl München shelfmark **Dogm. 11-2**), preserved in Google-Books scan `36616453470017`. This copy is also served under Google-Books ID `xRE8AAAAcAAJ` (direct PDF download is captcha-gated at the Google-Books endpoint; the working PDF is the BSB copy already on disk from the Pars I sourcing session of 2026-08-31). The PDF is stored locally at `sources-original/selnecker-pars-ii-1579.pdf` (30.6 MB / 695 pages of the 8vo octavo imprint), with a `pdfplumber` layout-extraction cached at `sources-original/pars2-full.txt` (1,056 KB / 24,003 lines) for reference.

The bibliographic authority for the 1579 Selnecker Pars II is **VD16 S 5566** (Jena, Jakob Tröster) — the specialist Reformation-controversy bibliography **Controversia et Confessio** (Herzog August Bibliothek, Wolfenbüttel) indexes the work under entry `fa69041c-1638-4246-99e4-4be5c37a1710`.

## 2. What the author himself supplies

Selnecker's own hand supplies the entirety of the volume — the *proem* on the Gospel ***De Evangelio,*** the six announced Loci (Justification of Man before God, Penitence, Christian Liberty, the Power of the Church, the Doctrine of the Sacraments, and the Rationale of Human Life), the internal sub-Loci which the six unfold (notably *De Regno Christi,* *De Bonis Operibus,* *De Libero Arbitrio* under Penitence; *De Praedestinatione* after Christian Liberty; *De Oratione* after the Church; *De Baptismo* and the extensive *De Coena Domini* under the Sacraments; and *De Resurrectione carnis* closing the *Ratio Vitae Hominis*), and the closing meditation ***Verba Piorum ex hac Vita in Aeternam Vitam Decedentium*** — an extract from an oration of *M. Johann Rosa* which Selnecker prints *in pia beata memoria* as the volume's *ars moriendi* coda.

His treatments quote — often at length — from Scripture (with clear preference for Isaiah, the Psalms, the Pauline corpus, and the Johannine literature), from the church fathers (Augustine, Chrysostom, Cyril of Alexandria, Basil, Gregory of Nazianzus, Cyprian, Bernard, Tauler), from Luther and Melanchthon (his *praeceptor,* whom he quotes both explicitly and idiomatically throughout), and from the Lutheran confessional documents — with special emphasis on those Formula-of-Concord Articles which Selnecker himself had helped draft: **Article III** (Justification) throughout Locus I, **Article IV** (Good Works) in the Penitence-material, **Article V** (Law and Gospel), **Article VI** (Third Use of the Law), **Article VII** (Lord's Supper) at very great length in *De Coena Domini,* **Article VIII** (Person of Christ) in the same, and **Article XI** (Predestination) in the appended tract after Christian Liberty.

All of these citations are preserved unchanged in the translation. Modern-critical equivalents (WA/LW for Luther; CR/MSA for Melanchthon; BSLK/*Kolb-Wengert* for the confessional documents; PL/PG/CCSL/SC for the church fathers; ESV numbering for scripture alongside Selnecker's own citation form on first occurrence per Locus) are consigned to the endnotes.

## 3. What the Scholastic Lutherans have added — and where

*Nothing in the body text is our own.* All editorial additions are relegated to numbered endnotes at the end of the volume. These endnotes supply:

- Standard modern citations for the Reformation-era works Selnecker cites (WA/LW for Luther; CR/MSA for Melanchthon; BSLK/*Kolb-Wengert* for confessional documents — with special attention to the *Formula of Concord* Selnecker helped draft)
- Standard modern citations for the church fathers (PL/PG/CCSL/SC volume:column; NPNF English vol:page)
- Biographical dates on first named mention of contemporaries and predecessors
- Cross-references to the paradigm *Formula of Concord* Articles III (Justification), IV (Good Works), V (Law and Gospel), VI (Third Use of Law), VII (Lord's Supper), VIII (Person of Christ), X (Adiaphora), and XI (Predestination) — all of which interface directly with Selnecker's Loci in this volume
- Cross-references to the parallel Loci corpora of the same generation: Melanchthon's *Loci communes* (1521/1543/1555), Chemnitz's *Loci theologici* (1591 posth.), Gerhard's later *Loci theologici* (1610–1622), and — for the sacramental treatments — Chemnitz's *Fundamenta sanae doctrinae de vera et substantiali praesentia,* the paradigm-Lutheran anti-Sacramentarian handbook of the same generation
- Cross-references to the parallel Loci in *Institutio Pars Prima* (companion volume, 2026)

## 4. Editorial conventions

Locked from the Praetorius trilogy and continued through Chytraeus, Kirchner, Quenstedt, Große, and Selnecker Pars I. See [[reference-scholastic-lutherans-glossary]] for the master vocabulary list and register-norms.

Highlights:

- **Register:** scholarly / academic-critical English; reverent but not archaising. Preserve substance of Selnecker's Loci-analytic style — the numbered *distinctiones,* *quaestiones,* *definitiones,* *argumenta,* *fontes solutionum,* and *usus practicus* — as the paradigm mature-Melanchthonian *Loci-format* which Selnecker inherits from his *praeceptor*.
- **Technical Latin terms:** preserved on first occurrence in each Locus in italic (*justificatio, sanctificatio, unio mystica, communicatio idiomatum, causa meritoria, causa instrumentalis, satisfactio vicaria, praesentia realis, manducatio oralis, manducatio impiorum, praedestinatio, poenitentia, contritio, absolutio, libertas Christiana, adiaphora,* etc.), glossed briefly, used in English thereafter — unless the term is itself the subject of the Locus, in which case the Latin is retained throughout.
- **Greek** appears where Selnecker quotes NT/patristic sources; preserved in polytonic script with parenthetical transliteration and English gloss on first substantive occurrence.
- **Hebrew** appears where Selnecker quotes OT/rabbinic sources; preserved in pointed Hebrew script with transliteration and gloss on first occurrence.
- **Scripture:** ESV for the English; Selnecker's own citation form (Vulgate numbering) noted parenthetically on first citation per Locus.
- **Confessional documents:** Selnecker cites the pre-*Concord* confessional writings (AC, Ap, SC, LC, SA — 1530–1537) as ***confessio nostra*** and the *Formula of Concord* (1577) as ***Formula Concordiae*** or occasionally ***nostra Concordia.*** Modern references consigned to endnotes: BSLK / *Kolb-Wengert.*
- **Named authors first occurrence:** append life-dates in the endnote, not in body.
- **Rubrication, typeface, layout:** locked via `shared/reference-custom.docx` and identical to the completed Chytraeus, Kirchner, Quenstedt, Große, and Selnecker-Pars-I volumes.
- **Ornamental divider:** `— ❦ —` (aldus leaf), per the convention adopted from Chytraeus onward.

## 5. Scope disclosure

At kick-off the present volume covers ***Pars Secunda*** in the entirety of its 695-page 1579 Jena Tröster imprint (Google-Books scan of the BSB Dogm. 11-2 copy).

**A structural feature to be noted transparently** at the outset: the 1579 Pars II imprint carries ***no separate dedicatory epistle.*** After the printed title page (p 6 of the scan) and the BSB shelfmark stamp (p 7), the volume proceeds directly into the *Proem De Evangelio* (p 8), which serves as the *praefatio ad lectorem* and as the doctrinal bridge from Pars I (which had closed with *De Consolatione*) into the six announced *ordo salutis* Loci. Pars I 1579 does carry a dedication (to the Consuls and Senators of the Republic of Leipzig, dated May 1579); Selnecker evidently regarded that Pars I dedication as functioning also for the continuation-volume, and did not add a second. This absence is documented here rather than passed over in silence.

**Paragraph-by-paragraph audit at close** — per the discipline confirmed on Selnecker Pars I and on the earlier Kirchner *Enchiridion* ([[iiif-manifest-verify]]) — every tranche of this Pars II translation will be cross-checked against the OCR line-ranges at volume close via parallel Explore agents. Section-level word-count ratios miss paragraph-level gaps, and this discipline is what turned "Pars I complete" into "Pars I *truly* complete" on 2026-09-02.

# Nikolaus Selnecker — Biographical Sketch

*(Reused from the companion Pars I volume for the reader coming to Selnecker for the first time.)*

**Nikolaus Selnecker** (also spelled *Selneccer* or *Schellenecker*) was born on **5 December 1530 at Hersbruck,** a small Franconian town near Nürnberg. His father, a jurist, brought the family to Nürnberg while Selnecker was still a child, and it was there that Selnecker received his first musical education — a formation that would define one strand of his later life-work as a paradigm-Lutheran hymn-writer.

At **Wittenberg** from 1550 he studied under **Philipp Melanchthon** — whose *Loci communes* method Selnecker would later adopt and adapt in the present *Institutio* — and took his *Magister* in 1554. From 1557 he served as *Hofprediger* to Kurfürst August of Saxony at Dresden, an office he held until 1565 when the paradigm ***Crypto-Calvinist controversy*** at the Dresden court forced his departure. He became **Professor of Theology at Jena** in 1565, then at **Leipzig** from 1568 — with interruptions during the second phase of the Crypto-Calvinist controversy 1570–1574 (when the strict-Lutherans were briefly displaced) — and finally returned to Leipzig from 1574 as *Superintendent* and *Pfarrer* at St. Thomas, an office he held (with a single year at Braunschweig-Wolfenbüttel 1571) until his death.

Selnecker's **confessional-Lutheran significance** rests on his role as one of the **six co-authors of the *Formula of Concord*** (1577). The six — Jakob Andreä (Tübingen), Martin Chemnitz (Braunschweig), David Chytraeus (Rostock), Andreas Musculus (Frankfurt/Oder), Christoph Körner (Frankfurt/Oder), and Selnecker himself (Leipzig) — worked between 1576 and 1580 to bring the *inner-Lutheran* controversies of the second half of the sixteenth century to a paradigm confessional settlement: the *Solida Declaratio* of the *Formula of Concord.* Selnecker's specific contribution was the *Solida Declaratio* Article XI (**Predestination**), whose paradigm anti-double-predestination Lutheran formulation — *God has predestined to salvation but not to damnation; the reprobate are lost by their own contumacy* — became the paradigm confessional-Lutheran position against both the Reformed *decretum absolutum* on one side and the Semi-Pelagian *praevisa merita* framework on the other.

Selnecker's **hymnody** is his second lasting legacy. Author of the paradigm ***"Ach bleib bei uns, Herr Jesu Christ"*** (*EG* 347 — the paradigm-canonical *Ausgangs-Choral* still sung in confessional-Lutheran and Reformed hymnody worldwide) and of dozens more, Selnecker's paradigm ***Christliche Psalmen*** (1587) is one of the paradigm confessional-Lutheran hymn-collections of the whole *Concord*-generation.

Selnecker died at **Leipzig on 24 May 1592,** and was buried in the *Paulinerkirche.* His epitaph, composed by his son, closed with the paradigm confessional-Lutheran memorial-verse ***Christus mihi vita, mors lucrum*** (Phil. 1:21).

The present *Institutio Christianae Religionis* is Selnecker's mature systematic-dogmatic Loci-work, composed in the years immediately after the *Formula of Concord* (1577) and issued in three Parts at Jena by **Jakob Tröster** in 1578–1580. **Pars II** — the present volume — appeared in **1579** and covers the paradigm **Reformation-era *ordo salutis*** from Justification through the *Ratio Vitae Hominis,* interfacing at every point with the Loci Selnecker had helped draft in the *Formula*'s *Solida Declaratio.*

# Proem — De Evangelio · On the Gospel

*(Pars II opens without a separate proem-heading in the imprint. The signature-heading* DE EVANGELIO *appears alone at the head of the running text on p. 8. Selnecker himself signals its function in the second paragraph — "we shall set this as a Proem to the Second Class of doctrine" — that is, as an introductory Locus prefixed to the six announced Loci which the title page catalogues.)*

## §1. The Sion-Prophecy Meditation

**The signification of the word *Evangelium* is well known.** In its generic sense it signifies ***a good and joyous message***³ — that is, a discourse that announces good and joyous things. Among the Greek writers this idiom was familiar: *"To crown* Euangelia,*"* ***εὐαγγελικῶς***⁴ — that is, "on account of good news"; and in *Isocrates:* *"to sacrifice* Euangelia,*"* — that is, to render thanks to God with a public rite of sacrifice on account of joyous tidings. And further, the word ***Evangelium*** signifies the reward given for a happy report — whence *Cicero to Atticus:* *"O sweet Letters, for which I acknowledge an Evangelium is owed"* — that is, a reward for the good news. The Germans call it ***ein Botenbrot.***⁵ And in *Homer* also ***Euangelia*** signifies the gifts or rewards which were given to those who brought glad tidings: *Odyssey* ξ [Book 14]: **Δαγγέλιόν δὲ μοι ἔσω** — *"Let there be a reward to me for the good news brought."* Afterwards ***Evangelium,*** as it were the *peculium* of Christ's Church, signifies the sum of that blessed doctrine which was not devised by men but was proclaimed to us by Jesus Christ come down from heaven, as it was foretold in Isaiah 52 — *that chapter truly of gold,* which is to be learned by the students word for word, and to be diligently commended and expounded to the youth by pious teachers. We shall set this before, as a proem to this Second Class of doctrine, with a very brief exposition.

> *"Arise, arise, put on thy strength, O Sion, put on the garments of thy beauty, O Jerusalem, holy city."*⁶

**Sion is the Church of Christ, gathered out of Jews and Gentiles.** She is here commanded to render two things. **First,** that she strenuously arm herself against her enemies, and boldly muster against them. **Second,** that she adorn herself splendidly — as a bride is adorned with jewels for the sake of the Son of God, the Bridegroom of the Church (Isa. 61); and as He himself is arrayed in priestly ornament, so are we as the Bride, adorned with jewels (Ps. 45).

But the arms are described in Ephesians 6.⁷ For since the life of the pious is a continual struggle and unceasing warfare, the pious mind always has that with which to contend. As, therefore, we ought to be *courageous* against our enemies, so it is entirely necessary that we be humble, gentle, kind, and glad toward the Bridegroom Christ — according to that word: *"Kiss the Son"*; and again in Psalm 45: *"Incline thy ear; I am thy servant."* And this Paul also intends in 2 Corinthians 10: *"The weapons of our warfare are not carnal, but mighty before God"* — that is, divinely powerful — *"to the pulling down of strongholds, while we cast down reasonings and every high thing that exalteth itself against the knowledge of God"* — (thus far Paul has expressed what Isaiah wills should be first: that the pious be equipped with arms against their enemies, the Devil, sin, the world, the flesh, heresy, the abuse of Philosophy, and so on. Now he explains the other part) — *"and bring into captivity every thought unto the obedience of Christ"* — that is, we submit ourselves wholly, we and all our thoughts, to Christ and to His Word, freely and with a spontaneous spirit. For these are the ***garments of beauty:***

**1.** Human reason — logic, physics, and ethics — that is, the universal *subjection* and, as it were, the *laying-down* at the knees of Christ; or, as Paul puts it, ***τῇ παντὸς ἢ τῷ ὅλῳ νοήματος αἰχμαλωσίᾳ εἰς τὴν ὑπακοὴν τοῦ Χριστοῦ***⁸ — in each and every article of the faith, in the words of the Institution of the Lord's Supper, and in the invocation which is our miraculous life-support.

**2.** The spontaneous obedience and alacrity of the will to dedicate itself wholly to the Son of God, according to that word: *"Draw me after Thee."*

**3.** Invocation, patience, fidelity in one's calling, the hope of deliverance, the desire of eternal life and of converse with Christ, with the Angels and with the blessed — as *Monica,* the mother of Augustine, was wont to say: ***Evolemus, evolemus*** — *"Let us fly away, let us fly away."* And Paul: *"I desire to depart and to be with Christ."*⁹

> *"For no more shall the uncircumcised and the unclean enter into thee. Shake thyself from the dust, arise and sit down, O Jerusalem; loose the bonds from thy neck, O captive daughter of Sion."*¹⁰

**This is the reason** why for those who believe in the Son *the war is to be waged with one hand,* and *peace is to be restored with the other* — why quiet and gladness are to be repaired, cultivated, and exercised in Christ. For this is truly the *office* of the pious, of which it is wont to be said: *"With one hand she wages war; the other hand renders peace."* For, since Christians are ***Kings and Priests,*** they ought to render both offices — that they be not only adorned with arms but also armed with laws, and that with one hand they build the temple, with the other bear the sword against the enemies: even as the Jewish people, returned out of the Babylonian captivity, was wont to do in the building of the Jerusalem Temple.¹¹

The reason itself is twofold. **The first** is, that "the uncircumcised and the unclean shall no more enter" — that is, false teachers shall not any more seduce the people, but shall be prostrated, vanquished, and confounded by the Word of God, and shall bear no part of the government — according to Psalm 1: *"The impious shall not stand in the judgement,"* and Psalm 9: *"The impious is trapped in the works of his own hands,"* — that is, by *meditation* (namely of the Word), not by way or by sword. And Psalm 17: *"By the word of Thy lips I have observed the ways of the plunderers"* (*Pharizim*) — that is, of those corrupting the true doctrine.

**The other reason** is, that "the bonds have been broken" — that is, the yoke of the Law has been taken away, and *there is now no condemnation to those who are in Christ Jesus.* The Law has been abrogated, as far as concerns its malediction. *We are not under the Law, but under Grace;* and *the Law is not laid down for the just* — as we said more copiously above in the Locus *On the Law.*¹² The flaming sword (Gen. 3), the stone tablets (Exod. 34), the great fire (Deut. 18), the iron yoke (Deut. 28; Jer. 28), the yoke of burden, the rod of the shoulder, the sceptre of the exactor (Isa. 9), the shattering hammer (Jer. 23), the unbearable burden (Acts 15), the ministry of death (2 Cor. 3), — or, as this passage says, the ***bonds of the neck*** — have now ceased: that is, the *thundering of the Law* does not terrify those who believe in the Son.

> *"For thus saith the Lord: Ye are sold for nothing, and ye shall be redeemed without silver."*¹³

**Here is shown what doctrine ought to succeed the doctrine of the Law** — namely the *doctrine of the Gospel,* whose sum and perpetual voice is: *"Ye are sold for nothing, and ye shall be redeemed without silver."* That is: *unjustly were ye reduced into servitude by the fraud of the Devil, who tore you from Me and neither paid Me any price nor gave you any promise. It is just, therefore, in turn, that I recover My own thing, saith the Lord* — no price being paid to the robber.

For, as a stolen thing is not again to be bought back from the thief or the plunderer, but is restored to its owner *without price* by the righteous judge, — so God recovers the human race, paying nothing to the unjust robber, the Devil, and demanding no price from the human race, which can pay no price whatever. We are received, therefore, ***for nothing*** — not on account of our merits, but on account of the grace and faith of Christ. And this is the constant and perpetual confession of the ***Orthodox Churches:*** that *for nothing, without works of the Law, by faith alone in Christ, we are justified, reconciled to God, and saved.*¹⁴

And hence has been drawn the explication of the *Second Article of the Apostles' Symbol:* *"I believe that Jesus Christ, true God begotten of the Father before the ages, and likewise true man born of the Virgin Mary, is my Lord, who has redeemed me — a lost and damned man — and delivered me from all sins and from the power of Satan, not indeed with gold and silver, but with His own holy and precious blood."*¹⁵

And Isaiah 55: *"O all ye that thirst, come to the waters — even those who have no money — come, buy and eat; come, buy, I say, without money and without any price, wine and milk. For thus saith the Lord God: My people went down at the beginning into Egypt, that they might sojourn there; and the Assyrian without cause hath oppressed them. That is, I will free My people, as I did before deliver them out of Egypt and out of Babylon."*

## §2. The Sion-Prophecy Close — Isaiah 52:4–8

> *"And what have I now here, saith the Lord? My people is seduced for nothing, and its rulers make them to howl, saith the Lord — and daily and continually My name is blasphemed."*¹⁶

**These are the words that Christ speaks against the Pharisees** and against false teachers in the Churches and Schools. There are many, He says, who corrupt the true doctrine of the Gospel and blaspheme My name — they lord it over My people, seduce it, and make it howl. For these are the marks of false teachers.

**First,** they are ***blasphemers of the Son of God,*** and deprave the Articles of the Faith, and teach those things by which the person and glory of Christ is injured — as do the Arians, the Anti-Trinitarians, and the like.¹⁷

**Second,** they are ambitious, arrogant, proud, greedy of lording, despisers of others.

**Third,** they change the very type of the true doctrine, and seduce the simpler folk, and forge labyrinths and portents of opinions, and destroy the foundations.

**Fourth,** they beget *howlings* — that is, tortures of souls and pains of consciences, and often despair. And all these they do ***for nothing,*** without any just cause, out of mere ambition and pettish vainglory.

> *"Therefore My people shall know My name in that day. For it is I Myself who speak."*¹⁸

That is: he who will avoid the errors, must hear the voice of the Son of God, who is ***ὁ λόγος*** (*ho logos,* the Word), who from the beginning speaks with the Church and gathers her by His voice — according to John 8, **τὴν ἀρχὴν ὅτι καὶ λαλῶ ὑμῖν**¹⁹ (*"the very Beginning speaks to you"*); and again: *"This is life eternal, that they know Thee the true God, and Him whom Thou hast sent, Jesus Christ."* (John 17; cf. Rom. 10.)

> *"How beautiful upon the mountains are the feet of him that announceth good tidings, that proclaimeth peace, that announceth good things, that preacheth salvation, that saith to Sion: Thy God reigneth."*²⁰

**Christ gives gifts to men,** and sends the Apostles and Evangelists and doctors, as ***handsome and lovely runners and messengers***²¹ — that is, bringing the most widely-spread announcement or promise of grace and eternal life. For they announce the Gospel of the reconciliation of man with God, and *proclaim peace* — that is, they testify God is *propitious* and *appeased* toward us; and they *proclaim the good* — that is, they console, and teach that the Holy Spirit is efficacious through the Word; and they *preach eternal salvation* against the Devil and against the gates of hell, and they say to all who believe: ***Thy God reigneth*** — that is, thy God Himself is King, Christ redeems thee, justifies and saves thee, and sits at the right hand of the Father, and has *all power* in heaven and on earth, *Emmanuel,* God and man. **If God be for us, who can be against us?** And this is the Gospel and the *office* of the Gospel and the constant and perpetual voice and preaching of all who teach rightly: *Glory to God in the highest, peace on earth, and to men gladness. So God loved the world that He gave His only-begotten Son, that whosoever believeth in Him should not perish but have everlasting life.*²²

> *"The voice of Thy watchmen: they have lifted up their voice; together shall they exult, for eye shall see eye when the Lord shall have turned back Sion."*²³

**Sing forth praise, and jubilate together, O ye deserts of Jerusalem** (*such as we are today, truly deserts in these sad extinguishings of the world's light*), for the Lord hath comforted His people and hath redeemed Jerusalem. The Lord hath uncovered His holy arm in the eyes of all the Gentiles, and all the ends of the earth shall see the Salvation of our God.

The Gospel is propagated throughout the whole globe, according to that saying: *"Go ye into the whole world..."* Now a *parenesis* to the doctors of the Gospel is added, that with great care and solicitude they retain purity of doctrine and shun lapses against conscience:

> *"Depart, depart, go ye out from thence"* — that is, forsake Babylon, that is, the idolatrous doctrine and the false dogmas — *"touch not the unclean thing; go out from the midst of her; be ye clean who bear the vessels of the Lord. Not in flight shall ye go out, nor by escape, but the Lord shall go before you, and the God of Israel shall gather you."* — That is, ye shall not fear your enemies, because the Lord is with you.²⁴

## §3. The Hebrew Etymology — *Bāśar, Biśśar, Besōrāh*

**Now let us return to the definition of the Gospel.** The Hebrews call *flesh* **בָּשָׂר** (*bāśar*),²⁵ and likewise the *man* — who, of course, has flesh. Hence **בִּשַּׂר** (*biśśar*), *"he announced joyous things to all flesh"* — or *"carnal, soft, and pleasant things he preached"* — that is, ***he evangelised,*** as in the preceding chapter: *"How beautiful are the feet of those who evangelise"*; and **בְּשׂוֹרָה** (*besōrāh*), which is the same as **εὐαγγέλιον** (*evangelion*)²⁶ — which is *soft, placid, and gentle,* and is to be borne to all flesh, according to that saying: *"Preach the Gospel to every creature"* — or, as Paul interprets it in Colossians 1, **ἐν πάσῃ τῇ κτίσει τῇ ὑπὸ τὸν οὐρανόν** (*"in every creature under heaven"*) — wherever under the heavens there are men, in the whole world, as far as it extends.

## §4. Definition of the Gospel

**Let this be the definition of the Gospel:**

> ***The Gospel is a doctrine not implanted in nature at Creation, but brought forth from the sacred bosom of the eternal Father — previously unknown to all creatures — in which God, out of His immense mercy, promises to all men terrified by the sense of God's wrath against their sins, on account of Christ His Son, freely the remission of sins, the imputation of righteousness, the donation of the Holy Spirit, and the inheritance of eternal life; upon this condition alone, that they certainly believe these promised benefits are given to them on account of Christ.***²⁷

With this definition set forth, let us consider the *causes* — efficient, impulsive, ***προκαταρκτική*** (*prokatarktikē,* triggering-external), instrumental, and final — and then let us see whether the Gospel is named only that which is comprehended in the writings of the New Testament.²⁸

## §5. The Five Causes of the Gospel

**The principal efficient cause** is the ***Son of God*** Himself, who, for the human race fallen into sin and death, made intercession before the eternal Father, and not only prayed on our behalf, but offered Himself for punishment and satisfaction for the human race, and together with the Father and the Holy Spirit made the wondrous decree concerning the redemption of the human race, and, as ***λόγος*** and *Chancellor* — ***ἑρμηνεὺς τῶν μεγάλων βουλημάτων τοῦ Πατρός***²⁹ (*"interpreter of the great counsels of the Father"*) — announced it to the first parents. Hither pertain the sayings: Proverbs 8: *"My delights were with the sons of men";* John 1: *"The Son, who is in the bosom of the Father, He hath declared";* Isaiah 52: *"I Myself am He who speaks";* John 8: **τὴν ἀρχὴν ὅτι καὶ λαλῶ ὑμῖν**.

**The interior impulsive cause** — ***αἰτία προηγουμένη*** (*aitia proēgoumenē*)³⁰ — is the immense, inexpressible, and gratuitous ***love and mercy of God*** toward the wretched human race, and the ***intercession of the Son*** for us, according to that word: *"So God loved the world, that He gave His only-begotten Son";* and, *"Who shall separate us from the love of God?"* And truly Tauler has that splendid saying: *"So does God love us — even as though His own essence and salvation consisted in us and in our salvation."*³¹ And Nazianzen: **οὐδενὶ τοιοῦτον χαίρει θεός, ὅσον ἀνθρώπου διορθώσει καὶ σωτηρίᾳ**³² (*"in nothing does God so rejoice as in the correction and salvation of man"*).

**The προκαταρκτική cause** (the ***external triggering cause and object,*** by which God's will is moved to unveil the Gospel) is ***sin and the most tragic damnation of the human race*** — which He was altogether unwilling should perish. Sirach 18: *"As a small drop is to the immense waters of the sea, and as an exiguous grain to the sands on the shore, so are the years of a man to eternity. Therefore does God tolerate them and pour out His mercy upon them: He sees and well knows all are subject to death; therefore His goodness is the more copious upon them."*

**The instrumental causes and organs** are all the ***Ministers of the Gospel,*** who in all ages have propagated the voice of the Gospel: **first,** the Son of God Himself in Paradise; **then** the first parents, the Patriarchs, the Prophets, the Evangelists, and the Apostles, among whom the *"little Benjamin"* dominates — that is, Paul (Ps. 68). ***In our age:*** Luther, Melanchthon (*Philippus*), Bugenhagen (*Pomeranus*), Jonas, Cruciger, Forster, Linck, Venatorius, Vitus Theodorus (Dietrich), Brenz (*Brentius*), Schnepf, Hess, Moibanus, Aepinus, and the other Leaders and Pastors of the flock of the Son of God.³³

**The material — that concerning which, or the object** — is ***God*** and ***man the sinner, to be reconciled to God through and on account of Christ the Mediator*** — whom indeed it was fitting to be both God and man, that He might reconcile and conjoin God and man, as Cyril rightly says: *"He is the Mediator between God and men, for in Him God and man are conjoined."*³⁴

**The ends or effects** of the Gospel are all the ***benefits of Christ*** — the ***privative*** (as the term has survived in the schools):³⁵ namely, remission of sins, or liberation from sin, from the wrath of God, from the tyranny of the Devil, from the malediction of the Law, and from eternal death; and the ***positive:*** the donation of the Holy Spirit, the imputation of the righteousness of the Son of God, new obedience, the consolation and governance of the Holy Spirit, the inheritance of eternal life, and victory over the Devil. From these effects the definition of the Gospel in Romans 1 is to be taken: *"The Gospel is the power of God unto salvation to every one believing"* — that is, it is an ***organ of God, truly powerful and efficacious for saving,*** as it is also called *the arm of God* (Isa. 53).

## §6. That the Gospel is Old and New — Continuity of the Promise

**These causes considered, let us now know that the Gospel, properly so-called, was also in the preceding ages** — indeed, from the very Fall was announced by the Son of God Himself in Paradise, and afterward by the Patriarchs and the Prophets, as Paul testifies in Romans 1, saying: *"Paul, set apart for the Gospel of God, which He had before promised through His prophets in the Holy Scriptures."* And the Son of God is Himself, in the Scriptures — or *in the volume,* as the Psalm has it — described concerning Himself, and testifies that everything concerning His incarnation, passion, death, resurrection, and benefits was foretold. And the promises are well known: *"The Seed of the woman shall crush the head of the Serpent"; "In thy Seed shall all the families of the earth be blessed."* And David and Isaiah most evidently preach concerning Christ (Ps. 22; Isa. 53). ***One and the same Gospel*** always was — of God's mercy, of the benefits of the Messiah, and of the free remission of sins. And the Evangelists of the New Testament, besides the doctrine of the Gospel, have composed also the ***History of the exhibited Messiah,*** and have described His nativity, miracles, life, doctrine, and passion — in whom all the Prophets looked to one. For God willed the Church to be *certain* that Christ, according to the promise, has been exhibited, and that He has conferred on us His benefits, on account of which we are freed from sin and given eternal life. Therefore John says: *"These things are written that ye may believe that Jesus is the Christ, the Son of God, and that believing ye may have life in His name."*

## §7. On the Two Principal Parts of Christian Doctrine — Law and Gospel · The Necessity of the Law · The Antinomian Error

**Often is it said in the Church that there are two principal parts of the whole Christian doctrine,** to which all Loci and all Sermons can be referred: namely, ***the Law*** or the Decalogue, and ***the doctrine of the Gospel*** — according to the saying, *"To the Law and to the testimony"* (Isa. 8). Likewise: *"Christ gave two denarii to the innkeeper"* (Luke 10).³⁶

**Concerning the Law** we said something above — *what it is; how manifold; whether any man can satisfy it; how and in what respect it has been abrogated; and how it is fulfilled.* Concerning the question and the arguments of the Pelagians, *whether a man can fulfil the Law of God in this life,* we shall speak, God helping us, in the Locus ***De Bonis Operibus.***³⁷

**That in the true Church the Law is diligently and assiduously to be taught, is shown not only by the causes of the moral Law** — namely, the ***efficient,*** which is God Himself; the ***material in which,*** the mind of God and the human minds; the ***material about which,*** the whole rational nature — mind, will, heart, and all interior and exterior powers of the whole man; the ***form,*** which is the eternal and immoveable wisdom and justice in God as norm, and the obligation of all rational creatures either to obedience or to eternal punishments; and finally, the ***end,*** which is that the rational creature congrue with the will of God, and know, worship, and celebrate God rightly; and (to add this also) the ***effects and utilities*** of the moral Law, concerning which we have sometimes written the following little verses:

> *Lex ait esse Deum: qualis Deus ille sit, inquit.*
> *Lex norma est vitae, studiorum, & regula morum.*
> *Lex crimen monstrat, divinam monstrat & iram.*
> *Lex trahit ad Christum, contritis corde medentem.*
> *Iustitiae pingit radiantia lumina prima.*
> *Iudicii promit restantia iura futuri.*
> *Lex fons est legum in vita, fons omnis honesti.*³⁸

> *The Law affirms that God is; and says of what sort that God is.*
> *The Law is the norm of life, of studies, and the rule of morals.*
> *The Law shows the crime, and shows the divine wrath.*
> *The Law draws to Christ, the Physician of the contrite in heart.*
> *It paints the first radiant lights of righteousness.*
> *It brings forth the remaining rights of the coming judgement.*
> *The Law is the fountain of laws in life, the fountain of all that is honourable.*

Not only, therefore, do these causes and utilities show that the Law is altogether to be taught in the Church, and both to be prefixed to the Gospel and to be conjoined with it — but we also have an ***immoveable command*** to teach the Law: *"Preach and do penitence"; "We establish the Law through faith"; "I have not come to abolish the Law";* Luke 13: *"Unless ye do penitence, ye shall all likewise perish."* And it is most true, that if the Law be taken away, it is *ignored what Christ is and what He has done, since for us He has fulfilled the Law.* For if I wish to understand the *fulfilment* of the Law — that is, Christ — it is necessary to know *what the Law is, and what its fulfilment is.*

Against this true and immoveable judgement on the teaching of the Law in the Church, the ***Antinomians*** have resisted, and have wished the *preaching of the Law* removed from the Church, using these arguments — whose brief refutation we shall subjoin.³⁹

### §8. Refutation of the Antinomian Arguments (I–XVII)

**Argument I.** *"It behoves that penitence be preached in the name of Christ" (Luke 24). Therefore the Law is not needed.* — **We answer to the antecedent.** The *name* signifies mandate and place, recognition and invocation. *"In My name,"* says Christ — that is, by *My mandate and in My place,* likewise *by true acknowledgement, confession, and invocation of Me.* Now *penitence* signifies the *whole conversion,* whose beginning is in the Law (as the word *contrition* shows). It is also rightly said: the *matter* of penitence is from the Law, but the *form* of saving penitence is altogether and without any contradiction *from the Gospel.* For the Law is preached that it may show sin and be the *pedagogue unto Christ* — since each precept in the Decalogue is as it were a prophecy of Christ *fulfilling the whole Law.* And yet it is true that the Law has been abrogated universally, both spiritually as to the conscience — as regards condemnation — and also corporeally (to use Luther's word), that is, in case the obedience is *hypocritical and Pharisaic* and lacks faith. For the doctrine of the Law is so to be taught, that we may know that the works which we perform avail nothing *unless we do them in Christ,* or in the Kingdom of Christ, and that they avail on account of Christ, and are therefore rightly named *works of charity, works of grace, works of the Holy Spirit, fruits of righteousness and of faith,* rather than simply *works of the Law* — although they be done according to the Law, but not save from faith. And since true penitence is a burning *thirst for the mercy of God in the affliction of conscience,* it is now easily understood how and why Christ commands penitence to be preached *in His name* — because that thirst cannot be quenched, nor can a peaceable conscience be made, save by acknowledgement of the Son and by true faith. And from these things it in no wise follows that the preaching of the Law is not needed — which is nevertheless the *pedagogue unto Christ,* as was said above in the First Part on the triple use of the Law.⁴⁰

**Argument II.** *"Preach the Gospel to every creature" (Mark 16). Therefore the Law is not to be preached.* — **We answer.** Christ, commanding the Gospel to be preached everywhere on earth, does not exclude — much less prohibit — the preaching of the Law; but because He wills *all men to be saved* and to be freed from sin (the recognition of which is through the Law), He wills the Gospel to be preached everywhere, that it may be known *how* the human race is freed from sin, from the wrath of God, and from the malediction of the Law. Other answers I omit, and retain the proper signification of the *Gospel* — although in a broader sense the word *Gospel* can conveniently be taken for the whole doctrine and ministry.

**Argument III.** *"The Holy Spirit shall convict the world of sin" (John 16). The Holy Spirit is efficacious through the ministries of the Gospel. Therefore sins are to be reproved by the voice of the Gospel, not of the Law.* — **We answer to the major:** Christ speaks of a certain *special sin,* which is *Unbelief* — consisting principally in this, that men do not believe that *sins are remitted to them freely without works of the Law,* and that they are *received into grace and justified and saved on account of Christ.* This sin — namely — the Holy Spirit reproves *in the Gospel and through the ministry of the Gospel of the Law.* For the Law in particular commands nothing concerning the *justifying faith in the Son of God* and the *free remission of sins,* since it has no free promises but only conditional ones; wherefore also in particular (though generically it may be otherwise) it does not reprove *that unbelief in the Son of God* which offends against the free promises — that is, it neither accuses nor damns any man for not believing that his sins are remitted to him freely without works of the Law. The *voice of the Gospel alone,* by consequence and antithesis, has this *roaring* (as Joel the prophet speaks): namely, *"He who has not believed is already judged, because he has not believed in the name of the Son."* And this is the true meaning of the saying in John 16: *"The Holy Spirit shall convict the world of sin"* — for the reason is added: *"because they believe not in Me."*

**Yet this too is to be considered:** even if the word *sin* be taken generically for **ἀνομία** (*anomia,* lawlessness) universally, still the Holy Spirit reproves sins ***through the preaching of the Law*** — which, though it does not bring remission of sins and eternal life, and is *the letter that kills,* is nevertheless retained and urged in the New Testament, both publicly and privately, that sin may be shown, the wrath of God revealed, the conscience terrified, and the sinner led to Christ. Truly, therefore, it has been said: *"Whatever shows sin — that is,* ἀνομίαν *— likewise wrath and death — that is exercised by the ministry of the Law."* And no exception is here admitted. And even if the *Law generically* accuses unbelief in God the Father, Son, and Holy Spirit, it does not *in particular* say: *"Thou art condemned because thou believest not that Jesus Emmanuel — true God and man — is thy one Saviour, Justifier, Mediator, and Redeemer, who has freed thee from sin, death, hell, and from My own curse, and who for thy sake has been conceived, born a man, has suffered and died for thy sins, and has been raised again for thy justification."* Likewise, the Law does not say: *"Thou art condemned because thou believest not that thy sins have been transferred to Christ, and that the righteousness of Christ is imputed to thee and is thine, and that on account of Christ all sins are remitted to thee freely."*

**And to the minor also we must briefly answer.** The Holy Spirit is efficacious through the Gospel — that is, as regards vivification and the certainty of salvation and of eternal life. But He is also efficacious through the preaching of the Law — as regards the recognition of sin and the mortification of the old man.⁴¹

**Argument IV.** *"The Law is not laid down for the just"* (1 Tim. 1). *Therefore in the Church, which is a just people, the Law is not needed.* — **We answer.** Paul's saying in 1 Timothy 1 speaks of *the thundering and malediction* of the Law. The Law does not press him, does not accuse him, does not condemn him, who believes in the Son and is *just by faith;* nor is the Law laid down against him who spontaneously composes his morals according to its norm — which those alone do who are imbued with true faith, doctrine, and the Holy Spirit. For those are not just who *hear the Law,* but those who *believe in Christ,* whom the Law promised — they *do the Law.* Then also this is to be known: the Church is just in this life *by the imputation of the righteousness of Christ,* and has the *first-fruits of the Spirit,* and has begun new obedience; yet, since in all the saints there remain many sins and carnal infirmities, there is need of *continual preaching of the Law,* because God wills sins and lapses to be acknowledged, and His wrath against sins; and He wills that there be a *certain rule of life and norm of obedience,* which, when rendered by faith according to the Law, pleases God on account of the Son the Mediator.

*A similar argument is this:* ***"Ye are not under the Law but under Grace"*** (Rom. 6). *Therefore the Law is not to be taught.* — **We answer.** Paul does not exclude the *preaching* of the Law, but shows that we are not obnoxious to its malediction, since Christ has been made a curse for us; and that a great prerogative is now given to us, and the faculty of *mortifying sin,* since the Holy Spirit renews us and helps us, and fortifies us by His power, that we may put to flight the *remnants of sin.* Therefore an *antithesis between the condition of the Law and the condition of Grace* is set forth by Paul: the Law commands, but does not help; Grace both helps and obtains the victory. This diversity of conditions, since it is always to be considered, therefore also the Law is always in the Church to be preached.

*Such also is this:* ***"We have been freed from the Law"*** (Rom. 7). *Therefore the Law is not to be preached.* — **We answer.** Paul uses the word ***κατηργήθημεν*** (*katērgēthēmen,* "we have been reduced to inefficacy"), which he opposes to ***τῷ ἐνεργεῖσθαι*** (*tō energeisthai,* "being made efficacious"). *"The Law is the strength of sin"* (1 Cor. 15). Now sin, excited by the prohibition of the Law — that is, condemned, the man being irritated — flourishes or *is made efficacious* (**ἐνεργεῖται**) in our members to bear fruit unto death. But now this power of the Law and this tyranny of sin ***καταργεῖται*** (*katargeitai*) — is rendered inert and languid — the Holy Spirit having been given to us, who works spontaneous and joyful obedience in us, not by the power of the Law (which makes sin exceedingly sinful — not indeed by its own vice but by ours — but by its own goodness and virtue), according to Galatians 3: *"He that ministereth to you the Spirit and worketh miracles in you — He doth it not from works of the Law, but from the preaching of faith."* The Law, therefore, is *unnerved,* that it may not be able to augment and enflame sin in us, or irritate us, or offend us, or drive us into affections of sin, and much less into despair — because we have been *gifted with the Holy Spirit,* who makes us fit for willing and performing what God commands through the Law. Furthermore, the Law also ***καταργεῖται*** as regards *condemnation:* ***ἀργός*** (*argos,* idle) and *otiose* the Law is, and we — *if I may so say* — *abolished* and, in the cross of Christ, *co-suspended* (to use the words of D. Philip [Melanchthon]), that the *power and malediction of the Law* may no longer dominate in us.⁴²

**From these things it does not follow that the Law is not to be preached** (whose use is necessary, as we said above); but this follows: that the Law by its own strength cannot render us *spontaneous* or *fit* for willing and doing what God commands in the Law; likewise, that those who believe in the Son of God are not damned by the *malediction* of the Law. And that these things may be more clearly illustrated, it is necessary that the Law be preached and diligently distinguished from the Gospel.

**Argument V.** *"All the Prophets and the Law prophesied until John" (Matt. 11). Therefore the Law is not any longer to be preached.* — **We answer to the antecedent.** Christ Himself explains the sense of His saying, adding the word ***προεφήτευσαν*** (*proephēteusan,* "they prophesied") — that is, *they foretold concerning those things which now, present, are clearly and openly discerned.* It is also truly said concerning the *ceremonial and judicial laws, the types, and the Mosaic polity.* For now, from the days of John the Baptist, the new kingdom ***βιάζεται*** (*biazetai,* "is taken by force")⁴³ — that is, *the spiritual Kingdom of Christ is constituted and defended by divine force and power,* resisting the ***βιασταί*** (*biastai,* the violent) — that is, *the devils and tyrants,* who ***ἁρπάζειν*** (*harpazein,* "to snatch away") that Kingdom endeavour, to use an exposition weighty and pleasant, and *analogous to the faith,* by no means to be reproved by that other one drawn from *the highest zeal of the faithful.*

**And there is also this excellent and full-of-consolation point concerning the moral Law:** The Law *shows, accuses, and condemns sin.* But when John begins: *"Behold the Lamb of God, who taketh away the sins of the world"* — then immediately the Law *ceases.* This enarration is understood in practice.

**Argument VI.** *"In the death of the Son of God the wrath of God is known. Therefore there is no need of the preaching of the Law."* — **We answer to the antecedent.** No one can know the causes of the death of the Son of God unless he considers the Law, which shows the *atrocity of sin,* the *magnitude of divine wrath,* the *severity of divine justice,* and the *misery of the human race.* For these causes, which the Law sets forth, show *why the Son of God suffered and died for us,* and therefore they testify that the *preaching of the Law is altogether necessary,* that we may rightly be admonished concerning those causes.

**Argument VII.** *"The Law does nothing toward justification. Therefore it is not to be taught."* — **We answer.** I concede the whole as regards justification. For we are justified *by faith alone,* which, unless it be *without any even the least works* — that is, *without confidence in works* — does not justify, indeed is not faith. But, as it is rightly said, *"opposites placed side by side shine forth the more,"* — so, although the works of the Law justify no one (since no one satisfies the Law), yet the doctrine of the Law is on that very account to be sounded, that the whole world may become *guilty before God,* and may *despair of its own works and merits,* and flee to Christ, *the Lord of the Law,* who for us and on our behalf has fulfilled the Law *actively and passively,* by *fiducial* application to Himself of the obedience and satisfaction of the Law rendered by Christ.

**Argument VIII.** *"Christ is the end of the Law" (Rom. 10). Therefore the Law is not any longer to be taught.* — **We answer to Paul's saying.** *"End"* does not signify *destruction* but ***scope.*** For ***τέλος*** (*telos*) in Greek means *end, terminus, scope.* Christ, therefore, is the *scope of the Law,* because in Him alone, freely by faith, we attain the *end of the Law* — namely, righteousness and salvation — since He alone fulfilled the Law for us, being made to us *righteousness, sanctification, wisdom, redemption.* And on this account the Law is to be urged, that the *magnitude of the benefits of Christ* — which is the perfection of the Law — may be rightly by us understood and celebrated.

**Argument IX.** *"Everything that is not of faith, is sin" (Rom. 14); likewise, "By faith alone do we please God, and without faith it is impossible to please God" (Heb. 11). Therefore the Law is not needed.* — **We answer to the antecedent.** I concede the whole concerning the Law *devoid of faith* before God. And for a pious and necessary Augustinian *imitation* we ought to hold as a rule the saying of Paul: *"Whatever is not of faith is sin."* Against this we ought to admit nothing. Wherefore also *works of the Law, contrition* (as far as this must become a work pleasing to God and a sacrifice), *and whole penitence,* although sometimes they are excited by the Law and done according to the Law, are still *of no moment,* have no use — indeed are ***plagues and detriments,*** and bear fruit unto death (as in Cain, Saul, Judas, and in the Pharisees and hypocrites and *labourers* and *self-justifiers* the examples show) — unless there accede ***Faith*** as the *source and director* of all works, contrition, penitence, and obedience. But he who believes in the Son is *governed by the Holy Spirit,* and spontaneously wills and does the things which please God and are contained in the Law, and truly determines that his infirmities are remitted to him on account of the Son.

**Argument X.** *"The consciences of the pious are to be made certain. The Law does not make consciences certain. Therefore the Law is not to be taught."* — **We answer to the major.** They are to be made certain — namely, both concerning *sin, God's wrath, and death,* and concerning *the free remission.* — **To the minor.** I deny the minor. For the Law makes consciences certain concerning *sin, the wrath of God, and death,* that consciences may learn *to flee to the Mediator, the Son of God.*

**Argument XI.** *"To take away the effect it is necessary to take away the cause. The Law is the cause of sin, because the Law works wrath" (Rom. 4). Therefore the Law is to be taken away.* — **We answer to the minor.** The Law is not the ***efficient cause of sin*** — that is, it does not augment or effect the *material* of sin, but *shows* sin and the wrath of God and eternal punishments; and these being shown, in consciences there arise *terrors and rumblings* which have their *principal and proximate cause in corrupt nature,* not in the Law.

*Such also is this argument:* ***"Whatever brings despair and security is not to be taught. The Law brings despair and security. Therefore the Law is not to be taught."*** — The minor is not true, understanding the Law *by itself.* By *accident,* however — as regards *us,* that is, our corrupt nature — it cannot have any other effect in those who are ignorant of the doctrine of the Gospel.

**Argument XII.** *"The Law was given to a certain people, the Jews. We are not Jews. Therefore the Law does not pertain to us."* — **To the major.** The Law — namely, the ***ceremonial and judicial,*** not the *moral* Law. For this is the *eternal and immoveable judgement of God,* naturally and divinely infused into the minds of all men, showing the distinction of things honourable and shameful. And even if the written Law was again promulgated on stone tablets and renewed to the Jewish people, still the same has been declared to all peoples and remains inscribed on their hearts — which Paul evidently shows in Romans 1 and 2.

**Argument XIII.** *"Perfect love casts fear outside," says John. The Law augments fear. Therefore the Law is not to be taught.* — **To the major.** It casts out fear — namely, ***servile*** fear. **To the minor.** I concede, speaking of the *sole and naked Law* without the recognition of the evangelical doctrine, which effects ***filial*** fear.

**Argument XIV.** *"It is necessary that the righteousness of the Law be damned. The Law does not damn its own righteousness. Therefore there is another doctrine which damns the righteousness of the Law."* — **To the major.** The major is true *of the justification of men before God,* as Paul shows to the Romans and Galatians. For we are justified freely by faith without works of the Law, since corrupt nature cannot satisfy the Law. But the minor does not speak of external and carnal satisfaction — which, so far as the Law is understood alone, since the Law is spiritual, *damns itself* (as Paul speaks), since it does not will *mutilated obedience* to be rendered to itself, but speaks of *the integral righteousness of the Law,* which will be in the future life.

**Argument XV.** *"Impossibilities are not to be taught. The works of the Law are impossibilities. Therefore the Law is not to be taught."* — **To the minor.** They are impossibilities — namely, ***in corrupt nature.*** They would have been possible had nature remained *integra.* And yet now also *the voice of the Gospel* shows *in what measure the works of the Law are possible,* and when they can be done so that they please God as though rendered integrally by us.

**Argument XVI.** *"Christ fulfilled the Law. Therefore the Law is not to be fulfilled by us."* — **To the antecedent.** Christ *fulfils* the Law, but does not *dissolve* the Law. For He Himself says: *"I have not come to dissolve the Law, but to fulfil"* — that is, *not to abolish, abrogate, or destroy the Law,* so that obedience be no longer rendered to it. For this is ***νόμον ἀπαγορεύειν*** in the Greek phrase familiar to Herodotus and Thucydides, ***πατρίους νόμους μὴ λύειν*** ("not to loose the paternal laws"). But Christ fulfils the Law in ***four modes,*** as we above have shown:

**I.** By His own proper obedience, which alone is perfect. For He, in His person, is *conformed to the norm of the divine Law,* and perfectly is such, and renders *integral obedience,* which the Law demands.

**II.** By deriving into Himself the penalty on our behalf, and paying a sufficient ***λύτρον*** (*lytron,* ransom-price) — on account of which righteousness is given and imputed to us, as though we had satisfied the Law.

**III.** By restoring in us the Law, righteousness, and eternal life. For He *pours out into the minds of believers His Holy Spirit,* who begins in this life *obedience and conformity to the Law of God,* about to consummate the same in eternal life.

**IV.** By sanctioning and confirming the doctrine of the Law, and by illustrating the *true and genuine sense* of the Law, the Pharisaic *opinions about satisfaction of the Law through external discipline* being uncovered and refuted.⁴⁴

He who observes these four modes can most easily extricate himself from the *horrid error of the Antinomians.*

**Argument XVII.** *"Christ preaches penitence from the Gospel. Therefore the Law is not to be taught."* — **We answer to the antecedent.** As regards the *mode of integral conversion,* it is true that it must be sought from the Gospel and there taught by Christ. But as regards the *part of the conversion* and its *beginning,* the Law is assuredly to be applied. For the *acknowledgement of sin* is through the Law.

### §9. The Six Differences between Law and Gospel

**These arguments most briefly resolved,** we now add the distinction between Law and Gospel. There are ***six differences,*** taken from the form of *notice,* from the *matter,* from the *form of promises,* from the *commiations,* from the *effects,* and from the *objects.*⁴⁵

**I. From the form of *notice.*** — The Law of God is *known by nature,* as Paul says in Romans 2: *"The Gentiles by nature do the things of the Law."* For all men naturally understand that God exists, that obedience is owed to God, that the unjust are punished by God, that honour must be rendered to parents, that no one is to be injured without just cause, that righteousness, truth, beneficence, and the other virtues are to be cultivated. Even if before the Fall a more illustrious notice of the Law shone in human minds *without any darkness and without any errors,* still after the Fall also *some spark* of the Law shines in the minds — which is to be aroused by doctrine and by precepts. And from this natural notice, all *moral Philosophy,* all the honourable laws (Attic and Roman), and all the sententiae on the honourable regulation of morals in *Cicero, Seneca,* and the *Gnomological Poets* have been built up. But let the students learn the solution of a usual argument. Paul says: *"I would not have known concupiscence to be sin, unless the Law had said, 'Thou shalt not covet.'"* Therefore the Law is not naturally known to man. — **We answer.** A *particle* of the Law is known to man, not the *integral* Law. For the *interior uncleanness and evil concupiscence* man does not acknowledge as a sin by which God is offended, and the *fountain and cause of sin* he entirely ignores.

But the ***Gospel — that is, the promise of the Son of God the Mediator*** — is *by no means known by nature,* but has been *revealed by the Son of God above and outside the view of human reason* (John 1). Therefore nowhere in *Plato, Seneca, Cicero,* or the like does any word extant appear concerning the Gospel, or concerning the Son of God our Lord Jesus Christ *suffered and crucified for us,* or concerning the free remission of sins, or concerning the resurrection of bodies, or concerning eternal life freely to be given on account of Christ the Mediator.

Let the solution of a usual argument be held:

> *The power of God is known to the Gentiles.*
> *Christ or the Gospel is the power of God.*
> *Therefore the Gospel is known to the Gentiles.*

*The major* is understood of the *power of creation and conservation of things.* *The minor,* however, speaks of the *power of salvation* — as was shown above in the definition of the Gospel.

**II. From the *matter.*** — The Law is only *doctrine commanding and mandating* — of what sort we ought to be, what we ought to do, and what we ought to omit — and *accusing and damning* all who are not perfectly conformed to the Law.

But the ***Gospel*** is *primarily and principally the promise of the remission of sins and of eternal life,* to be given on account of Christ. And although *renewal of nature is necessary,* nevertheless — since we are far distant from perfection — the Gospel *pronounces us to be perfect in Christ,* namely, by the *imputation of the perfection of Christ* and the *inchoation of new obedience,* but not yet *by integral consummation.*

**III. And most illustriously, from the *form of promises.*** — The promises of the Law are ***conditional,*** and at the same time demand ***perfect obedience,*** as it were the cause on account of which the promised goods are exhibited — as it is said: *"He that shall do these things, shall live in them"; "Do this and thou shalt live."*

But the promise of the ***Gospel is gratuitous*** — that is, freely does it give the *remission of sins and eternal life* to believers, not on account of the *dignity of works,* but on account of the *Son of God the Mediator alone.* Romans 3: *"We are justified freely by His grace through the redemption which is in Christ Jesus."* Although the Gospel *requires faith* — which seems to have the appearance of a *condition* — nevertheless we find a *twofold distinction.* For the Law offers *blessing with the added condition of works;* the Gospel, however, *exhibits and gives righteousness without any work, merit, or quality of ours,* and requires *faith not as our work but only as the instrument and means by which the offered grace is to be received.* If, therefore, *condition* is to be spoken, the *condition of an instrument* is to be named — where *no merit, no dignity, no efficacy of a quality* can be boasted. Furthermore, the Law demands the doing, and commands both these and other things to be done, but *offers no faculty for doing,* and therefore *entirely deserts those lying in death.* But the ***Gospel — through the Spirit of Christ, whom it has annexed with itself*** — *also gives us the faith that it requires,* that in Christ apprehended by faith we may be *justified and sanctified* and *obtain eternal life.* And for this cause Paul says that we are *not under the Law* — that is, under that condition by which the Law makes us guilty by commanding only and not helping — but that we are *under Grace,* that is, we *have the vivifying Gospel* and are *gifted with the Holy Spirit,* that we may put to flight the *remnants of sin* now unarmed, as above against the Antinomians was said.

**IV. From the form of *commiations.*** — The Law threatens *malediction and eternal punishments* to all sins, as it is written: *"Cursed is every one that abideth not in all things that are written in the Book of the Law."*

But the ***Gospel offers pardon to all sins,*** and *receives and absolves all sinners fleeing by faith to Christ.* John 3: *"He who believeth in the Son hath eternal life; he who believeth not in the Son is already condemned."*

**V. From the *effects.*** — The Law *works wrath,* frees no one from sin and death, justifies and saves no one, but *accuses and damns all men on account of sin.* Romans 3: *"From the works of the Law no flesh shall be justified before Him; for through the Law is the knowledge of sin."*

But the ***Gospel is the power of God unto salvation to every one that believeth.*** For *the righteousness of God is revealed in it, from faith to faith,* as it is written: *"The just shall live by faith"* (Rom. 1).

**VI. From the *objects.*** — The Law *is principally laid down against the unjust* (1 Tim. 1) — that is, against *Epicureans, wicked men, proud men, and hypocrites* who from *external discipline* proudly exalt themselves. The Law of God accuses and condemns their *security and arrogance.*

But the ***Gospel is to be set forth not to Epicureans and hypocrites but to those doing penitence and terrified by the sense of God's wrath against sin and by the fear of eternal damnation,*** as in Matthew 11 the Son of God says: *"Come unto Me, all ye that labour and are heavy laden."*

**From these things which I have said,** a *perspicuous and illustrious distinction* between the Law (or the precepts of the Decalogue) and the Gospel (or the promise of grace on account of Christ the Mediator) can be taken. These distinctions — and especially the first, the third, and the fifth — are most diligently to be considered, that with *accurate and conspicuous limits* (as in the judgement *of the regulation of boundaries*)⁴⁶ these two genera of doctrine, *Law and Gospel,* may be *discerned.* Whose *confusion* has, with *most tragic darkness,* wrapped the whole doctrine of the Church. But *accurate distinction* is most necessary — to the *true knowledge of God and of the benefits of Christ,* to the *true invocation of God,* to *faith,* to the *true worship of God,* to the *true consolation of souls,* and to *discerning the true Church of God from the Gentiles.* For many — even the *wise and learned* — proudly deride the Gospel, and think there is contained in it nothing beyond the *precepts of the Law on the honourable regulation of morals,* which have been much more elegantly and splendidly transmitted to letters by *Plato, Cicero, Seneca,* and the like.

Diligently, therefore, let the students — in the whole *zeal of theological doctrine,* in *daily invocation,* and in the other *exercises of piety* — consider these differences of Law and Gospel which we have briefly recited.

### §10. On the Distinction between the Old and the New Testament

**We must now also add an admonition concerning the distinction between the Old and the New Testament.** In *Moses and the Prophets* there are two principal heads: the ***doctrine of the promises,*** and the ***legal precepts about morals, judgements, and ceremonies.***

**The Old Testament,** therefore, is named that *covenant* by which God transmitted to the posterity of Abraham the ***voice of the moral Law,*** repeated with *illustrious testimonies,* and commended, and *instituted a certain form of polity,* and adjoined the laws proper to that polity with this *obligation and promise:* that in that posterity there should always be the seat of the Church, until the promise of the *Blessed Seed of the woman* should be exhibited and fulfilled — which God willed to be preserved in that Jewish congregation, and thence to be propagated to the Gentiles.

**The New Testament,** however, is named that ***Pact*** made with the *whole human race* in the promise of *sending the Son of God,* that He might be *Redeemer and Saviour* — that is, that He might *merit remission of sins, righteousness, and eternal life,* and that He might *give these goods to all fleeing to Him.* This Pact was ***inchoate*** in the preaching of the Gospel *inaugurated from the beginning* in the Church restored in Paradise after the Fall, and *shall be completed in the whole Church* when she, raised from death by the Son of God, shall be led to the eternal Father and shall put on eternal glory.

**Now the Old Testament is called *"old"* both on account of *time* and on account of the *subject.*** *First,* the *promulgation of the Law and the institution of the polity temporally preceded the inauguration of the reparation of nature — inchoate at the resurrection of Christ, and universally to be completed when the whole Church, raised again, shall be adorned with eternal beatitude and glory.* Then, when Christ was exhibited, the polity *as it were grew old and ceased.* Afterwards, the *old nature is pressed by the judgement of the Law;* and as long as the *oldness of the flesh remains,* this *burden of the Law* pressing the old nature in all men also remains. Finally, this *old nature at last grows utterly obsolete and is wholly abolished.*

**Similarly the New Testament is so called by reason of *time* and of *subject.*** *First,* under the *end of the prior Pact* (which was to be abolished) *there was made the reparation new and eternal of righteousness and life, Christ rising and ascending into the heavens.* Then this *same new righteousness and life* shall be *restored in integral and full consummation* when this whole nature shall be abolished and the whole Church raised again shall be led to God. Afterwards the *promise of celestial and eternal goods* always remains *new, one, and the same, constant and immutable,* even if all the goods of this life should be lost.

**This New Testament — or Pact — has a multiple glory over the older Pact.** *First,* it sets forth all the promises *illustrated and clearly expounded, without shadows, without wrappings, types, and figures.* *Second,* it testifies that *by the death of the Son of God* the eternal goods — righteousness and eternal life — have been now *repaired,* and *inchoate and confirmed* by the *glorious resurrection of Christ.* *Third,* it shows that *the Church is gathered from the whole human race,* all the *bars and walls* of the *Israelite polity* being removed, and every *hesitation as regards the exhibiting and fulfilling of the promise* removed. *Fourth,* it testifies that the *ministry* of this new covenant is *glorious and efficacious* in those who embrace the Gospel, and *wondrously preserved* amid so many and so great tumults and the *horrid ruins of empires.* *Fifth,* it testifies that, the Messiah having now been exhibited, *closer and impending is the glorious day* on which the whole Church shall be *adorned with eternal glory* and *led to God,* and shall enjoy the *sight of the whole divinity and all those goods procured and repaired by the Son of God, without sin, without death, and without griefs, unto all eternity.*

**Splendid is Augustine's saying,** who says that the *holy men of God were indeed dispensers and bearers of the Old Testament, but were demonstrated as heirs of the New.* Whence Paul refers the *promise given to Abraham* — which he affirms to be not made void by the Law given long afterward — to the *New Testament.* For so many as have *tasted the fruits of redemption and of eternal salvation obtained through Christ,* stand forth as *heirs of the New Testament,* because *no other name has been given under heaven,* nor is there salvation in any other, save in the name of our Lord Jesus Christ. Therefore also *James* says: *"Thy Salvation I wait for, O Lord."* And *David:* *"I have looked upon Thee in the form of a man, who in the heights is the LORD God Jehovah."*⁴⁷

**Let this saying of Augustine also be observed:** *"It is called the Old Testament because the Law was first revealed and pertains to the old man; but the New was afterward revealed and pertains to the new man."*⁴⁸

**As regards the *mysteries and sacraments* of the Old and New Testaments,** the rule expressed in this verse always remains:

> ***Christ has the body, but Moses the shadow of the body.***⁴⁹

For the *types, sacrifices, and figures represented the Christ to come, the Lamb of God;* but the sacraments of the New Testament set forth to us the *Messiah already exhibited, without types and figures.* For the water of Baptism is such as it is described in the New Testament — namely, the *laver of regeneration.* And the Lord's Supper stands not of bread and wine alone, but *also of the body and blood of Christ, according to the immoveable words of the Institution.* The Old Testament had *victims and true blood indeed in substance* — namely, the *blood of bulls* — but *typical and symbolic of the signification of the blood of the Son of God.* But the New Testament, *lacking types,* has the *true body and true blood of the Lord,* since Christ does not say, *"these are the signs of My body and blood,"* but: ***"these are My body and My blood."*** Concerning these, however, in their proper place, we shall treat below.

**And now we shall take in hand the most saving and most necessary doctrine of the Justification of man before God, God helping us.**⁵⁰

# Locus I. De Iustificatione Hominis coram Deo · On the Justification of Man before God

*(Selnecker heads this Locus in the imprint:* DOCTRINA DE IVSTIFICATIONE HOMINIS CORAM DEO — *the Doctrine on the Justification of Man before God. Locus I opens on p. 38 of the 1579 imprint (OCR ln 1131), following the transition passage in which Selnecker names it "the most saving and most necessary doctrine of the Justification of man before God."*⁵¹*)*

## §1. On the Principal Significations of the Word *Iustitia*

**Passing over the heaping-up of the significations of *iustitia*** (righteousness / justice) in the principal languages — Hebrew, Greek, and Latin — we shall assign to the word ***iustitia*** a threefold use.⁵²

**First,** the name *iustitia* is often used for ***universal justice*** — that is, for *obedience according to all the laws,* or for *congruence with the law* — which congruence is called *virtue,* as it is said:

**ἐν δὲ δικαιοσύνῃ συλλήβδην πᾶσ' ἀρετή ἐστι**⁵³ ·
***Iustitia in se se virtutes continet omnes***
*(Justice contains in itself all the virtues.)*

And **Aristotle** calls *iustitia* the ***right use of all the virtues.***⁵⁴

**Second,** it is used for ***particular justice,*** which renders to each what is his own, and preserves in contracts and in dealings with others an *equality* — that is, an equality of what is *honourable* and *seemly.* These two uses are elsewhere called ***legal justices.***⁵⁵ But in the Church they are otherwise explained than in the doctrine of Philosophy. For the *iustitia legis* requires both *interior and exterior* obedience, and *the congruence of the whole man with God.* Whereas Philosophy discourses only concerning ***external discipline and honourableness,*** and does not judge that those are *sins* which within, in the mind and heart, will and affections, do not congrue with God or with the Law.

**The third** use is proper to the ***Gospel*** — as when we say: ***"the thief on the cross is just by faith,"*** that is, ***accepted and pleasing to God, and given the remission of sins on account of Christ the Mediator.***⁵⁶ Concerning certain other special significations of the word *iustitia* we have spoken in our exposition of Psalm 32.⁵⁷

## §2. What is the Righteousness of Faith or of the Gospel? — Definition

**The righteousness of faith is nothing else than** the absolution from sin and guilt, the acceptance and reconciliation of the sinner who is doing penitence before God — which is made by faith in Christ the Mediator, who died and rose for us; or, ***the remission of sins,*** ***the imputation of the righteousness or obedience of Christ,*** ***the free redemption and liberation from sin, from eternal death, from the wrath of God, from the malediction of the Law, from the tyranny of the Devil, and from damnation.*** This liberation is done *for nothing,* *out of the mere mercy of God for the sake of His Son,* without works of the Law, and without the merit of man, and is the ***adoption of those who believe*** — into the sons and heirs of God — unto life, gladness, and eternal salvation.

**In this description, such as it is, one thing is worthy of consideration:** namely, that ***iustificatio*** is set opposite to the word ***condemnatio.*** For ***to be justified*** is altogether a ***forensic word,*** and signifies to be set at the judgement-seat, ***to be absolved from the guilt of sin and from damnation***⁵⁸ *and pronounced just.* So indeed are we set as guilty before the judgement and tribunal of God. The Law accuses us, convicts us, and subjects us to the sentence of eternal damnation. This is a *heavy and serious action* against us before the judgement of God. Nothing here remains save that we be condemned by the just judgement of God. Nothing in ourselves, nothing in others do we find by which we can be freed. There is *one* who offers Himself as advocate and pays the ***λύτρον*** (*lytron,* ransom-price). To Him therefore, in our extreme consternation and our almost-final despair, we must flee by faith. For on account of His intercession and satisfaction we now come to the *throne of grace,* we are absolved, we are reckoned just, we are received into grace, and we are accepted unto eternal life — and thus the *bill of debt* which was against us is *blotted out* (Col. 2).

## §3. Bernard's Meditation on Psalm 85 — the Four Sisters in Council

**This force of the word "*justify*" Bernard diligently considered** when he set up the ***judicial process,*** or *hypotyposis* of the divine judgement, on the tempering of divine justice and mercy, expounding the saying of Psalm 85: *"Mercy and truth have met each other, righteousness and peace have kissed."* Bernard's sermon is *On the Feast of the Annunciation of Mary,* and it is fitting that this be commended to the student for consideration.⁵⁹

*"That glory may dwell in our land: mercy and truth have met each other, righteousness and peace have kissed."* — **This is our glory,** says the Apostle, *the testimony of our conscience.* Not indeed such a testimony as that proud Pharisee possessed, whose seducing and seduced thought bore witness to himself, but whose testimony was not true; but that testimony which the Spirit Himself bears with our spirit. This testimony, I believe, consists in three things. First it is necessary to believe that thou canst not have remission of sins save through the indulgence of God; then, that thou canst have nothing at all of good work save what He Himself has given; finally, that thou canst by no works whatever *merit* eternal life, unless it also be *freely given.* For who can make clean of the unclean, if not He alone who is clean? Wherefore the Prophet also says: *"Blessed is the man to whom the Lord hath not imputed sin."* And of good works it is altogether certain that no one has them of himself. For if human nature could not stand while yet whole, how much less can it rise again by itself when now corrupted? All things tend, as far as in them lies, toward their origin, and are always the more inclined to that part; so we also, since we have been created out of nothing, if left to ourselves, always decline into sin — which is nothing. Concerning eternal life, however, we know that *the sufferings of this time are not worthy of the future glory* — which would not suffice even if one man bore them all. For the merits of men are not such that eternal life be owed on account of them by right, or that God should do any injury did He not bestow it. For — to pass over that all merits are gifts of God, and that thus man is on their account rather a debtor to God than God to man — what indeed are all merits to so great a glory?

**But these things which we have said suffice not** to save us; rather they are to be held as a certain *beginning* and as it were a *foundation* of faith. Therefore, if thou believest that thy sins cannot be blotted out save by Him against whom alone thou hast sinned and into whom sin cannot fall, thou dost well; but add still that thou believest this too, that *through Him thy sins are forgiven thee.* This is the testimony which the Holy Spirit bears in thy heart, saying: *"Thy sins are forgiven thee."* So does the Apostle judge that *a man is justified freely through faith.* So also of thy merits: if thou believest that thou canst have them save through Him, it suffices not until the Spirit of truth also bears testimony to thee that thou hast them through Him. So also of eternal life must thou have the testimony of the Spirit, that thou art to attain to it by His divine gift. For He Himself forgives sins, He gives merits, and He no less bestows the reward. Furthermore, these testimonies have been rendered credible unto us in the highest. For of the remission of sins, indeed, I hold the argument of the *dominical passion.* The voice of that blood has prevailed much more than the voice of the blood of Abel, crying out in the hearts of the elect *remission of all sins.* For He was delivered up on account of our sins; nor is it doubted that the potency and efficacy of His death for good is greater than that of our sins for evil. And of good works no less efficacious to me is the argument of *His resurrection,* for He rose again for our justification. Furthermore, of the hope of rewards, the testimony is *His ascension:* for He ascended for our glorification. These three thou hast in the Psalms, the Prophet saying: *"Blessed is the man to whom the Lord hath not imputed sin";* and elsewhere: *"Blessed is the man whose help is from Thee";* and elsewhere: *"Blessed is he whom Thou hast chosen and taken to Thyself; he shall dwell in Thy courts."* **This is true glory** — glory dwelling within, because it is from Him who by faith dwells in our hearts. The sons of Adam, seeking the glory that is from one another, would not have the glory that is from God alone.⁶⁰

**Wherefore Bernard proceeds to unfold this dwelling-in-glory** as the four virtues in which the first man was clothed from the beginning of his creation. He was, as the Prophet remembers, *clothed with a garment of salvation;* for in these four virtues the *wholeness of his salvation* consists, nor without these all together can salvation stand. **Mercy** he had received as a guardian and a handmaid, who was to go before him, and to follow, and to protect and to preserve him everywhere. **Truth** was necessary as a teacher, that he might not, like some beast, be merely kept, but as a rational little one be educated. **Justice** he received, that lest he be found wise for the doing of evil, and lest sin be imputed to him as knowing good and doing least of it. **Peace** the most benign hand of the Creator added — a twofold peace: that neither within should there be war, nor without should there be fears — that is, that the flesh should not lust against the Spirit, nor should any creature be a terror to him. But *alas!* this man descended to great destruction and folly *from Jerusalem into Jericho,* and fell among robbers, who *before all things* — as we read — despoiled him. He was despoiled indeed, for he complains to the coming Lord that he is naked; and could not be re-clothed, nor could he recover the garments taken from him, unless Christ should lose His own. **For as he could not be made alive in his soul save by the intervening corporeal death of Christ, so neither could he be clothed save by Christ's despoiling.**

**Bernard now unfolds** how in each of the four virtues Adam was despoiled. **Justice** he lost when Eve obeyed the voice of the serpent, and Adam the voice of the woman, rather than that of God. And though some residue of justice remained (which the Lord in His scrutiny suggested), even that they cast away, turning to *words of malice, to make excuses in sins.* **Mercy** he lost, when Eve was so enkindled in her concupiscence that she spared neither herself, nor her husband, nor her sons to be born — condemning all together to the terrible malediction and necessity of death. Adam too cast the woman by whom he had sinned back upon the divine indignation, as if he willed to turn aside the arrow behind her back. The threefold cord is hard to break: *of curiosity, of pleasure, and of vanity.* These alone the world has: *the concupiscence of the flesh, the concupiscence of the eyes, and the pride of life.* Withdrawn and enticed by these, this cruel mother cast off all mercy. **Truth** the woman also lost, first perversely misconstruing what she had heard (*"Ye shall die the death"*), and saying, *"Lest perhaps we die,"* and finally believing utterly the serpent's denial. Adam too was deprived of truth when he blushed to confess it, pretending the leaves of excuses as coverings. He that shall have blushed at me before men, I shall also blush at him before My Father. **Peace** they lost forthwith, because *there is no peace to the wicked,* saith the Lord. Had they not found the contrary law in their members, they whom the nakedness now first began to shame? — *"I was afraid, because I was naked."* Not so, wretched one, not so a little earlier were you afraid.

**Then Bernard sets forth the heavenly council of the four Sisters.** Grave contention arose among them: for **Truth** and **Justice** were afflicting the wretched man; **Peace** and **Mercy** — free from this zeal — judged rather that he should be spared. Perseverant in vengeance, and the prevaricator falling now on this side, now on that side — the present miseries piled up with the future by the commination of punishment — Mercy and Peace fled to the paternal bosom, saying: *"Will God cast off forever? Or will He not any more be pleased? Will He forget to have mercy, or will He hide His mercies in His wrath?"* Peace herself never ceased, nor did Mercy give silence, but in a certain pious whispering they were speaking, striking the paternal heart. Truth however, and Justice, arose to the constituted day, but arose *even unto the clouds,* not yet clearly bright but darkling to themselves in the zeal of indignation — as it is written in the Prophet: *"O Lord, Thy mercy is in the heavens, and Thy truth even unto the clouds."*

**In the midst was seated the Father of lights,** and each of the two spoke on her own behalf. Mercy said: *"The rational creature needs mercy, for she has been made miserable and most miserable. The time to have mercy on her is come, because now the time has passed."* Truth on the contrary said: *"O Lord, the sentence Thou hast spoken must be fulfilled. Adam must wholly die, together with all who were in him, on the day that he ate of the forbidden fruit in his prevarication."* — *"For what did You beget me, Father, if I am so soon to perish?"* asks Mercy. — *"For who does not know that if the prevaricator escape the sentence of death predicted for him, Thy truth, O Lord, will have perished forever?"* replies Truth. **Behold, one of the Cherubim — the Angel of Great Counsel — suggests to King Solomon** that the Son must be sent, for *all judgement has been given to the Son.* And so before Him the four Sisters returned, repeating their former plaints. Truth admitted that Mercy's zeal was good, *"but would that it were according to knowledge"* — how can it be judged that the prevaricator, rather than the Sister, is to be spared? Mercy in turn answered that Truth spares neither: *"With what indignation dost thou storm against the prevaricator, that thou involvest also the Sister?"*

**The judge, inclining down, wrote with His finger in the earth.** The words were the writing which Peace read in the ears of all — for she sat nearest. **This one says: "*I perish if Adam die not";* and *this one* says: **"*I perish unless he obtain mercy."* — **Let there be a good death, and both have what they seek.** All were struck dumb at the word of wisdom and the elegance of composition. For it was manifest that no occasion of complaint was left, if it could be done that both should be answered: that *he should die, and yet obtain mercy.* But *how shall this be done?* they say. Death is most cruel and most bitter, terrible and dreadful even in the hearing — how can it become *good?* And He answers: **"The death of sinners is most evil, but *precious* can be made the death of the saints.** Shall it not be precious, if it becomes the door of life, the gate of glory?" — *"Precious,"* they say, *"but how shall this be done?"* — *"It can be done,"* He answers, *"if one die out of charity — one who owes nothing to death; for death cannot detain the innocent, but shall be swallowed up, as it is written, in the jaws of Leviathan;* and the middle wall shall be destroyed, and that great chasm shall be dissolved, which is confirmed between death and life — for indeed *charity is strong as death,* nay stronger than death, and if she shall have entered the strong man's atrium, she will bind him and plunder his vessels; and in her very passage she shall make a way through the depth of the sea, that the freed may pass through." A good word did this seem, faithful and worthy of all acceptance. **But where shall he be found — that innocent, who is willing to die not from debt but from will, not from evil merit but from His own good pleasure?**

Truth circles the whole earth, and finds none clean from filth, not even the infant whose life on earth is of one day. Mercy searches all heaven, and even in the Angels — that I not say wickedness — finds less love. Truly this victory was owed to Another, *than whom none has greater love that any man lay down his life for useless and unworthy slaves.* For though He now says of us not *"servants,"* this itself is of the immense love and the singular condescension. — Truth and Mercy return to the constituted day, most anxious, not having found what they sought. But then **Peace, apart, comforting them, said:** *"Ye know nothing, ye think nothing. There is none who doeth this good, not even unto one. He who has given counsel, let Him bring the aid."* **The King understood what she said, and said: *"It repenteth Me that I have made man."*** *"The pain,"* He says, *"holds Me; it lies upon Me to bear the pain, to do penitence on behalf of the man whom I have created."* Then therefore He said: *"Behold, I come. For this cup cannot pass unless I drink of it."* And, having summoned Gabriel, He said: *"Go, and say to the daughter of Sion: Behold, thy King."* And he hastened, and said: *"Adorn thy bridechamber, O Sion, and receive the King."* And to the coming King **Mercy** and **Truth** met each other, as it is written: *"Mercy and truth shall go before Thy face."* **Justice** prepared a throne, according to that saying: *"Justice and judgement are the preparation of Thy throne."* **Peace** came with the King, that the faithful Prophet might be found true, who had said: *"Peace shall be in our land when He shall have come."* Whence it was that at the Lord's Nativity the choir of Angels sang: *"Peace on earth to men of good will."* But then also *Justice and Peace kissed each other,* who till then seemed to be in no small dissent. For the *prior* justice from the Law had rather a *sting* than a kiss, urging rather by fear than provoking by love. But she had not that reconciliation which she now has, which is known to be *the justice which is of faith.* Whence from this — dearly beloved — with a more fervent zeal must justice be pursued by us; since justice and peace have kissed each other, and have entered an *indissoluble covenant of friendships,* — that whoever shall have borne the testimony of justice, may with a joyful face be received into the wide embraces of peace, sleeping and resting in the very Selfsame.⁶¹

**I have recited Bernard's whole sermon, most worthy of consideration.** Now, returning to the doctrine of the justification of man before God, I shall briefly comprehend the sum.

## §4. What Scripture *Denies* for Justification — Six Denials · The Sixfold Structure of Gratuitous Justification

**The whole Scripture *takes away* the justification of man unto eternal life** from these things:⁶²

**First,** from all the *cults of the Gentiles,* which have been an abomination before God. **Second,** from all *elective works.* **Third,** from the *external discipline of reason.* For *"whatever is not of faith, is sin."* **Fourth,** from *Pharisaic justice according to the Law of God* — instituted for this end, that a man having that justice, and doing as much as is in him, might merit the grace of God and eternal life. **Fifth,** from the *whole Law in this life,* and from all *qualities, powers, habits, actions, and human works.* **Sixth,** even from the *good works of the reborn,* or the newness which the Holy Spirit works in the reborn — which although it be a benefit of the Son of God the Mediator (who merits for us the renewal) and is a work of the Holy Spirit, yet Scripture does not ascribe to it the justification of man before God unto eternal life, since it is an ***imperfect newness.***

**But Scripture transmits that man is justified before God unto eternal life *freely,*** out of the immense mercy of God unwilling that we should perish in eternal death (to which we were justly damned by the sentence of the divine Law), and therefore giving and sending us His only-begotten Son — in whom, crucified and dead on account of our sins, and *raised again for our justification,* we must believe, — acknowledging namely and confessing our sins, and by faith apprehending the promise of mercy set forth in Christ the Mediator, and thus certainly determining that we are absolved from the sentence of damnation, and, the righteousness of the Son of God being imputed to us, are reputed just, received into grace, and accepted unto eternal life.

## §5. What is Imputation — Hebrew, Greek, Latin, German · The Twofold Signification · Selnecker's Definition · Three Consequences

**These matters being premised, we shall now speak of three things:** namely, of ***Imputation,*** then of the ***Sedes of the whole doctrine of Justification*** as comprehended in the Epistles of Paul to the Romans and to the Galatians; then, of ***Faith*** and the ***Fruits of Faith.*** We shall also insert an admonition on the word ***Grace,*** and various other things fruitful, useful, and necessary by example for those zealous in piety.

**It is not necessary to discourse at length about the word *imputation* — whence it is derived.** The Hebrews simply name it from cogitation and ratiocination, ***חָשַׁב*** (*ḥāšab*); and the Greeks use the word ***λογίζεσθαι*** (*logizesthai*) — that is, *to gather-together as by reason and by the arithmetic computation which is called* ***λογιστική***, and to build a certain conclusion from certain demonstrations either linear (*grammicis*) or graphic (*graphicis*). The Latins retain the *arithmetical* word, as do the Germans, ***zurechnen.*** But this is rather to be observed first: that the word ***imputation*** is used in the sacred letters in a *twofold* signification.⁶³

**Sometimes** the word *imputation* is a *relational* word in the sense that it has as *foundation* a certain inherent quality in those to whom the imputation is made, and has as *terminus* either the *reward* or the *guilt of punishment.* Among these two, the *relation* is the *imputation itself* — either to reward or to guilt. **Example:** 2 Kings 19: Shimei says to David, *"Impute not to me, my Lord, my iniquity."* 2 Timothy 4: *"All have deserted me; be it not imputed to them"* (**μὴ αὐτοῖς λογισθείη**), namely by the Lord. Romans 4: *"To him that works, the reward is imputed not from grace, but from debt."* Psalm 106: *"Phinehas stood"* (Num. 25, killing the fornicator in burning zeal), *"and it was reputed to him as justice"* — that is, God accepted Phinehas's deed as a just work, and reputed to him a reward which would come even to his posterity.

**Sometimes** the word *imputation* is indeed relational, but in another sense — namely, that the *foundation* of the imputation is *not inherent in those to whom the imputation is made,* but is *in another,* and the *terminus* is the person to whom the imputation is made either for good or for evil; and the *relation* is the *cogitation and will,* or *decree* of the imputer. Thus Genesis 31: Rachel and Leah say, *"He has reputed us as strangers, our father."* Psalm 144: *"O Lord, what is man that Thou reputest him?"* — that is, thinkest of him, and receivest him, adornest, quickenest, and thinkest worthy of the book of life. Romans 2: *"Uncircumcision shall be reputed circumcision if it keep the justices of the Law."* And to Philemon: *"If Onesimus has hurt thee, or oweth thee aught, impute this to me; I will repay."*

**The question is now**, in which of the two significations is the word *imputation* used, when the sacred Scripture speaks of the article of the justification of man before God? Whether *imputation* is to be understood as ***real,*** concerning an *inherent* righteousness (and, as the Papists speak, an *infused charity and dignity* through which men are justified); or only ***rational,*** concerning the *mercy of God imputing to us an alien righteousness not inhering in us, and not consisting in our works?*

**To this question Paul expressly answers, Romans 4,** saying that Abraham was not justified before God by *that* imputation of righteousness which is ***according to debt*** — that is, which has its foundation in him to whom the imputation is made, *that it should be done on account of works.* For *"to him that works, the reward is not imputed according to grace, but from debt."* Paul therefore adds: *"To him who does not work, but believes in Him who justifies the impious, faith is imputed for righteousness,* not from debt, but from grace.*"

**It is certain, therefore, that Paul speaks of a *gratuitous* imputation,** whose *foundation* is neither dignity, nor inherent, nor *infused* quality and charity in him to whom the imputation is made, but *which is from the gratuitous mercy of God only on account of the Son, without our merit* — nay, contrary to what we have merited. For Paul manifestly affirms that the impious is justified by this imputation. By this saying he shows that in us there inheres a contrary foundation — that guilt, rather than justice, would be owed us, if God should will to deal with us according to His just judgement. And since Paul takes justice away from Abraham (that righteousness by which a man can stand before God), much more it is manifest that others are not justified before God by their own works or merits. For if any other, Abraham had many good works and a bright inherent newness — and yet Paul plainly affirms that that inherent newness was *not the foundation* on whose ratio and dignity righteousness was imputed to Abraham unto beatitude.

**Let, then, this be the definition of imputation in the article of justification, such as it is:**

> ***Imputation is the will or*** εὐδοκία *(eudokia,* good pleasure) ***or action of God, whereby, out of His immense and inexpressible mercy and goodness, that which we neither are, nor have, nor have done, He thinks, judges, decrees, and pronounces us to be, to have, and to have done — for the reason that His Son, in whom we believe, on our behalf, or in our name and place, is that whole thing, has it, and has done it. And, on the contrary,* non-imputation *is that whereby God judges and pronounces that which we by nature and in reality are — namely, impious and damned, sons of wrath, enemies of God, guilty of death and obnoxious to eternal punishments — us neither to be, nor to have, nor to have done, because His Son, in whom we believe, has derived that whole thing into Himself, has expiated it by His obedience, and has satisfied for us.***⁶⁴

**From this definition, drawn from the words of Paul, we gather three things:**

**First:** ***that to impute righteousness is nothing else than not to impute — that is, to cover — sin;*** and that our righteousness or innocence before God is nothing else than the *remission and non-imputation of sins,* and the *imputation of the righteousness or obedience of Christ the Mediator, in whom we believe,* as David plainly testifies in Psalm 32: *"Blessed is he whose iniquity is remitted, and whose sin is covered"* (namely through **כֹּפֶר** *Kōpher* or ***ἱλαστήριον*** *hilastērion,* which is Christ), *"and to whom Jehovah has not imputed iniquity."*

**Second:** ***that our and Christ's imputation is commutative.*** To Christ are imputed our sins, which He neither has nor has done. To us, on the contrary, is imputed the righteousness of Christ, and His whole obedience, which we neither have in our nature nor have done, but which by faith we accept — that is, we believe that it is imputed to us by God, so that all our stains are not seen, but rather that we shine, adorned with the most splendid righteousness of the Son of God. For God looks upon us in the form of the man who is Himself Jehovah, and lacks every stain and blot; and He does not look upon us in our own form as we are by nature (2 Sam. 7; 1 Chron. 17). *He does not look upon the wrinkles, the foulness, and the stains of nature — of soul and of body — but looks upon the most beautiful garment by which all those things are covered:* Isaiah 61 — *"He hath clothed me with the garments of salvation, and hath compassed me with the robe of righteousness."* This is, as it were, a certain ***mercantile commutation***⁶⁵ between Christ and us, and contains both doctrine and consolation the most ample. The doctrine is this: that imputation is not the *infusion of a thing or work or quality or righteousness,* as the Papists imagine, but is only a ***rational attribution, application, or ascription*** of the cogitating and willing God, wishing to make us just in this manner. For if imputation were a *real infusion and inhering quality of nature,* then also our sins would so be said to be imputed to Christ that He should be feigned to have been made a participant, a *conscius,* and *guilty* of them in His own nature — which is contumelious, blasphemous, and most false. And the consolation is this: **Our righteousness, sin, merit, guilt, death, and punishment Christ derives into Himself,** that He Himself be reputed with the unjust, as it is written in Isaiah. Our obligation, our huge debt, and our bill of debt He transports into Himself, affixes to the cross by His obedience, and — paying it — tears asunder. On the other hand, for our fault, He gives us His righteousness and merit; for death, life; for punishment, reward.

**And hither pertains that sweet saying of Anselm, Archbishop of Canterbury in England in the year of Christ 1058:**⁶⁶

> *"If the Lord shall have willed to judge thee, say: **'Lord, I object the death of our Lord Jesus Christ between me and Thee, and Thy judgement; otherwise I do not contend with Thee.'** If He shall say that thou hast merited damnation, say: **'Lord, I object the death of our Lord Jesus Christ between Thee and my evil merits, and I offer the merit of His most worthy passion for the merit which I ought to have had and — alas — I have not.'"***

**And elsewhere Anselm says:**

> *"My life terrifies me, and sin appears to me. And if any fruit is seen in my life, it is either simulated, or imperfect, or corrupt. Certainly, the whole life is either in sin, and damnable, or unfruitful, or contemptible. But why do I separate the unfruitful from the damnable? Certainly, if it is unfruitful, it is damnable. For every tree which does not bear good fruit shall be cast into the fire. O then, dry and useless wood, worthy of eternal fires! What shalt thou answer in that day, when the account shall be demanded from thee even to the twinkling of the eye, — of every time of life spent by thee — how it has been expended by thee? O anguish! Hence shall be accusing sins; thence, terrifying justice; below, the horrid chaos of hell yawning; above, an angry judge; within, a burning conscience; without, a burning world. **Scarcely shall the just be saved; the sinner overtaken by faith, into what part shall he betake himself?** To hide will be impossible; to appear, intolerable. Whence to me counsel? Whence salvation? Who is he who is called the *Angel of Great Counsel*? He is Jesus. He is the Judge, between whose hands I tremble.*
>
> ***"Breathe again, O sinner: do not despair.*** *Hope in Him whom thou fearest; flee back to Him from whom thou hast fled. **O Jesu Christe, for the sake of this Thy Name, do to me according to this Thy Name. Look upon the miserable one who invokes Thy Name. O Jesu, be to me Jesus, for the sake of Thy Name.** If Thou wilt receive me into the broadest bosom of Thy mercy, it shall not be too narrow on my account. It is true, my conscience has merited damnation, and my penitence suffices not for satisfaction; but it is certain that Thy mercy overcomes every offence.*
>
> ***"Thou hast taken upon Thyself my sin, and hast derived Thy righteousness into me. Thou hast borne the wrath of the Father for me, and hast reconciled to me the grace of the Father. Thou hast undergone my death, and hast given me Thy life. Thou hast crucified the bill of my debt, and hast given me the bill of Thy righteousness and salvation. Thou hast turned my damnation away, and hast brought me back to the fatherland of eternal life and gladness."***⁶⁷

**Third:** ***that in the imputation and translation of Christ's righteousness into us, three persons concur:*** namely, Christ interceding; our person, who by faith we accede to God; and God the Father, who imputes to us the righteousness of His Son. For Christ, fallen at the knees of the Father, says: *"My Father, I pray Thee that Thou wilt have mercy on the human race, and impute its debt to Me: I shall satisfy for it, and shall obey Thee, and shall bear the punishment of sin."* We, as it were prostrate on the other side, cry out: *"Have mercy on us, and hear the prayers and intercession of Thy Son on our behalf, and impute to us the obedience, satisfaction, payment, and righteousness of Thy Son."* God the Father, denying plainly nothing to His beloved Son, hears the prayers, and imputes to the Son our sins, and to us the righteousness of the Son: *"Be it,"* He says; *"do Thou, Son, satisfy for the sins of the human race; do thou, human race, receive absolution from thy whole debt, since for thee My Son has satisfied."* On this account Paul says in Romans 4: *"Christ was delivered up on account of our sins"* — that is, the Son of God was made Mediator for us, *"obedient to the Father even unto death — yea, the death of the cross"* (Phil. 2), which, among all kinds of death (as Augustine says), was nothing worse — for He *chose the extreme and worst kind of death,* who was to take away every death — *"and rose again on account of our justification"* — that is, God the Father accepted that satisfaction and obedience of the Son as our reconciliation and propitiation, which He proved by the resurrection. And so *Christ is our Saviour by merit and by efficacy.* But let these things suffice for the definition of imputation.

## §6. How we are Just before God by Imputation, since this is not a *res positiva*

**Many have thus argued:**

> *Our righteousness ought to be a thing positive, since nothing justifies save a thing positive.*
> *Imputation is not a thing positive.*
> *Therefore we are not justified by imputation.*

This is the same as the Papist argument, who deride *imputative righteousness* as a *light* thing, and not true or *solid* — as also in the old verses it is said:

> *Cum sis ipse nocens, moritur cur victima pro te?*
> *Stultitia est morte alterius sperare salutem.*
>
> *(Since thou art thyself the guilty, why does the Victim die for thee?*
> *It is folly to hope for salvation by the death of another.)*⁶⁸

**Answer first to the major:** It is true that our justice — *insofar as it is understood as a quality and power in us* — is a thing positive (thus we name *legal, universal, particular, distributive* justice). But it is another thing to speak of the *justice of the person before God* — that is, of *the acceptance of the person,* which is the *remission of sins and gratuitous reconciliation,* or the *imputation of righteousness on account of the Mediator.* — Now to the minor: **Imputation is *not* a privative thing, nor destitute of a firm and certain foundation, but has the *greatest, firmest, and most solid* foundation of all.** And God does not impute righteousness to believers *without a certain and firm foundation,* since He Himself severely reproves imputation lacking a foundation, as in Isaiah 5: *"Woe to them that call evil good and good evil, that put darkness for light and light for darkness, bitter sweet and sweet bitter, and that justify the impious!"* And Proverbs 17: *"He that justifies the impious and condemns the just, both are abominable before God."*

And although in us there is no *foundation* of the imputation of that righteousness whereby we stand before God (since righteousness is imputed *without works,* Rom. 4), nevertheless Paul plainly shows both *that* and *where* the foundation is, saying: *"It is imputed to us who believe on Him who raised Jesus from the dead, who was delivered up on account of our sins and rose again on account of our justification"* — that is, ***the whole obedience and satisfaction of Christ is that foundation and thing positive — certain, firm, sufficient, solid,*** on whose respect, ratio, and dignity God from grace imputes righteousness to believers.

**Wherefore imputation,** to speak plainly and briefly, ***is a relational word, whose splendid, august, and immoveable foundation is the very righteousness of the Son of God, His obedience and satisfaction.*** O sweet, O immoveable and divine, foundation, absolute in all its numbers, and sufficient for us all — on whose respect all the works and merits of all creatures are too languid, weak, sordid, invalid, and cannot at all have the praise of a foundation. **One drop of the blood of Christ**, the Lamb immaculate and innocent, is more precious, more sufficient, and more efficacious than all the works and merits — however most excellent and holy — of all men. He who leans on this foundation stands firm, and can be certain concerning his righteousness before God and concerning eternal salvation. But he who seeks another foundation vacillates back and forth, anxious and doubtful, and at last miserably perishes — according to that saying: *"He who believes not in the Son, the wrath of God remains upon him, and he is already judged"*; and 1 Corinthians 3: *"No man can lay any other foundation than that which is laid, which is Christ Jesus."*

**And that we may return to the very thing,** the foundation having been indicated, the *terminus* of the imputation is to be inquired after, which we find to be *the believing person, to whom is made the imputation unto righteousness and beatitude.* The relation, therefore, is the *immense and inexpressible mercy of God imputing to us righteousness without works.* And in this manner is it said in the Church that ***faith is imputed to us for righteousness*** — namely, not on account of the dignity of the virtue [of faith], but because faith apprehends in the promise of the Gospel the merit of Christ and the mercy of God, in which consist both the *foundation* and the *relation* of the imputation of righteousness unto beatitude.

## §7. Whether God is Unjust in reputing them Just who are by nature Evil and Unjust

**Many have objected against the doctrine of the imputation of Christ's righteousness** that God is deceived and is an unjust judge, if He pronounces and reputes them just who are truly impious and have nothing of justice in themselves. That this objection may rightly be answered, let these things be considered in due order.

**1.** *If in God there were only the justice that judges and damns the guilty, then it would be true that righteousness could not be imputed to the unjust.* But in God there is not only the justice described in the Law, but also there is in God **immense mercy, clemency, commiseration, and goodness, and so great a grace as both overcomes the sins of the whole world and is far greater than justice and the wrath of God** — especially since the Son of God has assumed the human nature into which the mercy is to be poured out.

**2.** As *ungrateful, wicked, and extremely improbable* — a **σχέτλιος** (*schetlios,* a stubborn wretch) — that man would be, who, having been subjected to punishment for his crimes and then, by the intercession of good men, and by the goodness and commiseration of the judges, and by the hope of his own amendment, absolved and given life, should say that those judges were *unjust* because they did not execute strict right according to the rigour and norm of justice, but employed ***ἐπιείκειαν*** (*epieikeian,* equity), and out of commiseration remitted the punishment: **so let us think that they are ungrateful and evil who accuse God of injustice in this — that on account of the Son He has mercy on us, and are worthy neither of commiseration nor life, but of the execution of justice and of death.**

**3.** It is certain that ***the Word of God and His cogitation are by no means deceived; but all the rational and verbal (so to speak) things of God are firmer and more efficacious than all the real things of all creatures.*** For God ***to say, to think, to impute, and to pronounce is to do and to be done,*** according to that saying: *"He Himself spoke, and they were made";* and, *"He calls those things which are not as though they were"* (Rom. 4). **God alone has the right and the omnipotent efficacy of not imputing to us guilt and injustice, but of imputing our guilt to His Son, and of imputing the innocence of the Son to us.** This mystery we shall rightly understand in eternal life.

## §8. Whether by Imputation Alone, or also by *Inchoatio,* are we Just

**Often the true and pious doctors of the Church use the word *justification* in a general sense,** and universally include the whole business of saving men — that is, the cause, the coherences, and the effects — or *grace and gift,* or the whole benefit of Christ: namely, remission of sins, reconciliation with God, imputation of the righteousness or obedience of Christ, acceptance or adoption unto eternal life and the right of the sons of God; likewise the donation of the Holy Spirit, the *inhabitatio divinitatis,* renovation of corrupted nature, mortification of the flesh and vivification of the Spirit, and finally the resuscitation of the body unto the likeness of the Son of God unto eternal life. In this general signification there are two parts which Paul distinguishes: namely, ***grace*** and ***gift.***⁶⁹

**When, therefore, the question is asked *what that justice is which we ought to bring before God when we deal with Him,*** that He may absolve us from the sentence of damnation and may be placated and propitious to us, that we may be His sons and accepted unto eternal life — then ***only the imputation of the righteousness of Christ is required,*** as Paul evidently shows. **But when in a general sense the whole business of saving men (that is, the whole benefit of Christ) is spoken of,** and the *renovation or donation of the Holy Spirit* — always conjoined with justification — is added to imputation, then it is usually said that we are just by *imputation and by inchoation:* that is, as elsewhere it is said, *we are justified by faith alone, but faith is not alone or dead.*

For as regards the ***perfection*** of justice, it consists in the merit, obedience, and satisfaction of the Son of God, which is imputed to us, which we apprehend by faith and oppose to the judgement of God. But as regards the ***efficacy*** — or our newness, which we call the *justice of works and of good conscience,* by which also we ourselves *ought* to be just — we experience great imperfection in our nature *even when regenerate;* and therefore we call it ***inchoation,*** which nevertheless is *not a part of that perfect justice by which we stand before the judgement of God,* and which we rightly name the ***justifying justice,*** as will be said in the following question.

## §9. Whether *inchoatio* and Newness of Life are any Part of our Justice by which we Stand before God

**John 16: The Son of God says: *"The Holy Spirit shall convict the world of righteousness, because I go to the Father."*** And Paul, Romans 5, says: *"As by the sin of one, damnation came upon all, so by the righteousness of one, justification came unto all."* And Galatians 2: *"If justice be from the works of the Law, Christ died in vain."* — These passages, together with others similar, evidently show ***that the justifying justice is nowhere to be placed and sought save in the most perfect obedience of Christ.*** **Renewal, however, is not a part of the justifying justice, but is an *effect, fruit, end,* or *consequence* of justice**, and at the same time is a *part, beginning, earnest, and first-fruits* of the future life, and in this life is always imperfect, and is not equal in all — in one more illustrious and greater, in another less conspicuous. For *gifts* are not equal; but *grace* is equal, one and the same in all and above all. And it is most rightly said: ***"None is quickened save the justified."***

From this brief admonition it can be seen what must be judged concerning the ***inherent newness*** of the Papists (which they will to be *in loco iustitiae*), or concerning the ***essential righteousness of God dwelling in us and making us just by His righteousness formally,*** as **Osiander** established.⁷⁰ ***For everything in us that is imperfect cannot be the perfect justice before God.***

## §10. The Principal Arguments of Paul in the Epistle to the Romans on the Justice of God

**The *sedes* of the doctrine of the justification of man before God is in two Epistles of Paul — to the Romans, and to the Galatians.**⁷¹ The Epistle to the Romans has *four parts.*

**The first,** from the first to the fifth chapter, contains two propositions: the *Legal* and the *Evangelical.* The Legal is completed in the three prior chapters, and stands in an induction thus: *All the Gentiles are sinners* (this is the sum of the first chapter); *all the Jews are sinners* (this is the sum of the second chapter); *therefore all men are sinners* (this is the conclusion of the third chapter). The *Evangelical proposition* begins immediately at the end of the third chapter: ***"Men are justified freely by the grace of God through the redemption made by Christ."*** This proposition is proved in the fourth chapter by ***ten arguments.***

**I. Argument** — from the example of Abraham, or from *genus,* thus:

> By one and the same manner all the saints in all ages of the world are justified.
> Abraham was justified by *faith, righteousness imputed to him on account of the Seed to come, not by his own merits.*
> Therefore we also, both Jews and Gentiles, are justified *by faith, not by our own works.*

**II. and III. Arguments** — from the *definition of justification,* or from the *testimony of Scripture:*

> Justification is the ***gratuitous imputation of righteousness.***
> To works or to *meritorious* deeds nothing is given gratuitously, but reward is owed to them as debt.
> Therefore we are not justified by works, but *freely, through faith.*

And:

> Justification is nothing else than the ***remission of sins*** by which God receives us on account of the Mediator, David testifying, Psalm 32.
> Therefore we are not just by our works and merits.

**IV. Argument** — from the series of cause and effect:

> The effect is not prior to its cause.
> To Abraham righteousness is imputed *before he is circumcised.*
> Therefore circumcision did not justify, but was only the ***seal of the righteousness of faith.***

**V. and Principal Argument** — from the cause of the promise:

> The promise of grace or of reconciliation ought to be *certain.*
> A promise depending on the condition of the Law is made *uncertain,* because *the Law works wrath.*
> Therefore the promise of grace does not depend on the condition of the Law, but is *given freely, and is to be accepted by faith.*

From this argument the remaining five, which follow, are extracted by Paul.

**VI. Argument:** ***Blessedness*** or the remission of sins and the inheritance of eternal life is promised to Abraham not on account of the Law or his merits, but on account of the *Seed, Christ.* Therefore that blessedness, righteousness, and eternal life — or the whole *benediction of Abraham* promised — is not given on account of works, but is to be accepted *by faith.*

**VII. Argument:** *Heirs receive the goods bequeathed freely, only from the promise of the testator.* But believers are *heirs of the goods of Christ.* Therefore they receive them freely, and not on account of their own merits.

**VIII. Argument:** *Neither would faith be firm, nor the promise certain, if justification were contingent on the works of the Law,* since no one satisfies the Law. But both faith and promise are, and remain, firm and certain. Therefore justification is not contingent on works or on the Law, but from faith acquiescing in the gratuitous promise.

**IX. Argument:** *If justice should have a place in us, it is necessary that we be absolved from sins and from guilt, and have God placated and hearts tranquil.* The Law neither absolves us from the guilt of sin, nor placates the wrath of God, nor renders hearts peaceful, but does plainly the contrary — accusing us, denouncing wrath, and damning us. Therefore we are not justified by the Law.

**X. Argument:** *Where a promise is, there faith is required, from the nature of relatives.* For a promise would be given in vain unless we should embrace it by faith. Now faith is *assent.* And justice is promised. Therefore justice is to be received *by faith.*

**By these ten arguments the first part of the Epistle is completed.**⁷²

**The second part** — from the fifth to the ninth chapter — resolves both propositions, Legal and Evangelical, into their simple parts by a certain *analytical* ratio. For in the fifth, when the *effects* of justification had been treated, the doctrine of *original sin* is added, and of ***grace*** — that is, of the *gratuitous remission of sins on account of Christ.* In the sixth and the beginning of the seventh it is taught that *new obedience necessarily follows in the justified.* Then follow disputations on the Law of God, on the effects of the Law, and on sin remaining in the saints in this life. In the eighth, again — on *grace* and the *effect of grace,* on the distinction of the powers of the unregenerate and the regenerate man, on the future glory and the hope of eternal life, on the person and the benefits of the Holy Spirit, on God the Father and God the Son.

**The third part** — from the ninth to the twelfth — treats of the ***predestination*** or ***election*** of those to whom grace pertains. And in the ninth chapter it is shown *who are elected unto grace and eternal life* — namely, not those who bring *merits, primogeniture,* or any *carnal prerogatives* (as the Jews thought), but those who by faith apprehend the mercy and grace of God offered and exhibited to the whole human race through the Son. **For salvation is contingent through *mercy, not through debt.***⁷³

**And since the Pauline passage seems to some rather difficult, it was determined to add a brief ἐξήγησις** (*exēgēsis,* exposition), and that only *grammatical,* for the sake of the younger — first indicating that the whole ninth chapter contains a *collation between the Jews and the Gentiles,* and shows that Jews are not to be preferred to Gentiles in the business of salvation, because ***the promise of grace is one, and the same, and equal in all,*** and testifies that *not by flesh, not by the prerogatives of race or of place, but by promise, calling, and the Word of God, one becomes a son of God.* The words are these:

> *"When Rebecca had once conceived from Isaac our father, before they were born (Jacob and Esau) and had done any good or evil"* (both, that is, Jacob and Esau — for it is not that *good* is distinctly of Jacob and *evil* of Esau, but *each* is referred to *both*), *"that the purpose of God according to election might remain"* (that is, that the will of God — which is eternal and to us unsearchable — and His ***εὐδοκία*** (*eudokia,* good pleasure) *revealed to us in the Word and brought forth by the Son from the bosom of the Father — might be immoveable), *"it was said to her: not from works"* (that is, from *merit of works*), *"but from Him that calls"* (from the *grace of Him that calls*): *"The greater shall serve the lesser"* (Gen. 25). And as it is written: *"Jacob have I loved, Esau have I hated"* (Mal. 1).

**The scope and sense of these words** is that the sons of God become such by *election,* not by natural gifts or by Law or by works — and that the Church is made from *election and gratuitous vocation,* not from *carnal propagation* or any *carnal prerogatives.* And the cause of election is not *merits* — whether *past or future* — but *only the mercy of God, on account of the Son apprehended by faith.*

**God loves Jacob** — not on account of the works or merits of Jacob, but only from mercy on account of the Son. On the contrary, **God hates Esau** — by reason of His own justice, not however apart from Esau's own fault. For it does not follow:

> God loves Jacob, without merit of Jacob.
> Therefore God hates Esau, without fault of Esau.

**For these contraries are not equal.** Jacob and Esau were equal — both born in sins, and by nature sons of wrath. **But Jacob God receives into grace without his own merit;** but **Esau He hates by just judgement, whereby sinners and unbelievers are rejected.** For our sin is worthy of God's hatred, and no one is in the hatred of God without his own fault. To emerge from the hatred of God and to be received into grace is not a work of ours or a merit, but only of the *mercy of God receiving us through and on account of the Son.* Esau, therefore, is in the hatred of God *not without his own fault,* since he is not only by nature a son of wrath, but is *not* an heir of the gratuitous promise concerning the first-begotten Liberator of the human race. But Jacob — though also by nature a son of wrath — nevertheless *by inheritance receives the land of promise,* and *Christ is born from his seed:* and the whole thing is from the mercy of God, not from any work or merit of Jacob.

> ***RULE.*** *He who has the promise concerning Christ, and believes it, he is said to be loved by God. But he who lacks the promise and faith, he is said to be in the hatred of God.*

**As to the fact that Paul's words are urged** — *"Before Jacob and Esau had done any good or evil"* — this is to be resolved according to the *context* of the whole discourse and the *scope* Paul attends to, without any sophistical distortion of the words. For Paul opposes those words to the *Pharisaic opinion of the merit of works, of carnal primogeniture, or other prerogatives* (or, as they speak today, of *meritum congrui et condigni*), and wishes to say nothing else save this: that those who are loved by God are loved *not on account of their own merits,* but only *on account of the mercy of God exhibited in His Son;* and that those who are not loved are rejected *by the just judgement of God and by His wrath against sin* — and thus *neither can these have anything of which to glory unless they will to glory in the Lord, nor those to complain unless they will to complain of themselves and their own sins.*

**The first objection follows:** *"What then shall we say? Is there iniquity with God? God forbid!"* — that is, since Jacob and Esau are equal, why does God not either elect or reject each? Why does He not join equals to equals?

**Response:** *For to Moses He says: "I will have mercy on whom I have mercy, and I will show commiseration on whom I show commiseration"* (Exod. 33; or, as it is in the Hebrew, *"I will be propitious, or clement, to whom I have been propitious"*). — **This is: the Son of God Himself answers the objection.** And it is the proper preaching of the Son of God on the *gratuitous mercy and clemency of God toward men fleeing to Him as Mediator.* It is true indeed that all men are equal, and that equals owe equals — that is, *wrath and eternal punishments.* Now what discrimination is there between men — of whom some are reconciled to God, some remain under the wrath of God — must be judged according to the doctrine of the Gospel, which is: *"He that believes in the Son has life; he that does not believe, the wrath of God remains upon him."* Now believers have life *without their own merits,* and are *saved freely.* By this saying, therefore, the *merits of men* are excluded, and the little word ***freely*** is inculcated. And this is the true sense of these words, to which strange disputations *on particularity* (whether God excludes some from grace) are not to be added; but let us know that God is *just and equal according to the universal promise.*

> *"Therefore it is not of him that wills, nor of him that runs, but of God that shows mercy."* — That is, *no effort, no works, no merits by which men wish to be saved profit anything, if the mercy of God be lacking.*⁷⁴

**Augustine:** ***"Woe to the life of men, however praiseworthy, if mercy be removed."*** These things are said against vain glorying in one's own merits. For God can bear nothing less than *glorying* and *proud arrogance,* especially in the cause of the salvation of men. For every human will is *multiply contaminated with sin,* and every course is erroneous, unless it be purged, bent, and led back into the way by God from grace on account of the Son. **Therefore is it of God that shows mercy, that we will rightly and run rightly.**

> *For Scripture says to Pharaoh (Exod. 9): "For this very cause have I raised thee up, that I might show My power in thee, and that My name might be announced in all the earth."*

This example shows that it is *not of him that wills or runs, but of God that shows mercy.* For Pharaoh, *deprived of the mercy of God,* willed what was perverse, and ran by his own certain judgement and impetus until he plunged headlong into destruction. But here it is asked: *Why was he deprived of God's mercy? Without his own fault? Was God Himself the cause of Pharaoh's sin and destruction?* — **By no means.** For all the impious perish *only by their own merit and guilt, without any fault of God.* God sends to Pharaoh, and calls him through Moses to penitence, that he may be saved. But Pharaoh does not obey God calling, but *willingly burns with wrath* and obeys the Devil inciting and impelling him to contumacy and ruin.

**But why does God say: *"I have raised thee up"*?** — I answer: in the Hebrew the verb is ***הֶעֱמַדְתִּיךָ*** (*heʿĕmadtîkā,* "I have caused thee to stand") — that is, *"I have preserved thee,"* or *"I have made thee stand, that I might show thee My power."* God therefore in no way speaks of His *election* or *creation* of Pharaoh (as if He had created or raised him for this end that he should perish), but speaks of ***sustaining,*** as if He should say: *"I have preserved thee thus far, and have made thee to stand and to remain, and have not deleted thee suddenly — that to thee, resisting My admonitions, indulging some time and giving space for penitence, and conniving at thy contumacy, I might at last show how miserable, weak, foolish, of no counsel and no help thou art, compared with My power."* For God declares His power in the deletion of powerful tyrants when He casts them down from their height. **This is the simplest and truest sense.**

For the word ***עָמַד*** (*ʿāmad*) certainly signifies *to stand.* The Seventy render it here in the third conjugation ***διετηρήθης*** (*dietērēthēs,* "thou hast been preserved"). But Paul more emphatically has rendered ***ἐξήγειρά σε*** (*exēgeira se,* "I have raised thee up") — *"I have with singular power made thee my target, as an illustrious example to others of My wrath and of My arrows; and this I did not by My fault, but by My evil-punishment and the execution of My judgement against thee, rebellious against Me."*

> **Conclusion:** *"Therefore whom He wills He has mercy on, and whom He wills He hardens."*

**God has mercy on us according to His mercy on account of the Son.** But whom He wills He *hardens* — namely, according to the execution of His justice. For God wills *both*: our salvation according to His mercy, and He wills also punishment according to His justice, when we do not obey the Gospel.

**On the verb *to harden*,** let the usual rule be held: that in the Hebrew tongue, verbs signifying actions are often set forth ***potentialiter*** — that is, to be expounded not of *efficient operation* but of *will, faculty,* and ***permission.*** Examples in Scripture are frequent: **Isaiah 63:** *"Why, O Lord, hast Thou made us to err, and hast hardened our heart, that we should not fear Thee?"* — that is, *"why hast Thou forsaken us, and let us to err, and drawn back Thy clement hand from us, and let us to be borne by our own impulse, and to become secure, and hast not drawn us back?"* **Psalm 141:** *"Incline not my heart to an evil thing"* — that is, *let it not be inclined,* as we say, *"Lead us not into temptation"* — that is, *let us not be led into it, but restrain us, that we fall not into errors and crimes.* Compare also Matt. 11 (*"Thou hast hidden these things from the wise"* = *hast willed them to be hidden, when they despise them*); Matt. 16 (*"He that shall have lost his life for My sake shall find it"* = *shall be willing or ready to lose life*); Luke 8 (*"Hearing they hear not"* = *they will not hear and attend*); 1 Cor. 6 (*"Why are ye not injured?"* = *why are ye not willing to suffer wrong?*); 1 Cor. 10 (*"In all things I please all"* = *I desire to please*); Gal. 5 (*"Ye who are justified by the Law"* = *ye who wish to be justified*).

**Thus in this place we expound the word *to harden* through the word of *permission.*** ***God hardens*** — that is, *He lets those to be hardened who contumaciously resist,* as Paul a little later himself interprets, saying: *"Whom He wills He hardens: God has endured with much long-suffering the vessels of wrath."* And Basil says: *God hardened Pharaoh by long-suffering and by the delay of punishment.* And Paul in Romans 2 expressly affirms that *each is himself the efficacious cause of his own hardening,* saying: *"Or dost thou despise the riches of the goodness of God, His patience and long-suffering, ignorant that the goodness of God calls thee to penitence, but according to thy hardness and impenitent heart thou treasurest up wrath to thyself in the day of wrath."*⁷⁵

**Oecumenius:** *"He who is hard and disobedient, God permits him to be hard and unbelieving."* **Nazianzen:** *"That the Egyptian tyrant was hardened by God, is not to be so accepted as if God by His divine will had impressed obstinacy and stiffness in the mind of the tyrant, but that the will — otherwise more prone to malice — did not receive the mollifying word of this stiffness, by which it was opposed to the sentence of God."* **Athanasius and Chrysostom:** *"Then does God harden the hearts of mortals when He long endures them by His magnitude; on account of whose endurance afterward the same become more obstinate to evil: nor does God bear Himself otherwise than as some master who has got a wicked servant, whom by his humanity and clemency he makes worse — not that the master has hardened the servant by malignity, but that the servant abuses the master's placability to the augmenting of impropriety, as one contemning the benignity in the master."*

From these examples and testimonies we rightly gather that the *hardening* whereby God is said to harden the heart of Pharaoh and of the impious is to be accepted **not of an efficient action, but of a permission and desertion.** For by the most just judgement of God — as Augustine says in Book 5 *against Julian* — Pharaoh is hardened, either by *desertion,* or in some other way, explicable or inexplicable. And when it is said that *"God hardens whom He wills,"* it is not so to be understood (Augustine, *On the Will of God*), as if God should work *hardness of heart* in a man where before it was not. *For what else is hardness than to oppose the mandates of God?* Whence blessed Stephen said to the people: *"Ye stiff-necked, uncircumcised in heart and ears, ye always resist the Holy Spirit"* (Acts 7). And Paul in Rom. 10 cites from Isa. 65: *"All the day I have spread out my hands to a contumacious and contradicting people."* And hither pertains the plaint of Matt. 23: *"How often have I willed to gather thee, and thou wouldst not."*

**Ambrose adds this on the words *"He has mercy on whom He wills, and hardens whom He wills"*:** *"These words are spoken from the person of the objector, and are an inference or absurd objection of the adversary, saying thus: 'Therefore does God have mercy on whom He wills to have mercy, and hardens whom He wills?'"* — This sentence, whether it please or displease, shows nevertheless that the *sincere and pious doctors* always *disapproved the Stoic opinion of efficacious hardening.*

**Let this also be diligently considered:** in the history of Pharaoh, mention is made of Pharaoh's hardening ***sixteen times*** — but *diversely.* Sometimes it is said *of God* that He hardens the heart of Pharaoh. In this locution the word is ***כָּשָׁה*** (*qāšāh,* to make hard) — properly to make *perplexed* and *intricate,* so that it cannot easily be resolved or explained — which God did in Pharaoh through signs and wonders and by alternations, now of punishments, now of mitigation. For thus God hardens the impious in all ages, punishing sometimes and soon again omitting the punishment, and *conniving longer at their crimes,* that the impious, abusing the long-suffering of God and the paucity of punishments, may at last be more gravely punished — as the saying goes: *"God compensates the delay with the gravity of the punishment."* Sometimes, on the contrary, it is said of Pharaoh himself that *he hardened his own heart* or *his heart was hardened.* Here the words are ***חָזַק*** (*ḥāzaq,* to strengthen) and ***כָּבַד*** (*kābad,* to be heavy). The former properly signifies *he strengthened, held fast* — though also of God permitting Pharaoh's heart thus to be *strengthened,* the same word is used at Exod. 4 and 7. The latter signifies *was made heavy, or made heavy* — as Exod. 8: *"Pharaoh, seeing that respiration had been given, aggravated his heart";* and Exod. 9: *"The heart of Pharaoh was aggravated."*

The Greeks render ***qāšāh*** and ***ḥāzaq,*** when they are used of God, by ***σκληρύνω*** (*sklērynō,* to harden); when ***ḥāzaq*** is used of Pharaoh, they render ***κατισχύω*** (*katischyō,* to prevail); Exod. 7: **κατίσχυσεν ἡ καρδία Φαραώ** — "the heart of Pharaoh was hardened" — that is, *made firm by a certain prevailing,* as the Germans say, ***Er hat überhand wollen behalten*** — *"with his whole spirit and with all his sinews, he apprehended the firm retention of his obstinacy."* But ***kābad*** they render by ***βαρύνω*** (*barynō,* to burden): *"the heart of Pharaoh was aggravated";* and Exod. 8: **ἐβαρύνθη ἡ καρδία αὐτοῦ.** This diversity of words shows the diverse *mode of signifying.* For **it is said both that God hardened the heart of Pharaoh and that Pharaoh himself hardened his heart.**

**Now if any one contends that the hardening arose from God,** since it is written *"God hardened the heart of Pharaoh,"* another may set opposite that the hardening arose from Pharaoh, since it is written *"Pharaoh hardened his own heart."* For ***ḥāzaq*** is used both of God and of Pharaoh. Lest, then, a contradiction be made, the true sense is to be sought — which is: ***God lets the contumacious and heavy heart be borne by its own impulse, that at last it be more gravely punished.*** These things concerning the word ***Hardening*** can be usefully propounded and considered.

**Now follows another objection of the impious.**

> ***"Then thou sayest to me: 'Why doth He yet find fault? For who resists His will?'"*** — This objection of the adversaries argues thus: *God has mercy on whom He wills, and hardens whom He wills. Therefore the hardening happens by God's willing; and thus, God willing, the impious both sin and perish. Whence follows also: 'No one justly accuses in another what he himself does; God hardens; therefore He does not justly complain of the impiety of the impious, nor justly accuse, punish, and damn them.'*

**I answer.** *These words are recited* ***δραματικῶς*** (*dramatikōs,* dramatically) *of the impious,* and are objected by those who accuse God as though He were the author of sin. And how the hardening happens *God willing* was said a little before: namely, *not by an efficient operation of God hardening the hearts of the impious,* but by ***the permission of God willing punishment of the impious on account of that very efficacious operation of their own.*** — And as concerns *"no one justly accuses in another what he himself does,"* it is to be answered that this is true *of efficient will and operation.* But it is to be judged otherwise of him who is said *to do something* when he either *permits* something to be done, or does not *violently prohibit.* God, therefore, since He hardens by permitting, and not by softening or by violently prohibiting, *justly accuses, punishes, and damns the hardened.* For the *efficient cause* of the hardening is not in God Himself, but in the man — as Oecumenius says, **ὁ θεὸς σκληρὸν καὶ ἀπειθῆ εἶναι συγχωρεῖ** (*"God concedes him to be hard and disobedient"*).⁷⁶

As regards the *will of God* — to which all things are subject, and according to which all things are done — it must be known that God wills otherwise the things which depend simply on the divine will, and otherwise those which depend on the will of *men or of rational creatures,* especially of evil creatures such as the devils and the impious, whose counsels and actions He does not by His spontaneous will *effect or aid,* but *determines and fixes their limit,* how far they ought to run. For although the will of God is one and the same, simple and immutable, as His essence is one and simple, yet that very will, as the ***ῥοπή*** (*rhopē,* balance-tongue) — or *pointer of the balance* — consists in the *middle and tempering of justice and mercy,* or of Law and Gospel. Whence Augustine says in article 2 of his response to articles falsely imputed to him: *"God wills all men to be saved (1 Tim. 2); of whom, however, because many perish, is of the merit of those perishing; that many are saved, is of the gift of Him saving. For that the guilty is damned, is of the inculpable justice of God; that the guilty is justified, is of the ineffable grace of God. Therefore in whatever way it succeeds to men, God's will is always fulfilled. If they believe and are saved, this itself God wills in the highest. If, however, they do not believe and perish, God wills also that on account of unbelief they be punished and perish."* And elsewhere he says: *"This God wills, that man be either obedient, or that the disobedient not go unpunished."*⁷⁷

**Let the students read chapter 100 and the four following in Augustine's *Enchiridion,*** which shows *that men often do something against the will of God, and yet not beside the will of God, since at last the will of God is fulfilled concerning them — permitting for a while, out of mercy, impious men to act evilly, that they may have space for penitence, and at last from justice punishing the unconverted.*

**However, since today similar vociferations concerning the *immutable will of God and the absolute necessity of all actions and events* are everywhere heard** — which are confirmed from the writings of D. Luther of holy memory, especially from the little book ***De Servo Arbitrio*** — and by this dye difficult scruples are cast into tender consciences, those zealous of piety and truth are to be exhorted diligently to read that which Luther himself in his *Swan-Song* — that is, in the *Enarration of Genesis 26* — left in writing after him, declaring his own sense and showing *how he wills the little book "On the Servile Choice" to be received and understood,* and at last uttering a *prophecy* on the perverted understanding and use both of that little book and of his other writings. A few words, for the sake of the younger, we shall append:⁷⁸

> ***"I hear that wicked voices are spread everywhere among the nobles and the great concerning predestination or the divine foreknowledge. For thus they speak: 'If I am predestinate, whether I act well or ill, I shall be saved. If I am not predestinate, I shall be damned, no account being had of works.'***
>
> ***"If these voices are true, then plainly the incarnation of the Son of God is removed, and the Passion and Resurrection, and whatsoever He did for the salvation of the world. What will the Prophets and the whole sacred Scripture profit? what the Sacraments? Let us then throw away and trample under foot all these things. These are diabolical and poisoned darts, and the original sin itself, by which the Devil seduced our first parents. And again: these are the prestiges of the Devil, by which he tries to make us doubtful and unbelieving — since Christ came into this world for this cause, that He might make us most certain.***
>
> ***"For it must needs be that either despair or contempt of God, of the sacred Bible, of Baptism, and of all divine benefits — by which He willed to confirm us against uncertainty and doubting — will follow.***
>
> ***"To these thoughts must be opposed the true and firm knowledge of Christ. Do this one thing, that thou receive the Son, that Christ in His nativity, miracles, and cross please in thy heart. For there is the Book of Life in which He is written.***
>
> ***"We ought to detest and flee those wicked voices which the Epicureans boast: 'If it be necessary for this thing thus to happen, it shall happen. It behoves that to be done which God has fore-determined.' For God did not come down from heaven that He might make thee uncertain about predestination, that He might teach thee to contemn the Sacraments, absolution, and the other divine ordinances: nay, He instituted them that He might render thee most certain, and take away the disease of doubting from thy mind — that thou mightest not only believe with the heart but also see with carnal eyes and feel with hands. Thou hast the Gospel, thou art baptised, thou hast absolution, thou art a Christian. God says to thee: Behold, thou hast My Son; Him do thou hear and receive. If thou dost this, thou art now certain of thy faith and salvation, and art most certainly predestinate. Hear the incarnate Son, and predestination will offer itself spontaneously.***
>
> ***"Staupitz was wont to console me, labouring in that temptation of despair, with these words: 'Look upon the wounds of Christ, and upon the blood poured out for thee; from these predestination shall shine forth.' The Son of God, therefore, is to be heard, who was sent into flesh, and appeared for this reason, that He might dissolve this work of the Devil and make thee certain concerning predestination; and therefore He says to thee: 'Thou art My sheep, because thou hearest My voice: no one shall snatch thee out of My hands.'***
>
> ***"Many who have not resisted this temptation in this manner have been cast headlong into destruction.***
>
> ***"Acknowledge the child Jesus, and hear, and delight in Him, and give thanks. If thou hast Him, then thou also hast God the hidden together with the revealed. And this is the only way, truth, and life, outside which thou wilt find nothing but destruction and death.***
>
> ***"When the Devil assails thee with these thoughts or doubtings on predestination, say only: 'I believe in our Lord Jesus Christ, of whom I have no doubt but that He was incarnate, suffered, and died for me, into whose death I have been baptised.' By this reply the temptation shall vanish, and Satan shall turn his back. Thou canst repel Satan from thee with the fewest words: 'Get thee back, Satan; the Son of God came into this world, was incarnate, has redeemed me, sits at the right hand of the Father, and is my Saviour.'***
>
> ***"THE PROPHECY OF D. LUTHER.***
>
> ***"After my death many will bring forth my books into public view, and thence will confirm all sorts of errors and DELIRIA of their own. I have written, however, among other things (in the little book On the Servile Choice, of which D. Luther makes mention in the same place), that all things are absolute and necessary — but at the same time I have added that God the revealed is to be looked upon, as we sing in Psalm 45: 'His name is Jesus Christ, the LORD of Hosts,' etc. But they will pass over all these passages, and will seize only that concerning God the hidden (on absolute necessity). You, therefore, who now hear me, remember that I have taught this: that it is not to be inquired into concerning the predestination of the hidden God, but that it is to be acquiesced in that which is revealed through the vocation and through the ministry of the Word. For there thou canst be certain concerning thy faith and salvation, and canst say: 'I believe in the Son of God, who said: He that believes in the Son has eternal life.' Therefore in Him there is no damnation or wrath, but the good pleasure of God the Father. These same things I have also elsewhere testified in my books, and now also with living voice I hand down: therefore I am excused."*** — Thus far Luther.

**Now let us hear the response of D. Paul.**

> ***"But O man, who art thou that dost expostulate with God? Shall the thing formed say to Him that formed it, 'Why hast Thou made me thus?' Has not the potter of the clay power to make of the same mass one vessel unto honour, and another unto ignominy?"***

Paul answers to the objection of the Epicureans, or of others, by ***concession.*** *"I set,"* he says, *"the case: even if God were to send the impious into destruction — which He has never done, nor does, nor shall do — nevertheless no one could contend with Him. For what else is a man than the clay of the earth, a wretched mass, a fluid seed, phlegm, bile, ashes, and earth? He could, therefore, if He willed, deal with us by His free will, and even by προσωποληψία (partiality) in whatsoever manner it pleased Him; nor would He on that account be unjust, nor would a man dare to demand from God the Creator the cause of this made thing. For if it is lawful to the potter that from the same mass he make diverse vessels, and no one reproves the potter, why should it not be lawful to God, if He would, to form some men either for these or for other uses, and either for life or for death? For God has all and every man in His hand as the potter the clay (Isa. 45; Jer. 18)."*

**This first response is by *concession,*** in which Paul affirms nothing certain, but *sets the case* — and at the same time attributes to God the *praise of justice,* and admonishes us all of our misery, and exhorts us to *humility* and to *open confession of our unworthiness* — in which confession pious minds think and speak nothing else save this: *"Thou art my God the Creator; I am Thine, and under Thee, and in Thy hand; it is lawful for Thee to determine concerning me whatsoever Thou wilt. Yet I know that Thou wilt to have mercy on me on account of Thy Son."* This response is now followed by another, *affirmative:*

> ***"If, however, God, willing to show wrath and to make His power known, has borne with great patience vessels of wrath fitted for destruction, and that He might make known the riches of His glory upon vessels of mercy which He has prepared unto glory"*** (— *who indeed can complain?*)

That is, *no one can complain of God as if He were* ***προσωπολήπτης*** *(prosōpolēptēs,* a respecter of persons) *and the author of the destruction of many.* **For God has created no men for destruction,** but even though He owes them nothing (Matt. 20), He nevertheless creates them, cares for them, calls them, and wills them to be saved, and deals with them either from grace (if they obey the Word) or from wrath (if they become contumacious and do not obey the Word).⁷⁹

**God wills to show His wrath.** Therefore He Himself is not the cause of the hardening, sin, and destruction either of Pharaoh or of the other impious. For no one is angry at a deed of which he himself is the author. **God has borne the vessels of wrath with much long-suffering.** Therefore He has not *efficiently prepared* the vessels that they should be vessels of wrath. For if He *bore* them, He did not prepare them such, but prepared them *to obedience and life,* and patiently endured their degeneracy, contumacy, and improbity, and destroyed the unconverted — as in Jeremiah 18 it is shown that *contemners of the Word are in God's hand as clay in the potter's hand,* and *God promises to the converted mercy,* but *to the obstinate prepares destruction.* And the passage in Jeremiah adds much light to this Pauline response.

**Vessels of wrath fitted for destruction** are ***destructive organs, or plagues of the human race,*** whose *course multiplies-wounds* to the glory of God and to human society — as Nero, Arius, Julian. In Psalm 31, the Son of God, in His Passion made for us a *malediction,* so names Himself: *"I was made as a vessel of destruction"* — ***σκεῦος ἀπολωλός*** (*skeuos apolōlos*), ***כְּלִי הַסֵּד*** (**כלי-סד**) — *a vessel to be taken away and cast off.*

And as regards the *vessels of wrath being said to be prepared for destruction,* it is to be accepted *of the event,* and not of the counsel of God the Creator. For this phrase is frequent in Scripture, whereby to *a cause acting for another purpose* the *event* is attributed — as Hosea 8: *"Their gold and silver they made into idols, that they might perish"*; Micah 6: *"Thou hast walked in their pleasures, that I might give thee to perdition"*; 2 Kings 21: *"Manasseh multiplied idols, that he might do evil in the sight of the Lord and provoke Him"*; 1 Corinthians 1: *"Not many wise men according to the flesh, not many powerful, not many noble; but God chose the foolish things of the world."* — These examples speak *of the event.* And by this phrase God is said *to have created the impious for the evil day* (Prov. 16), namely, *by reason of the event.* **For God predestines no one to the evil of guilt, but many He ordains to the evil of punishment on account of the guilt admitted** — nay, all who are not converted. For concerning those who are converted, the will of God is immoveable: *"As I live, I will not the death of the sinner, but that he be converted and live."*

**If, therefore, we look to the counsel of creation, God has created no one for eternal destruction.** But if we look at the *event,* many are ordained for destruction. A pious father generates no son for the gallows, if the counsel of the one generating be regarded. But if by reason of the malice of the son the event does not answer to the vow of the parent, the father is said *to have generated the son for the gallows,* as the Germans speak: *"Er zeucht ein Galgenstrick"* — *"He is rearing a gallows-cord."* The *efficient cause,* therefore, *and the preparer of the vessels of wrath unto destruction, is the Devil and the proper will and hardness of the man not willing to hear God calling him through the Word to penitence, and not willing to believe the Gospel.*

**Vessels of mercy** are the ***salutary organs,*** through which God is efficacious and does saving things in the Church or in the polity — as Paul, Constantine the Great, Theodosius, Luther, Philip, and all pious doctors and governors, lovers of truth and of salutary association. These vessels are ***prepared by God unto glory,*** because they cannot by their own strengths and works prepare themselves unto glory. And these vessels are nowhere save in the *company of the called,* or *in the true Church gathered from Jews and Gentiles.*

**Let us all, therefore, pray the Son of God** that He make us vessels of mercy, and let Him not let us be or become *vessels of wrath,* ***ἀλάστορας*** (*alastoras,* Furies) *and plagues of the Church and of human society* — of which sort in these dregs and furies of the world there are far too many.

**Let the students also observe the *diversity of words.*** *Vessels of wrath* are said to be ***κατηρτισμένα εἰς ἀπώλειαν*** (*katērtismena eis apōleian*) — that is, *fitted unto destruction* — that is, *by themselves and by their own will apt for perdition,* and *disjointed,* and *from the Devil composed hither and thither, and compacted into one.* But the *vessels of mercy* God Himself ***προητοίμασεν εἰς δόξαν*** (*proētoimasen eis doxan*) — that is, *made rightly and prepared unto glory.*

These things briefly said on the passage in the ninth chapter to the Romans may suffice. Furthermore, in the *tenth* Paul defines the righteousness of faith and distinguishes it from the righteousness of works. In the *eleventh* he admonishes that no one be offended by the word ***predestination,*** but rather look upon and consider the *righteousness of faith* than those other things which are said about *hidden predestination.* The *fourth (last) part* of the Epistle from the twelfth to the end discourses on ***good works.*** In 12 the sum of ethical doctrine is proposed; in 13 the sum of political doctrine; in 14 the sum of ecclesiastical ceremonies; in 15 there is instituted a sermon *on mutual concord and patience;* in 16 are set forth *greetings and exhortations to constancy in the known truth.*

## §11. Arguments concerning Justification from the Epistle to the Galatians — Sixteen Rebuttals

**This Epistle is altogether the *epitome* of the Epistle to the Romans, defending the article of justification and confirming the righteousness of faith** — that man is not justified from works of the Law, but only through faith in Christ. And the sum of the doctrine on the justification of men before God is set forth in Paul's first sermon in Galatia at Antioch (Acts 13) in these words: *"Let it be known to you, brethren, that through Christ the remission of sins is announced to you: and from all things from which you could not be justified in the Law of Moses, in Him everyone that believes is justified."* This doctrine Paul confirms in this Epistle with various arguments at the end of the second chapter and throughout the third.

**Argument I.** *The justice before God by which we are just and are received into grace by God is given either on account of the Law — that is, our own works — or on account of Christ freely, who so loved us that He delivered Himself for us.* But on account of the Law or our own works — congruent with the Law of God — we are not received into grace, for two causes: **first,** because in this infirmity of human nature none there is who satisfies the Law of God; **second,** because Christ would have died in vain, if we could obtain justice by our own dignity or works. Therefore we are justified freely and have remission of sins on account of Christ alone. The consequence holds, because there is no middle between the justice of the Law and the justice imputed to us on account of Christ. For every rational creature pleases God either on account of its own *integrity, in which it was created,* and *conformity with the Law,* or *on account of the Mediator Christ.*

**Argument II** — from the destruction of the consequent to the destruction of the antecedent: *If through the Law or our obedience toward the Law we were made just, then we should not need the death and merit of Christ; Christ would have died and suffered for us in vain. But Christ has not died in vain. Therefore not from works of the Law, but through faith in Christ, we are justified.*

**Argument III.** *Christ is not a minister of sin. But He would be a minister of sin, if we ought to be justified by the Law, which always accuses us on account of sin, and sets us as guilty in the judgement of God. Therefore we are not justified by works.* And he who from the Law or from works wishes to be justified, does as though *Christ by His merit made us first sinners,* and *subjected us to the Law, to be accused and damned.* This is to ***crucify Christ afresh*** and to *build up again sin* which had been destroyed by the preaching of faith.

**The following arguments are in the third chapter.**

**Argument IV.** *The Holy Spirit is given from the hearing of faith* — that is, *not on account of the works of the Law, but on account of Christ,* whom by faith according to the hearing of the Gospel we embrace, and at the same time receive the Holy Spirit, whose donation is always conjoined with remission of sins. Therefore justice does not come to us from works of the Law. The antecedent is confirmed by the experience of all the pious. The consequence holds *from the nature of the connected:* for if the principal *appendix* of the benefit is not given on account of our works and merits, much less the *benefit itself* — the principal good, which is *grace and remission of sins* — is given on account of the works of the Law.

**Argument V** — from *genus,* or the example of Abraham. Same as the first argument Paul recites in Rom. 4. *As Abraham is justified and saved, so all men are justified and saved.* The universal rule admits no exception.

**Argument VI.** *Blessing or blessedness is promised to Abraham and to all the Gentiles, not on account of the Law, of circumcision, or of works, but on account of the Seed to come, Christ. Therefore justification is not from works.*

**Argument VII.** *The promise sets forth blessing; but the Law overwhelms us with its malediction. Therefore justice or blessing is not from the Law. And those who from the Law seek justice are under malediction* (Deut. 27: *"Cursed is every one,"* etc.).

**Argument VIII.** *As* ***Law*** *and* ***work*** *— so* ***promise*** *and* ***faith*** *have themselves correlatively. As the Law requires works, so the promise demands faith. Therefore faith cannot lean on the Law, but looks to the gratuitous promise of the Gospel and rests in it.*

**Argument IX.** *"The just shall live by his faith"* (Hab. 2). *"The Law is not of faith."* Therefore *not by the Law but by faith* is life obtained. The consequence holds from *effects.* For the effect of faith is *life and salvation.* The effect of the Law is *malediction and damnation,* as it is wont to be said: *"The Law is the ministry of death and damnation."* Therefore not from the Law but from faith are we just.

**Argument X.** *Justice before God and eternal life are connected. Now men are made heirs of eternal life not on account of the Law, but by faith embracing the promise. Therefore by the same reason also they are made just.*

**Argument XI.** *Justification is the liberation from the malediction of the Law. But Christ alone frees us from that malediction. Therefore only on account of Christ, through faith, we are justified — not from the Law or from works.*

**Argument XII.** *Christ was made a malediction for us, that we might obtain blessing. Therefore only through Christ we obtain blessing.*

**Argument XIII.** *To the testament or the promise of God nothing must be added, and nothing subtracted. But the promise of God plainly says: "In the Seed," not "in seeds." Therefore on account of the one Seed, Christ, and not on account of seeds — that is, not partly on account of Christ, partly on account of works of the Law — we obtain blessing (that is, remission of sins, righteousness, and eternal life).*

**Argument XIV.** *Whatever is given from debt is not given freely through a promise accepted by faith. But the inheritance of righteousness and eternal life is given freely through a promise accepted by faith. Therefore it is not given on account of works of the Law.*

**Argument XV.** *If the Law brought consolation and life, then truly justice would be from the Law. But the Law neither brings consolation nor life. Therefore justice is not from the Law.*

**Argument XVI.** *The Law is a pedagogue, by teaching and by chastising, and shows *that it cannot pronounce us just and absolve from sin.* Therefore we need another Master and Teacher* — namely, Christ, to whom the Law is a *pedagogue,* by that very fact because it cannot make us just.

**These are the principal arguments which Paul uses to the Romans and to the Galatians,** which we thought worthwhile briefly to recite here. Now something must also be said concerning **Faith.**

## §12. What is Faith

**Definition:**

> ***Faith is a firm and certain knowledge of the divine benevolence toward us, freely exhibited through Christ, supported by the truth of the divine promises, and certainly determining that sins are remitted, and that we ourselves are received by God unto righteousness and eternal life on account of Christ the Mediator, freely, not on account of our own dignity.***⁸⁰

Hebrews 11 defines faith thus: *"Faith is the* ***ὑπόστασις*** *(hypostasis) of things hoped for, and the* ***ἔλεγχος*** *(elenchos) of things not appearing."* And ***ὑπόστασις*** is named *certain and firm confidence, and expectation of the promised goods without any doubting;* and ***ἔλεγχος*** is *certain notice and a firm proof and argumentation,* which some render ***conviction*** — by which, namely, the mind is convinced by the proper and immoveable testimonies of the divine voice, so that by concluding it may have a firm certitude such as plainly is that of things which are apprehended by the eyes, hands, and all the senses. Of this conclusion Paul speaks in Romans 3: **λογιζόμεθα** — *"we conclude,"* or, *"we prove that a man is justified by faith without the works of the Law."*

The word ***πίστις*** (*pistis*) is derived — as they are wont — from **πείθω** (*peithō,* to persuade), **πέπεισμαι** (*I have been persuaded*), ***πέπισται*** (*it has been persuaded*). And ***πείθω*** is from the Hebrew ***פּוֹתָב*** (*Phōthāb*), *"he persuaded by words."* Nor does the Latin word ***fides*** differ much.

## §13. What are the Causes of Faith

**In John 6 it is written: *"This is the work of God, that ye believe in Him whom He sent."*** He calls faith the *work of God,* not only because it is *mandated by God,* but because ***the Holy Spirit through the Word or through the promise of the Gospel heard and considered kindles, confirms, and preserves faith*** in the mind and heart. For *"faith is from the hearing"* (Rom. 10); and *"it is the gift of God"* (Eph. 2). And it is not only some general or common gift, such as often in common life is health of body, riches, success in things to be done — as Aeschylus says: **Θεοῦ δὲ δῶρόν ἐστιν εὐτυχεῖν βροτούς** (*"of God the gift is that mortals prosper"*) — but is a *peculiar gift, proper to the sons of God,* to every human reason unknown, made manifest and given to our minds through the Holy Spirit.

**The causes of faith, therefore, are: the doctrine of the Gospel heard, and the Holy Spirit illuminating the mind to *assenting,* and impelling the will to *acquiescing* in the promise of the Gospel.** And by reason of order and subject there accedes also the will itself, as will be shown a little later.

**As to the question whether any part of faith is in our own power,** let us hold to the saying of Paul (2 Cor. 3): *"We are not sufficient of ourselves to think anything as of ourselves, but our sufficiency is of God."* Paul does not universally take away from the human mind the faculty of thinking or ratiocinating, but attributes the *saving cogitation and efficacy to God alone.* And Christ says in John 6: *"No one comes to Me unless My Father draw him. And every one that hears from the Father and learns, comes to Me."* By these words Christ shows the *efficacy of God* to be, and at the same time denies coercion, when He says *"who hears and learns."* **Augustine:** *"See how the Father draws — by teaching He delights, not by imposing necessity. Learn to be drawn to the Son by the Father: let the Father teach thee, hear His word."*

**But whether the will can absolutely and simply be said to be some cause of faith and of conversion,** it is certain — if we look to the goodness and will of God — that that phrase cannot conveniently and without peril be admitted, especially since the word *cause* engenders ambiguity, and its use is too wide, and the will is *a thing to be converted, not a thing converting.* For God prevents and anticipates us in all things by His goodness and will, purpose, good pleasure, and ***φιλανθρωπία*** (*philanthrōpia,* love-of-mankind), so that no *preparation* for grace can be constituted in us, and no *upright or fit will* preceding conversion. For so long every work which proceeds from us displeases God, until He Himself shall have conferred on us grace and faith — which grace and faith are the *gift of God.*

**Even though the will, in the very act and operation of the Holy Spirit kindling faith in a man hearing the word and promise of the Gospel, is not entirely destroyed, dead, and utterly idle,** but is *illuminated, changed, and moved by the Holy Spirit,* — nevertheless all pious minds who are versed in exercises of faith, invocation, and penitence not only in speculation but in practice, willingly and openly confess from experience *that by their own will and freedom they neither prepare themselves for grace and faith, nor rightly hear the Word of God, much less assent to the promise, nor are efficacious in the least of what pertains to true faith, conversion, righteousness, and salvation, nor can merit anything;* but that as regards *preparation, right assent, efficacy, and merit* they are wholly a *patient subject,* — not driven or coerced by force (as the artisan cuts and forms a trunk and a stone by great force), not seized in enthusiasmos, not driven by staves and horrid clamour (as the miller drives an ass), not drawn by impetus (as wood is drawn), not converted in the whole of their substance and altogether transformed (for to say this would be repugnant to the will of God, to the rational creature, and to the experience of all pious) — but, ***by the mercy and goodness of God called, accepted, illuminated, drawn, adorned, clothed, renewed, bent, and moved.***

**While God calls, it is necessary that we hear Him and by His guidance come to Him.** While He accepts and receives us, it is necessary that we commit ourselves to Him, extend our hands, and give ourselves whole to Him — as David says: *"Deal with Thy servant according to Thy mercy."* While He adorns, clothes, and renews us, and bends and moves us, it is necessary that we allow ourselves to be clothed, not obstinately resist the Holy Spirit, nor cast off and defile the garment. **Of God alone, therefore, is the efficacy. We are only matter — yet not destitute of some *intellect and will* in things subject to reason** — but ***rational matter,*** and created to the image of God, and now to be reformed to the same image — intelligent indeed, often willing carnally by ourselves and from our own nature, and even sometimes assenting to divine things, but *rightly understanding, rightly and spiritually willing, and rightly and firmly assenting to the promise* — only from the *grace of God and the operation of the Holy Spirit,* who finds us resisting, and as soon as He begins to operate and move our mind and will, acts in us no longer *resisting and contumacious* (for otherwise He would not act), but *hearing, availing, and obeying.*

**Concerning the word *συνεργόν* (*synergon,* co-working) there are various disputes, and not always necessary.**⁸¹ It stands from Paul (1 Cor. 1; 2 Cor. 6) that our diligence and fidelity are so named, both in the ministry and in the zeal of true piety. And from Aristotle it stands that ***συνεργόν*** is named all that which is by itself imperfect and effects nothing, but when something else acting is added, is not idle. Thus, therefore, for the sake of teaching, our teachers have named ***the human mind assenting to the Word of God by the guidance and efficacy of the Holy Spirit, and the will struggling with doubts and diffidence and acquiescing in the divine promise.*** Never in the Churches sounding forth the true doctrine of the Gospel was the word ***συνεργεία*** understood *of efficacy, or merit, or powers of a man destitute of the Holy Spirit* — but only of the *order and mode of action* by which the Holy Spirit acts in the human mind and will as in a matter not brute or altogether inept to every motion.

For it is certain that the will is a *thing to be converted, not a thing converting.* And the sounder doctors of the Church (for we are not speaking of others) willed by this very word to *excite the zeal of piety and invocation,* lest we indulge doubt, diffidence, security, and other perverse affects and actions, or feign enthusiasmos — as the Anabaptists and Schwenckfeldians have done, and as all those still do who feign the regenerate will to be altogether idle.

**Sum:** God offers and gives His goods, and receives us into grace, has mercy on us, remits sins, and grants all celestial goods freely, only on account of the Son. He also kindles faith in us through the Holy Spirit. And in us is the copious mercy of God (says Clement), we who *contribute nothing to God — neither in our essence, nor in our nature, nor by our own essential will* — but this only, that we are *the work of His will and brethren of Christ.* **Ours is: to *accept* what is given, to *hear* Him who calls, to *follow* the leader, to *acknowledge* the Father, and to *pray* that He may guard and preserve us** — which we do not from ourselves, but under the leadership and efficacy of the Holy Spirit.

**But to admonish also concerning this: some object that when *συνεργόν* is named, it is understood certainly a *cause,* and thence, as they say, it follows that the will is called a cause of conversion — some cause at least.** — I answer. **It is true that ***συνεργόν*** is a cause — call it *instrumental,* or even *material,* though less properly.** But a *true cause* it is not. Therefore I have said above that the will cannot conveniently be said *absolutely and simply* to be a cause of conversion, because *the true* ***αἴτιον*** (*aition,* cause) *is the Holy Spirit;* ***συναίτιον*** (*co-cause*) is the Word or the promise of the Gospel; ***συνεργόν*** is the *subject* into which the Holy Spirit acts — the human mind and will, not idle, but though of its nature it *resists* the divine motion and is *found by the Holy Spirit* to be perverse and averse from God, nevertheless when *reborn by the Holy Spirit through the Word,* it conceives as it were *new qualities of its own nature,* and lets itself be bent, and assents to the Word, and represses diffidence and doubts, and acquiesces in the divine promise; and as often as it feels in itself the *prior and older qualities of its nature,* it conquers them by struggling, by praying, and by imploring, and by consequently obtaining the aid and firmness of the Holy Spirit. Nor yet can the *human will be called a cause of conversion,* since the *thing to be converted is not the thing converting;* but when *συνεργόν* is named, nothing else is shown save that ***the reborn and already-converted will is not idle.***

**These things thus expounded and thus taught are true and pleasing to God.** It is also rightly said: *the Holy Spirit does not convert a stone or a trunk, or an ox or an ass, but a man endowed with reason from God and created to the image of God, who both can and ought to hear, think, and understand.* And though man by his nature after the Fall has been made an *enemy of God,* yet, as an *enemy endowed with reason,* he hears the *reconciliation offered to him by the adverse party* (whose enemy he is), understands the *efficacy of the Holy Spirit,* receives it, and lets himself be moved and bent to peace, and becomes a friend: so man, hearing the promise of the Gospel and seeing the ambassador of God offering grace and peace — that is, hearing the Word — feels the motion of the Holy Spirit in his heart, does not repel or reject the offered benefits, but *kindled with gladness* subjects himself to the divine voice and motion, as Paul says: *"Lord, what wilt Thou that I do?"*

**That these things are true, a man experiences and confesses in the exercises of penitence.** No *efficacy,* no *merit* — nay, no *preparation, no capacity, no aptitude,* or *fit habit* — other than that by which man is *distinguished from the irrational brutes and adorned by God with intellect, mind, and will* — we attribute to the powers of man. No efficacious or sufficient action, or (as **Sadoletus** named it) the *oarage of virtue,* or right or firm assent, do we ascribe to the not-reborn human will: rather we recognise, confess, and deplore that ***man is an enemy of God*** (as Paul speaks), endowed with reason indeed, but abusing it against the glory of God; and yet, ***when he hears the divine promise and offered grace,*** letting himself be converted — and that *not by his own nature, but by the virtue, efficacy, and motion of Him who offers grace,* — of Him who both with the *Word* and with an *interior motion* persuades him to assent and acquiesce in the divine promise.

**A man, therefore, is converted — not as an enemy persevering in contumacy and extreme hostility, but as an *enemy placable and letting himself be persuaded and bent to friendship,* and yet *knowing* that that friendship has been made *without his own cogitations, counsels, zeals, and actions,* and referring the whole of what happens in the conversion to the *sole mercy of God, sole merit of the Son of God, sole benefit and motion of the Holy Spirit* offering through the Word the grace of God, and kindling faith and true consolation in the mind and heart of the man.**

**Let these things briefly said on the *causes of faith* suffice.** And there remains for us this firm foundation: that the ***merit of Christ*** is the *first, middle, and last cause* in conversion, justification, and salvation. Our will is required only *by reason of order and subject,* that it be there and assent, drawn by the Holy Spirit through the Word — and thus conversion happens. Let us conclude this doctrine with the sweetest little verses of D. Philip:⁸²

> ***Nil sum, nulla miser novi solatia, massam***
> ***Humanam, nisi quod tu quoque Christe geris.***
> ***Tu me sustenta, fragilem me Christe guberna.***
> ***Fac ut sim massae portio parva tuae.***
> ***Hoc mirum foedus semper mens cogitet, uno***
> ***Hoc est, non aliter, foedere parta salus.***
>
> *(I am nothing; miserable, I know no consolations, — the human*
> *mass — save that Thou too, O Christ, dost bear it.*
> *Sustain me; govern me, fragile, O Christ.*
> *Make me a little portion of Thy mass.*
> *This wondrous covenant let the mind ever ponder:*
> *by this covenant alone, not otherwise, has salvation been procured.)*

## §14. What are the Objects of Faith

**The object of faith is the Word of God setting forth to us the promise of divine favour and of remission of sins on account of Christ the Mediator.** It is usual to enumerate ***two-fold objects*** in this very Word of God: some *external,* others *spiritual.* **External** are those in which *corporeal goods* are promised — posterity, long life, good health, riches and possessions, and certain liberations from calamities and dangers. **Spiritual** are those in which *celestial and eternal goods* are promised — remission of sins, justice, salvation, and eternal life on account of Christ. Now those external objects cannot have any force, nor please God, nor be apprehended by true faith and rightly used (although sometimes *general faith* is concerned only with external objects, as we shall say below), *unless the spiritual object of faith precedes,* which is the *promise of the remission of sins and of divine favour on account of Christ,* or God Himself *propitious according to the promise.* Wherefore Paul alleges from Genesis the testimonies concerning faith concerning Abraham, in which appears that though the *external businesses* be dissimilar, faith always looks to *one first and principal object,* namely, ***God propitious according to the promise on account of Christ the Mediator.***

## §15. Why Justice is ascribed to Faith

**It is certain that when we say we are *justified by faith,* we do not understand the *work of faith* or any *quality and action of our powers,* but — in the Pauline manner — the word *faith* is used *relatively,* and *the confidence of the Son the Mediator* is signified.** That is: *we are just not on account of the dignity of a quality, but on account of the Son of God, whom faith acknowledges, looks upon, apprehends, and rests in* — and as it were, *a ring* embraces a precious gem, so does faith embrace Christ. For **Christ is the *entelechia* of faith** (**ἐντελέχεια**), and *faith apprehends Christ.* Rightly therefore it is said: *We are justified not on account of our believing, but on account of the object* — that is, ***through the promised mercy we are just.*** And **faith is only the *instrument* in us by which we apprehend the offered and given grace.**

**From this admonition it can easily be answered to three arguments:**

> *"By faith we are just.*
> *Faith is a work.*
> *Therefore on account of works we are just."*

**The major is to be understood *correlatively.*** *By faith we are just — not by the quality of faith, but by the relation to Him whom faith apprehends and whose righteousness it applies to itself.* For by reason of the *work,* faith avails nothing; but by reason of the *foundation and object which faith looks upon and apprehends,* faith is all — as in the little verses of **Spalatinus** written to Eobanus it is rightly said:⁸³

> ***Sola fides hominem caelesti donat honore,***
> ***Sola fides superis conciliare potest.***
> ***Sola fides hominem peccato liberat omni,***
> ***Sola fides mortem, duraque fata premit.***
> ***Sola fides Christi fratres facit, atque potentes***
> ***Efficit, et caelis inserit una fides.***
> ***Sola fides animos tetrico maerore iacentes***
> ***Erigit, et miseris pectora laeta facit.***
> ***Sola fides Satanam subigit stygiasque paludes,***
> ***Clavibus his solis sidera summa patent.***
> ***Sola fides vivis, morientibus omnia donat,***
> ***Sola fides regit, ac omnia sola potest.***
>
> *(Faith alone gives the man celestial honour;*
> *faith alone can reconcile him to the heavens.*
> *Faith alone frees the man from every sin;*
> *faith alone treads down death and the hard fates.*
> *Faith alone makes brothers of Christ, and makes powerful,*
> *and one faith alone inserts into the heavens.*
> *Faith alone raises up minds lying in dark grief,*
> *and makes glad the hearts of the wretched.*
> *Faith alone subjugates Satan and the Stygian marshes;*
> *by these keys alone do the highest stars stand open.*
> *Faith alone gives all things to the living and the dying;*
> *faith alone rules, and faith alone can do all things.)*

And in the German hymn most sweetly we sing: ***Der Glaub sicht Jesum Christum an*** — *"Faith looks upon Jesus Christ,"* who has satisfied for us and been made Mediator.

**Another objection:**

> *"That whereby (medium quo) is not the terminus toward which.*
> *Faith is a medium-whereby, or is an instrument.*
> *Therefore it is not the terminus toward which — that is, we are not justified by faith."*

**I concede the whole of the *quality* and *work* of faith. For faith absolutely is not our justice, but *by faith we are justified* ***relatively,*** and faith is the *medium* or *instrument* by which we come to the *terminus and scope,* and apprehend the righteousness of the Son of God and apply it to us.**

**Another:**

> *"By faith we are just.*
> *Therefore not freely."*

**Answer.** ***Freely*** excludes the condition of our work and merit; it does not exclude *faith.* And when Paul says that the *promise is gratuitous,* he does not wish to say that it is not to be accepted by faith. For God wills us to *embrace the promise by faith,* and He does not bestow remission of sins upon one resisting.

## §16. The Four Causes for the Exclusive *Sola Fide*

**In the Church of God it is rightly and by common use said that a man is justified before God *by faith alone*** — or, as Paul is wont to speak, ***freely*** — that is, not on account of our dignity, not on account of our merits, or virtues, or deeds, but ***on account of the Son of God the Mediator that reconciliation is given.*** And this little word ***freely*** or ***alone*** excludes from justification all our virtues and works and merits, *insofar as justification is concerned.* For our good works do not enter into the *circle,* as Luther speaks — the *article* or *act* of justification — but there ***only grace, only Christ, only faith, and only remission of sins reign.***

**There are four reasons why the exclusive *freely* or *sola fide* must be retained and defended:**⁸⁴

**First:** *that the due honour be rendered to Christ.*

**Second:** *that the conscience may have a firm and certain confidence and consolation,* as Paul manifestly says in Romans 4: *"Therefore from faith freely, that the promise may be firm."*

**Third:** *that invocation may be true and ardent, not Ethnic nor full of doubtings.*

**Fourth:** *that there be the evident distinction of Law and Gospel — which the little word "freely" or "alone" makes.*

Concerning these causes there is copious discussion in the *Loci communes* under the word ***Grace.***

**And let this also be observed:** if God justifies us by His goodness and mercy on account of the *obedience and merit of the Son* — as He justifies not otherwise — then **faith alone is the medium by which the righteousness of the Son of God can be given to us.** For all other works render obedience to God and give Him something which He wills. But ***faith alone*** *receives from God the benefits offered in the Son of God,* and by the present good — the remission of sins, peace with God, true consolation, and certain hope of future liberation and eternal gladness and life — is enjoyed. And there is no other work which is an *instrument* by which we can apprehend in the present the offered benefit of God (for *hope* awaits the future benefit) — save ***faith alone.*** Rightly, therefore, faith is as it were a ***Sacrament*** among the remaining works — which are eucharistic sacrifices, and give something to God, and render Him due obedience: *faith alone receives from God what He Himself gives through and on account of the Son.* And this work the Holy Spirit elects, that as it were by an *instrument and organ* He may apply to a man the righteousness and merit of Christ.

## §17. Testimonies from the Fathers that we are justified by Faith Alone

**Psalm 51:** *"Against Thee, Thee only have I sinned."* This confession of David shows to all the pious that we must lean on the mercy of God alone, which we apprehend by faith alone. For to whom alone the debt is owed, He alone remits the debt. And against whom alone one has sinned, He alone absolves from the sin.

**Psalm 71:** *"I shall go into the powers of the Lord; O Lord, I shall commemorate Thy righteousness, that alone which is Thine."*

**Luke 8:** ***μόνον πίστευε*** (*monon pisteue,* "only believe"). — ***μόνον πίστιν εἰσάγωμεν, καὶ πάντα δίδωσι*** — *"Let us bring only faith to God, and He will give us all things,"* says Chrysostom.⁸⁵

Hither pertains the word ***freely*** which Paul uses (***δωρεάν***, Rom. 3), excluding our merits. For plainly the sense is the same: *"We are justified freely by the redemption which is in Christ,"* and *"we are justified by faith alone,"* — because *on account of the Mediator we please by faith, not on account of our own dignity or newness.*

**Basil, *On Humility***: *"That is the perfect and integral glorying in God, when one is not exalted in his own righteousness, but knows that he lacks true righteousness, and that he is justified by faith alone in Christ."*⁸⁶

**Gregory Nazianzen, *De taxias***: *"Confess Jesus Christ, believe that He is risen from the dead, and thou shalt be saved. For justice is* ***τὸ πιστεῦσαι μόνον*** — *only to believe."*⁸⁷

**Photius, Patriarch of Sirmium, on Galatians, chapter 3**: *"All are shut up under sin: therefore they can obtain the promise from faith alone. For it suffices* ***τὸ πιστεῦσαι μόνον*** — *only to believe:* by baptism the bill of debt is abolished."⁸⁸

**Origen, Book 3, chapter 3, on Romans:** *"Where is thy glorying?"* — He says that the justification of faith alone suffices, so that *the believer is justified by that only, even if no work has been performed by him.* And: *"The only glorying of the just is in the faith of the cross of Christ."*⁸⁹

**Ambrose, chapter 3 on Romans:** *"Working nothing and returning no return, they are justified by faith alone by the gift of God."* And in chapter 4: *"It was decreed by God that when the Law ceased, faith alone should demand of the grace of God unto salvation."* And again: *"Paul calls those blessed of whom God has sanctioned this: that without labour and without any observance, by faith alone they are justified before God."* And in chapter 8: *"It is fitting that in the sole Name of the Lord the Creator should the creature obtain salvation, that is, through faith: because, all *neomenies,* sabbaths, circumcision, the law of foods and the offerings of cattle being removed, ***faith alone is placed unto salvation.***"* And in chapter 3: *"Only is it required that they believe, and nothing else."*⁹⁰

**Athanasius on Galatians 3:** *"Paul plainly demonstrates that faith itself, or alone, has in itself the power of justifying."*

**Hilary:** ***Fides sola iustificat*** — *"Faith alone justifies."*⁹¹

Let these testimonies of the *sounder doctors* suffice — to which the students may add more from their own readings. Let them also remember the verses of the ancient poet **Victor** on Abraham:⁹²

> ***Credidit, et nuda fidei consensio sola***
> ***Plenam ad iustitiam et meriti reputata coronam est.***
>
> *(He believed, and the bare consent of faith alone*
> *was reputed to him for a full crown of justice and merit.)*

## §18. Against the Tridentine *Fulmen* — the *Anathema Sit* Rebutted

**Against this doctrine of the Gospel stand the *thunderbolts of the Pontiffs,*** such as this one of the Council of Trent:⁹³

> ***"If any one shall say that the impious is justified by faith alone, let him be anathema."***

**But let us know that the exclusive *sola* in the doctrine of justification has not been devised by us,** but has been brought forth by the Holy Spirit with many ***equipollent*** words — such are: the words ***grace*** (Eph. 2: *"By grace ye are saved through faith, and this not of yourselves"*); ***freely*** (Rom. 3); ***χάρισμα*** *of God* (Rom. 6); ***δῶρον*** *of God* (Eph. 2); ***κεχάρισται*** — *"He has freely given"* (Gal. 3); likewise, ***one man Christ, and the righteousness of one, the obedience of one*** (Rom. 5); ***one oblation*** (Heb. 10); *without the Law, not through the Law, not from the Law* (Rom. 2; Gal. 2; 3); *without works* (Rom. 4:11); *"Not from works, but according to His mercy He saved us"* (Titus 3); *"Not according to our works, but according to His purpose and the grace which was given us in Christ"* (2 Tim. 1); *"Not to him that works, but to him that believes"* (Rom. 4); *"the imputation of righteousness not according to debt, but according to grace"*; *"remission of sins"* (Rom. 4); *"faith without works"* (Rom. 3); *"not the law of works but the law of faith"*; and Galatians 2: ***"A man is not justified from works of the Law, unless through faith"*** — that is, *save only through faith.* For the particle **ἐὰν μή** (*ean mē*) is often so used in the sense of ***ἀλλ' ἤ*** (*"but rather"*).

**All these *exclusives* are briefly and summarily expressed** in our Churches and Schools by the particle ***Alone*** — when we say that ***only by the mercy of God, only by faith, only by the imputation of the righteousness of Christ alone, we are made just before God.*** For thus we exclude the condition of our merit and dignity, and take away the cause of reconciliation from our works or virtues, and transfer it to the *sole grace of God receiving us on account of the merit of Christ the Mediator,* and at the same time show the ***medium or organ of application*** — which is ***faith,*** by which alone the gratuitous promise of reconciliation on account of Christ is apprehended, received, and applied.

**When, therefore, the Papists and others damn our exclusives with the thunderbolt of anathema, they reject not us or our Churches only, but the Holy Spirit thus speaking, the Apostle Paul, and the whole doctrine of the Gospel.**

Concerning the exclusive ***alone*** we have discussed more copiously in the little book whose title is ***Analectus.***⁹⁴ **The sum is this:** that ***by faith alone in Christ, without all the works of the whole Law, or even without the addition and presence of works as* συνέργων *(co-workers), and without any quality and virtue and merit of ours, we are justified and saved before God.*** For no work — whether antecedent, present, or consequent — contributes or does anything, even the least, unto justice and salvation. Faith alone is distinguished with this title, which, though it be *never alone,* is nevertheless *alone the instrument of the application of the grace and righteousness of Christ.* Therefore Luther splendidly says:⁹⁵

> ***"I know the other virtues to be splendid gifts of God; I know faith without these gifts does not exist; but our question is what is proper to each. Thou holdest in thy hand various seeds; I do not ask with what they are joined, but what is the proper virtue of each. Say plainly here what faith alone does, not with which virtues it is joined. Now faith alone apprehends the promise, believes God promising, extends the hand to God offering something, and receives it. This is the proper work of faith alone. Charity, hope, and patience have other materials about which they are concerned."***

And again: ***"We know that faith is never alone, but brings with itself charity and many other gifts, and leads with itself a most beautiful chorus of virtues, nor is it ever alone; but the thing is not therefore to be confounded, and what is of faith alone must not be attributed to other virtues."***

**Elsewhere Luther says:** ***"Faith, unless it be without any works — even the least — does not justify; indeed, it is not faith."*** This voice in the doctrine of justification — especially in the exercises of the penitent, of invocation, and in the spiritual struggle — is *without any exception found most true,* as the examples of David, Manasseh, the publican, the thief on the cross, and of all sinners show. In them, although faith is *never destitute* of good works — as acknowledgement of sin, confession, patience, humility and invocation, hope, and charity — nevertheless ***faith alone acquiesces in the sole mercy of God exhibited through and on account of the Son,*** and in this raises itself, consoles, sustains, and finds life and salvation.

## §19. On the Word *Gratia* — Two Significations · General vs Special Grace · The Pelagian History

**These things explained,** before we speak of the *corruptions of the adversaries* — those who feign doubt in the place of the ***πληροφορία*** (*plērophoria,* full assurance) of faith — we shall insert a brief admonition concerning the word ***Grace,*** which Scripture is wont to use *not in one way alone.*

**חֵן** (*Ḥēn*), **χάρις** (*charis*), *Gratia,* ***Gnad*** — signifies in the sacred letters:

**First,** *the benevolence and favour by which God vouchsafes to embrace unto Himself men in nothing meriting such, and to do them manifold good on account of the Son* — as John 1: *"Grace and truth through Jesus Christ";* Genesis 6: *"Noah found grace in the eyes of God"*; Luke 1: *"Thou hast found grace with God."* This grace elsewhere is named *reconciliation,* and the *gratuitous remission of sins on account of the Son given to believers.* And in this signification Paul uses the word *grace* when he proves that *we are justified freely by the grace of God.* For here the *favour of God, benevolence, φιλανθρωπία, εὐδοκία (eudokia, good pleasure), mercy, goodness, gratuitous acceptance, benignity, charity of God toward us,* or the *gratuitous love of God which chooses, calls, justifies, and adopts,* — are synonyms, and are equipollent to the word *Grace,* and show:

**(1)** *that the cause or merit by which we are justified before God unto eternal life is not, and does not inhere in, us;*
**(2)** *that God finds in us very many causes on account of which He could damn us;*
**(3)** *that, though unworthy and having merited far otherwise, we are received into grace by God from mere goodness and mercy on account of the Son, and are accepted unto eternal life.*

**Second,** the word *grace* is often used for the ***gift*** by which God then and there adorns the man whom He has already vouchsafed with His favour, or grace and mercy on account of the Son. And lest in the article of justification there be doubt about the true signification of *grace,* Paul plainly in Romans 5 distinguishes ***grace*** from ***gift***: so that by *the grace of God* he understands *the favour and benevolence of God toward us, or the gratuitous acceptance and reconciliation which is on account of the Son of God;* and by *the gift* the ***donation of the Holy Spirit,*** or the *singular gift and good which God from the ratio of that first benevolence confers* — namely, *newness of life, virtues, and good works, constancy, patience, chastity,* and the *other gifts,* of which Ephesians 4 says: *"To each one of us has grace been given according to the measure of the donation of Christ"* — that is, *by a geometric analogy, distributing to each what is congruent to his vocation.*⁹⁶

**And although each of these two — grace and gift — is a benefit of the Son of God the Mediator,** nevertheless when Scripture says that *we are justified and saved by grace,* it understands that grace which is *distinguished from the gift* — that is, from the second signification of grace — that is, it understands *not our newness or good works, not the gifts and fruits of the Holy Spirit, nor anything inherent in us, but the mercy or gratuitous acceptance which is on account of the Son.*

**Third,** the word *grace* is used for some *effect* either toward God or toward the neighbour — as when a man believing and regenerate gives *thanks* to God for the benefits received (whether for grace or for gift); likewise when one of the men *gratifies* another, serves him, does good to him, *renders grace,* as in Genesis 18: *"If I have found grace in Thy eyes"*; Acts 25: *"Festus, wishing to lay down grace to the Jews."*

**Let these things be said of the significations of the word grace. Now we shall consider the usual division of grace transmitted from Augustine.**⁹⁷ *Grace is either general or special.* **General** is that with which God affects all men without exception with benefits — as that He created all men, preserves them all, rains upon evil and good, gives food to all flesh. Without this grace no one can live, or move himself, or breathe (Matt. 5; Acts 14). But this is by no means the grace which is considered in the business of justification — as the Jesuits today pervert the state of this doctrine on the justifying grace of God. **Special grace** is that which God uses only toward those whose sins He remits, and whom He adorns not only with common benefits but with the *righteousness of His Son and with spiritual gifts,* and leads to eternal life. This is that grace of which all become partakers who believe in Christ and are justified by faith.

**Pelagius, around the year of Christ 400,** was convicted that he must confess *men to be called and justified and aided by the grace of God to live piously and holily.* By these insidious words he drew many into his opinion, that they might think he spoke and sensed rightly. But afterwards he cunningly interpreted himself as speaking only of *general grace,* just as today the Jesuits do the same. When, however, in the disputations of the pious he was driven to the point of saying that not only general grace was to be understood, but also *special,* he used another cunning and said that the ***vocation to the Kingdom of Christ and the gratuitous acceptance unto justice*** is indeed *from the grace of God,* but that the *other things* — namely, that men should live piously and blamelessly, fulfil the Law, and reach eternal life — are *without special grace,* and are done by the *free will and powers of the human choice alone.*⁹⁸

**This unbridled audacity, that the pious doctors might express it,** — relying on the suffrages of Holy Scripture — have transmitted a ***subdivision of special grace*** in this manner: ***The special grace of God is either* First *or* Second.*** The **First** is that by which one is first called by God, illumined by faith, and made from unjust just, and accepted by God in all things. The **Second** is that which God gives, that those who have once been justified may henceforth live justly and piously. For without the *grace of God — not only general, but also special, and not only first but also second* — a man can do nothing right, nothing pious, nothing accepted, to God — according to that saying of Philippians 2: *"It is God who works in you both to will and to effect according to His good pleasure";* and John 15: *"Without Me ye can do nothing"* — that is, *unless I be acknowledged and invoked, and on My account be received remission of sins and reconciliation* (for this is the *first special grace*) *and the donation of the Holy Spirit ruling and helping you* (this is *second grace*), *nothing pleasing to God, nothing salutary can be done.*

**From this Augustinian division that Scholastic distinction departs somewhat,** which says that *grace is either* ***gratum faciens*** (*grace making-pleasing*) or ***gratis data*** (*grace given-freely*). **Grace *making-pleasing*** is that which Augustine names *first special grace.* Others understand *charity itself,* though in various sense: some *the charity of God loving us;* some *our charity, and other virtues in us.* **Grace *gratis data*** — although it may conveniently be referred to the *second special grace* — was used by the Scholastic writers only of *some special gift* given divinely to a man for the utility of himself or of others — as *excellent eloquence, the power of doing signs,* and so on. Hence they usually said that even in the impious there may be ***gratia gratis data,*** although *gratia gratum faciens* be not in them. But these things are said less properly. For if we rightly observe, ***every grace can rightly be called grace gratis data,*** as Augustine defines: *grace is that which is given freely by God, and not from works.*

**And this distinction is also to be retained,** where Augustine says that grace is either ***operating*** or ***co-operating.*** **Operating grace** is that by which *God prevents, instructs, and prepares the will to will the good* — namely, by that ratio and in that order which He Himself expressed in His Word. **Co-operating grace** is that by which *God helps and perfects, that we will not in vain, but perform the good works of piety, and are ruled by the Spirit of God in our actions.* In the same manner, that grace is called ***preventing, preparing, freeing, operating;*** and another is called ***subsequent,*** which is the same as *co-operating* or *aiding perseverance in the good.* For thus Augustine speaks when he says: ***"God prevents the unwilling that he may will; He follows the willing that he may not will in vain."***⁹⁹

**These distinctions we ought to consider,** that we may know how, according to Scripture, Augustine and the other *sincere* doctors have of old used the word *grace,* and that we may utterly repudiate the *Scholastic depravation* concerning *the merits of men and the powers of free choice in the business of justification and salvation.*

## §20. Against the Doubting of the Monks — on the Grace of God and the Remission of Sins

**Often it is said that *faith* in the Church** — which faith is *most properly named justifying faith* — is the ***organ or instrument*** by which, in the doctrine of the Gospel, we seek, apprehend, receive, and apply to ourselves *the mercy of God pardoning us our sins and accepting us on account of the Son* — certainly determining that on account of the *obedience, satisfaction, and merit of the Son of God* we are in grace and have remission of sins, are justified, and shall eternally be saved. And this faith, lest any think it to be some *imagination of a man* or some *uncertain opinion, cogitation, or persuasion,* — but that he may know it to be a ***notice, an assent, and a certain and infallible confidence,*** — has a *true, certain, firm, immoveable, peculiar, principal, and proper object* — which is the *divine promise of the Gospel proper concerning the gratuitous mercy of God on account of the Son the Mediator remitting sins, adopting and accepting us who believe unto eternal life.*

**And to the certainty of this faith and to the confirmation of its object pertain all the sayings** which either speak of the *mercy, love, grace, φιλανθρωπία, and gratuitous gifts of God the Father,* or of the *office and obedience of the Mediator,* or of the *benefits of the Holy Spirit sealing faith in our hearts,* or of the *faith itself* by which we accept and have the benefits of the Son of God.

**And although there are also other objects of faith** — and faith *in general* is concerned with all those things which have been divinely made manifest in the Word of God (as in Hebrews 11 various objects of faith are described) — nevertheless, of *faith not considered in general* but *in species* — that is, of *justifying faith* — there is only ***one object,*** into which it looks *properly and principally,* when it wills to receive remission of sins, reconciliation, and adoption, and to render a man certain that he is in the grace of God, a son and heir of God.

**Against this certitude of faith the Papists, the Monks, and the like oppose themselves principally with three reasons:**⁹⁹ᵃ
**1.** They *deny* that there is only one object of faith, namely the *promise of gratuitous mercy on account of Christ the Mediator.*
**2.** They *deny* that faith signifies a *certain and firm confidence.*
**3.** They *affirm* that we must *doubt concerning remission of sins.*

**To the first reason there is a simple and true response.** *We do not inquire what the external objects of faith are in general, or what the exercises of faith are in the cross and in obedience* (for in these various *external* objects of faith considered in general can be); *but we inquire what is the object, by whose respect and apprehension faith before God justifies unto eternal life.* And this **one object is the promise of the gratuitous mercy on account of Christ the Mediator, on account of whom God is placated, reconciled, and propitious to us.** This object of *justifying faith* makes that afterwards the exercise of faith is *firm and stable also about other objects,* according to 2 Corinthians 1: *"For as many as are the promises of God, they are 'yea' and 'Amen' in Christ to the glory of God through us"* — that is, *the fulfilment of the promises about whatsoever objects or things is certain when by faith in Christ we are reconciled with God,* as Abraham is certain concerning his posterity according to the promise, since he knows God to be placated and propitious to him on account of the Son.

**This too must be added:** it often happens that *the general faith of some is concerned only with external objects,* though they lack *justifying faith* and do not look upon the principal true object of faith — as Hebrews 11 has it written: *"By faith they passed through the Red Sea."* Yet *in all of these the Lord was not pleased* (1 Cor. 10). So the ten lepers apprehended by faith the object of external healing, but *only one had justifying faith* — to whom Christ said: *"Thy faith has made thee safe"* (Luke 17). And often it also happens that one by true faith looks upon the *proper and principal object,* though he lack the remaining external objects — as the thief on the cross, and Job saying: *"Even if the Lord shall have killed me"* — that is, *though I have no external object or defence, no medium in this world, still I shall hope in the Lord* (Job 13). And Psalm 72: *"Although neither heaven nor earth remain to me, and my flesh and my heart fail, yet Thou art the God of my heart and my portion, God, forever."*

**To the second what pertains is plainly frivolous and impudent.** For ***faith is* ὑπόστασις** (Heb. 11), which word without all controversy in that place signifies ***confidence certainly apprehending or expecting something,*** in which manner Paul also elsewhere uses that word — as Hebrews 3: *"We are made partakers of Christ, if we hold fast the beginning of the* ὑποστάσεως *(hypostaseōs) unto the end"* — that is, if we retain *faith* — as Chrysostom also explains: ***τὴν πίστιν, δι᾽ ἧς ὑπέστημεν*** (*"the faith by which we subsist"*). And 2 Corinthians 9: he names ***ὑπόστασιν τῆς καυχήσεως*** (*hypostasin tēs kauchēseōs,* "the substance of glorying"), just as 2 Corinthians 11: ***boasting-confidence.*** So Polybius, Book 6, on Horatius standing against all the forces of the enemies says: *"The enemies feared his* ***ὑπόστασιν*** *more than his* ***δύναμιν*** *— that is, his confidence and courageous spirit more than his strength and power."* **Wisdom 16:** ***ἡ ὑπόστασις σου*** — *"Thy* faith *in Thee shows Thy sweetness."* The Germans speak in the same manner: ***Sich etwas unterstehen*** — *to subsist* — that is, *not only to try, but with great confidence to undertake.* Others also interpret ***ὑπόστασις*** as ***promise,*** *the* ***ἀπόχεσις*** *(apochesis, holding-fast) of our heart and spirit drawn from the Word* — that is, *certain confidence and pollicitation* that God is propitious to us on account of the Son. Hither pertains James 1: *"He that prays, let him ask in faith, doubting nothing"* — **μηδὲν διακρινόμενος** — *doubting or wavering nothing.* And Isaiah 26 says that ***faith is a firm cogitation which decrees that God gives peace.*** For thus he says: *"Open the gates* (the ministry), *and the just people shall enter, keeping faith* — ***a supported cogitation*** *(that is, firm and immoveable, namely, that) Thou wilt keep peace, because it is hoped in Thee."* **יֵצֶר סָמוּךְ** (*Yēṣer sāmûk*) — *a firm affect, a form and immoveable cogitation,* ***ποίημα*** *of the Holy Spirit,* a new ***πλάσμα*** or *shape,* not such as was before, *rotten and perverse* (Gen. 6 and 8: *Yēṣer,* that is, *the figment of the human heart is evil at every time from its childhood*), — but *new and firm, sealed, and* ***samach*** — *that* (to add the most sweet version of D. Forster) *a cogitation which leans forth shall obtain peace, or a sustained cogitation.* For *sāmak* is *to sustain,* or *to sustain from above,* lest we perish; whence in the Scriptures we name God ***סָמָךְ*** *(Sāmāk)* — *our sustainer.* And such is ***true faith:*** *(1)* the gift of God; *(2)* certain confidence; *(3)* sustained by God; *(4)* leaning on the Word and the divine promise.¹⁰⁰

And with this faith we are everywhere commanded to accede to God, praying and confiding, without ***ὀλιγοπιστία*** (*oligopistia,* little-faith) or doubting or diffidence. Let us know, therefore, that ***faith signifies not only a notice or a carnal assent, but a certain, firm, and immoveable confidence,*** which God requires of us, and wills that to Him be attributed the *praise of veracity* in the preservation of His promises — which praise they take away from God who *waver and doubt concerning the gratuitous remission of sins which is promised to us,* and who *charge God with lying* (1 John 5) and *diminish the merit of the Son of God.*

**With faith, therefore,** ***πεποίθησις, παρρησία, καὶ πληροφορία*** (*pepoithēsis, parrēsia, and plērophoria* — *reliance, boldness, and full assurance*) are said, which are true, and are to be opposed to Papist waverings. But at the same time *tender and pious minds who complain about the weakness of their own faith are to be admonished* that judgement concerning true faith must not always be taken from *sense.* For *sense does not precede but follows faith,* and often it is *hidden and overwhelmed by the cross and by other calamities.* From the ***Word,*** therefore, must judgement be made about true faith. And the Word sets forth to us these four things:

**First,** that ***the power of the Lord is perfected in weakness*** (2 Cor. 12). For God is powerful in the greatest weakness; He quickens in the midst of death, He illumines minds in the dense fog, He does His works in the middle of contraries.

**Second,** that faith is not always a burning light and a sensibly-perceptible confidence and a courageous flame openly shining, but often is *only smoking flax* (Isa. 42; Matt. 12) — sometimes in knowledge (Rom. 14), sometimes in confidence (Mark 9). And often faith is great, as in the Centurion, in the Canaanite woman (Matt. 8; 15); sometimes it is weak, as in that one of Mark 9: **τῇ σῇ δύναμει, μικρῇ πίστει βοήθει** — *"help thou by Thy power the weak faith"* — as Christ says: *"O suffering one, help by Thy faith."*

**Third,** that the *dignity of faith* is not to be *reckoned or estimated from its own firmness and perfection, but from the object which faith apprehends.* For since faith does not err in its object, but seeks and endeavours to apprehend it — however weak the confidence — amid trepidations, ***certainly it is true faith, though extremely modest and weak.***

**Fourth,** that ***Scripture commands us to pray after the example of that one:*** *"I believe, Lord; help Thou my weakness."* This prayer testifies that faith is present, though its sense is either exceedingly small or none at all.

## §21. On the Third Papist Dogma — the Trent Certitude-Anathema and Eight Firm Arguments in Response

**Now we shall speak of the third Papist dogma,** which in the Council of Trent is set forth in these words: ***"If any one shall say that it is necessary for a man to believe with certainty that his sins are remitted for the remission of sins,*** *etc., or that no one is truly justified save he who believes himself to be justified,* ***let him be anathema.***"¹⁰¹ Against these *blasphemies against the Son of God* let us hold these ***eight firm and immoveable arguments and testimonies.***

**First:** *the nature, propriety, and dignity of the promises, and the power of God.* For we ought not to look upon something in ourselves, but upon Christ Himself and upon the promises made by God concerning Christ and on account of Christ, by which God has testified *that He wills to be Father, helper, and preserver of believers.* And God is faithful, loving and preserving His promises (Rom. 4); to whom he who has no faith, ***as though he had struck God with a slap of a lie,*** accuses Him (1 John 5) — which how great a sin it is, pious minds can consider. Nor need we ascend into heaven, being curious about the *hidden counsel and decree of God,* but we have the *Word and Promise* (Rom. 10): *"the word of faith which we preach,"* in which God has made known His will to us — so that with Paul (2 Cor. 2) we can say: *"We hold the mind of Christ."* This *majesty of the promises,* therefore, *prohibits and rejects doubting, and requires faith,* according to that saying: *"Therefore from faith, that the promise may be firm"* (Rom. 4).

**Second:** *the nature of faith,* which is a ***firm and certain knowledge of the divine benevolence toward us freely exhibited through Christ, supported by the truth of the divine promises, and made manifest to our minds through the Holy Spirit,*** and is ***ὑπόστασις, πληροφορία, παρρησία, ἔλεγχος,*** and ***πεποίθησις*** (Heb. 10 and 11) — according to Matt. 9: *"Have confidence, son, thy sins are remitted to thee"*; and Luke 7: *"Thy faith has saved thee."*

**Third:** *the Holy Spirit kindling, confirming, and sealing faith in our minds and hearts,* and *testifying that we are sons of God, beloved in the Beloved* (Rom. 8; Eph. 1, 4; 2 Cor. 1, 5). For He is the ***arrhabōn*** — that is, the *pledge and cognizance* — ratifying and confirming the pledge or promise, that faith may be held undoubted.

**Fourth:** *the example and experience of faith,* such as was in Abraham (Rom. 4): *"He who believed against hope in hope, and was not weakened in faith, nor hesitated in the promise of God, but was made strong by faith, most fully knowing that He who had promised was also mighty to perform — wherefore also God imputed this to him unto righteousness."* Similar things are everywhere read in the Psalms: *"In Thee, Lord, have I hoped; I shall not be confounded,"* etc. And hither pertains that Pauline exclamation: *"Who shall accuse?"* (Rom. 8).

**Fifth:** *the diverse effects of faith* (Rom. 5): *to have peace with God; to stand in the grace of God; to glory under the hope of the glory of God; to be loved by God* (Rom. 8); *to invoke God in the name of Christ and to be heard by God* (1 John 5), etc.

**Sixth:** *the doctrine of the true use of the Sacraments,* which were therefore instituted by the Son of God, that the promise of the Gospel might be confirmed and sealed to believers — as Paul names circumcision *the seal of the righteousness of faith* (Rom. 4); and 1 Peter 3: *baptism is called* ***ἐπερώτημα*** *(eperōtēma, stipulation) and the covenant of a good conscience;* and in the Supper it is said to each one: *"Receive, eat; this is the body of Christ delivered for thee,"* etc. It is fitting, therefore, that in the use of the Sacraments each one believe that his sins are remitted to him.

**Seventh:** *the doctrine of the Gospel not only reprehends doubting* (Matt. 6, 14; Mark 11; Rom. 4; James 1; Luke 12; 1 John 5, in which places ***ὀλιγοπιστία, καὶ τῷ διστάζειν, διακρίνεσθαι, καὶ μετεωρίζεσθαι*** mention and accusation are made), *but also commands that we must strive against doubting and ask of God the strength of faith* (Mark 9: *"Aid my unbelief"*; Luke 17: *"Increase to us faith"*). It also commands the exploration and examination to be instituted (2 Cor. 13): *"Try and prove yourselves whether ye be in the faith."* And this examination is made:

**(1)** *by the sense of pain over admitted sins;*
**(2)** *by confidence in the promise of the remission of sins on account of Christ;*
**(3)** *by prayer and by groaning to God, that He make us vessels of mercy and temples of the Holy Spirit, and rule us in our vocation;*
**(4)** *by the struggle of the Spirit against the flesh, or by hatred of sin and victory;*
**(5)** *by love of the Word of God and use of the Sacraments;*
**(6)** *by patience in the cross, humility in life, zeal in doing good to others, modesty, temperance, chastity, truth, and other fruits of faith;*
**(7)** *by desire of eternal life.*

**Eighth:** *the distinction of Law and Gospel evidently shows that doubting must be resisted.* For since the Law leaves us in doubting on account of our unworthiness and weakness (we cannot satisfy the Law), the Gospel immediately succeeds the Law, and sets forth to us *the doctrine of the gratuitous remission of sins on account of the Son,* and commands us to be certain of the grace of God and of our salvation. This distinction of Law and Gospel would not be, if doubting were to be indulged.

**These and more arguments and testimonies of Scripture show that it is blasphemy against the Son of God and a most impudent lie, if any one says that God wills that we give place to doubting and be uncertain whether we are in the grace of God on account of Christ.**

## §22. Papist Objections Rebutted — Eight Answers

**But the Papists object the saying of Solomon, Ecclesiastes 9:** *"Man knows not whether he is worthy of love or of hatred. Therefore no one can determine that he is accepted to God."*

**I answer.** The scope and context of Solomon's discourse evidently shows that these words are to be understood *of external cases and events,* and that this only is said, — that *in the world it cannot be discerned from those events whether God loves or hates some, since to the just certain sad things happen equally with the impious, and the impious no less than the pious experience joyful successes.* Solomon therefore does not deny that a pious man must certainly determine that he has God propitious — namely, from *faith* — but denies that judgement about this matter is to be taken from external cases and events, which to the impious are often common with the pious.

**Then they object that saying of Sirach 5:** *"Of the propitiated sin, be not secure. Therefore doubting is commanded, when security and confidence are prohibited."*

**I answer.** Sirach in that whole chapter transmits *precepts against carnal security,* lest any one heap up sins without penitence, moved by that false persuasion *that it is lawful for him to indulge in sin because the propitiation has been made and set forth.* For thus the words sound: ***περὶ ἐξιλασμοῦ μὴ ἄφοβος γίνου προσθεῖναι ἁμαρτίαν ἐφ᾽ ἁμαρτίας*** — *"Concerning propitiation, be not without fear beyond measure, so as to add sins to sins"* — that is, *do not think that by thy sacrifice or opus operatum thou canst satisfy for sin, nor suppose that propitiation is set forth to thee that it may be lawful to sin and abuse the grace of God, nor be secure even though God for a while conceals-with the eyes at sins; but fear God, and flee sin, and defer not penitence.*

**Another:**

> Jonah 3: *"Let each do penitence: who knows? Perhaps the Lord will be converted and will pardon."*

**I answer.** These words are not of *doubting,* but *of one hoping well, and casting himself and all his matters upon God's mercy, and committing himself to the divine will.* And the more learned know that these particles — ***מִי יוֹדֵעַ*** (*Mī yōdēa,* "who knows"), *if perchance* (2 Sam. 12; Joel 2) — are particles by which the pious use this manner, when they *hope well and desire the aid of God and liberation from corporeal evils,* and *wish that they had already emerged from those evils.*

**Another:**

> *"God alone is the searcher of hearts"* (Rom. 8).
> *To affirm that one is in grace, is to search the heart.*
> *Therefore we ought not to determine that we are certainly in grace.*

**I answer to the major.** *God alone is* ***καρδιογνώστης*** — as concerns all things known and unknown to us universally. But the *minor is false.* For we do not search the heart when we say that we are in the grace of God, but we ***embrace the voice and promise of God opening to our hearts His heart and mind toward us,*** and willing us to *believe with the heart unto righteousness* (Rom. 10).

**Another:**

> *The pious ought to fear God and to work out their salvation with fear and trembling* (Phil. 2).
> *Therefore we ought not to determine by certain faith that we are in grace.*

**I answer.** A ***twofold fear of God*** is described in the sacred letters: **filial** and **servile.** The **filial** befits all the pious, and requires and confirms the *confidence of grace,* and always retains *faith, hope, and purity of doctrine* with great care and solicitude, and shuns lapses against conscience. Thus ***fear is rightly conjoined with faith.*** For he who acknowledges God as Father and himself as a son by Him, embraces by faith the grace offered — nor can he doubt of God's mercy toward him more than a carnal son doubts of the benevolence of his parent.

**Another:**

> *No one is certain of perseverance — whether he shall persevere in faith and grace unto the end. Therefore there cannot be certain confidence about our salvation.*

**I answer.** Although many by their own fault, by security, diffidence, and works of the flesh cast off and lose faith and the Holy Spirit, — yet it is certain that those who *mortify the actions of the flesh by the Spirit, and by faith adhere to Christ, asking of God the gift of perseverance, and struggling against the petulance of the flesh, and confirming their faith by the Word and by the frequent use of the Sacraments,* — by no means hang in ambiguous doubting, but are ***certain concerning perseverance,*** according to the promise: *"He who has begun in you shall perfect unto the day of Jesus Christ"* (Phil. 1). For we have God the Father as ***ἀρχηγόν*** (*archēgon,* Leader); the Son as ***τελεσιεργὸν*** (*telesiergon,* Perfecter); the Holy Spirit as ***βεβαιωτὴν μόνιμον*** (*bebaiōtēn monimon,* Confirmer stable) — the confirmer stabilising and supporting in us true faith. *Who then shall separate us? I am certain that neither things present nor things to come shall separate us* (Rom. 8).

**Bernard, *On the Seven Fragments, Sermon 3***: *"I consider three things in which my whole hope consists: the charity of adoption, the truth of the promise, the power of rendering. Let foolish thought murmur as much as it wills, saying: 'Who art thou, or how great is that glory, or by what merits dost thou hope to obtain it?' — I confidently answer: 'I know Whom I have believed, and I am certain that in exceeding charity He has adopted me, that He is true in promise, that He is powerful in exhibition.' This is that triple cord which is difficult to break — which, sent down to us from our fatherland into this prison, let us firmly, I beseech, hold, that He Himself may lift us up, He Himself draw us and drag us on unto the sight of the glory of the great God."*¹⁰²

**Another:**

> *The pious often bear various temptations by which they are so shaken that they suspect they have fallen from the grace of God — which being so, they surely cannot determine that they are certainly accepted by God.*

**I answer.** The pious are not so certain of their salvation *as though they were now immune from all temptations, and could be free and secure,* but *they know that after having received grace they are called to combats before others,* and therefore ought to pray: *"Lead us not into temptation, but deliver us from evil."* For:

> ***Nunquam bella bonis, nunquam certamina desunt,***
> ***Et cum quo certet, mens pia semper habet.***
>
> *(Never do wars fail the good, never do combats fail;*
> *and the pious mind always has with what to contend.)*

Then they know that ***in the very temptations and combats faith becomes more illustrious,*** and *God by His power and goodness gives an outcome, that they may bear all things* (1 Cor. 10). For:

> ***Semper vera fides inter certamina crescit,***
> ***Et victrix palmae praemia vera capit.***
> ***At non certanti non est speranda corona.***
>
> *(Always the true faith grows in the midst of combats,*
> *and the victorious [faith] receives the true rewards of the palm.*
> *But to him who does not contend, no crown is to be hoped for.)*

**2 Timothy 2:** *"No one is crowned unless he shall have contended lawfully."* Then they know that ***however vehement the temptations,*** the faith of the mercy of God — however weak — is *always tenaciously fixed in the heart,* by which as by *sword and shield they are armed against the snares of the Devil and of the world,* as Paul says (2 Cor. 4): *"We have this treasure in earthen vessels, that the eminence of the virtue may be of God and not from us: while in all things we are pressed, but not made anxious; we labour, but are not destitute; we suffer persecution, but in it are not deserted; we are cast down, but do not perish."*

**Another:**

> *Humility of mind and open confession of infirmity and unworthiness please God.*
> *Therefore confidence displeases Him.*

**I answer.** I concede the whole *of confidence and of carnal security.* But another thing is ***faith in Christ,*** which always has *humility joined with itself,* and *plainly despairs of its own works and merits,* and has its salvation only in the wounds of Christ, and always has in heart and mouth this: *"Enter not into judgement with Thy servant; have mercy on me on account of the Son, sprinkle me with hyssop; without Thee there is nothing in me."* Likewise: *"I am conscious of nothing to myself, but in this I am not justified"* — that is, *I do not lean on my dignity or on my innocence or on my justice, but I seek another obedience and the merit of the Son of God, which I set against the judgement and wrath of God.*

**These things, which are the faith and the humility of believers, both show and repress and coerce all insolence, carnal security, and vicious affections** — like also: *"He that stands, let him see lest he fall"* (1 Cor. 10); *"Do not be high-minded, but fear"* (Rom. 11), etc. For since in believers and in the saints the *flesh* still is, it is necessary that by such admonitions be repressed *the insolence, security, and petulance of the flesh.*

**Another:**

> *A great part of men abuse the doctrine of the certitude of faith unto security.*
> *Therefore it must be taught otherwise concerning faith.*

**I answer.** *Abuse does not take away or change the truth of doctrine.* Then, *to the secure and impenitent* is not set forth the doctrine of faith, but the *thundering and malediction of the Law;* but *only to those pale-and-afraid and acknowledging their sins and fleeing to the throne of grace* is the doctrine of faith set forth. For these must be aided by true and firm consolation according to the promise of the Gospel, lest, while they indulge fearful doubting, they be overwhelmed by despair.

**Another:**

> *So great is the weakness of the flesh, that perfect confidence and certitude of faith without the temptation of doubting cannot in this life be had.*
> *Therefore useless is the doctrine of the certitude of faith.*

**I answer.** *There is not required an absolute confidence,* nor is *faith estimated from its own firmness and perfection,* but it is shown that ***faith, however weak, ought — from the object which it apprehends — to seek a certain and firm consolation, and to struggle against doubting and trepidation, and to acquiesce in the promise of the Gospel,*** as was said above concerning *weak faith.*

This split derives from the Stoic and Middle-Platonist cause-terminology; cf. **Diogenes Laertius VII. 55** and **Alexander of Aphrodisias**, *De Fato* 22–23 (*Bruns* 191–193). The Reformation-era transmission of the terminology was through Melanchthon's *Erotemata dialectices* (1547) and Chytraeus's *Regulae studiorum* (1562). Selnecker's application to the doctrinal *definitio Evangelii* is programmatic and appears again in the parallel Locus-openings of Kirchner, Chytraeus, and Chemnitz.

²⁹ **ἑρμηνεὺς τῶν μεγάλων βουλημάτων τοῦ Πατρός** — *"interpreter of the great counsels of the Father."* Selnecker's Greek here echoes **Clement of Alexandria, *Protrepticus* 10 (PG 8:225)** and **Origen, *Peri Archon* I. 2. 6 (PG 11:135)** — the *Logos*-as-Chancellor tradition. The OCR-corrupted *Χριστῦ* (for *Χριστοῦ*) has been silently restored.

³⁰ **αἰτία προηγουμένη** — technical Stoic term for the *interior antecedent* impulsive cause (as opposed to the *προκαταρκτική* — the *external triggering* cause). See **Alexander of Aphrodisias**, *De Fato* 22 (*Bruns* 191); **SVF** II. 976–977.

³¹ **Tauler.** — Attribution to **Johannes Tauler** (c. 1300–1361), the Rhineland mystic whose *Predigten* (1498 Leipzig; 1521 Basel enlarged edition) Luther read with great enthusiasm and to whom Luther attributes much of his early "*theologia crucis*" formation. The specific *sic diligit Deus* saying is not readily located in Tauler's extant corpus (cf. **Corin, *Sermons de Tauler* — critical edition 1911, 3 vols**) and may derive from the *Theologia Deutsch* (1518 Wittenberg — Luther's own edition), which Reformation-era readers routinely conflated with Tauler.

³² **Gregory of Nazianzus, *Oratio* 45.13 (Oratio in Sanctum Pascha II)** — **PG** 36:641; **NPNF** II.7:427. Selnecker's citation of Nazianzen's *"in nothing does God rejoice so much as in the salvation of man"* is a standard patristic-Lutheran auxiliary to the *sic dilexit* theme of John 3:16.

³³ **Selnecker's catalogue of modern Ministers of the Gospel.** — A characteristic Reformation-generational roll-call: **Luther, Melanchthon, Bugenhagen, Justus Jonas, Cruciger, Forster, Wenceslaus Linck, Thomas Venatorius, Vitus Theodorus (Dietrich), Brenz, Erhard Schnepf, Johann Hess, Ambrosius Moibanus, Aepinus.** Note that Selnecker in this Pars II passage restricts the modern catalogue to first-generation Reformers, whereas in Pars I *De Verbo Dei* Ch. 2 he extends the catalogue to include his own generation (himself, Chemnitz, Chytraeus, Andreä, Musculus, Körner). Compare **VD16** for each name; biographical dates and modern editions to be supplied per the standard glossary.

³⁴ **Cyril of Alexandria**, *In Ioannem* — the ***Mediator Dei et hominum*** formulation echoes 1 Timothy 2:5 and the paradigm-Cyrilline *Christological* argument. Precise Cyril-locus not yet identified with certainty; may be *In Ioannem* I. 9 (PG 73:161) or *In Ioannem* IV. 2 (PG 73:591). The saying became a Lutheran catchphrase through Chemnitz's *De duabus naturis in Christo* (1571) — cf. Chemnitz **§9** (Preus tr. 1971, pp. 71–72).

³⁵ ***Beneficia privativa vs positiva*** — the technical scholastic distinction. *Privative benefits* are those which *take away* an evil (remission, liberation); *positive benefits* are those which *confer* a good (donation of Spirit, imputation of righteousness, new obedience). Selnecker's parenthetical *"as the term has survived in the schools"* acknowledges the term's scholastic pedigree (cf. **Peter Lombard, *Sententiae* III. dist. 19**; **Thomas, *ST* III q. 46**) while retaining it as pedagogically useful for the Lutheran *ordo salutis.*

## From the Proem *De Evangelio* — §7 Two Principal Parts of Doctrine

³⁶ **The Two-Denarii Parable — Luke 10:35 (the Good Samaritan).** — Selnecker's typological reading — *Law and Gospel = the two denarii Christ gave to the innkeeper* — is a standard Reformation-era exegetical trope, going back to **Augustine, *Quaestiones Evangeliorum* II. 19 (PL 35:1340)** and later to **Luther's *Kirchenpostille* on the Thirteenth Sunday after Trinity** (WA 22:250ff.).

³⁷ **Cross-reference to *De Bonis Operibus.*** — This Locus is treated in Pars II as a sub-Locus under *De Poenitentia* (OCR ln 8265ff., forthcoming in T4).

³⁸ **The seven-verse mnemonic on the utilities of the Law.** — Selnecker's own didactic hexameters, evidently composed for the *Institutiones* itself and later collected in his verse-catechetical work *Elenchus Praeceptorum et Verba Formulae Concordiae Christiana* (Leipzig 1580). The seven utilities correspond to a Melanchthonian schema of the *usus Legis moralis:* (1) natural theology; (2) rule of life; (3) revelation of sin and wrath; (4) *paedagogus ad Christum;* (5) *primae iustitiae imagines;* (6) *futuri iudicii norma;* (7) *fons omnis honesti* in civil life. Elements (3), (4), and (6) map to the *usus paedagogicus* (FC VI); (2) and (7) to the *usus politicus;* and (1), (5), and (6) to the *usus didacticus / tertius usus.* Cf. **FC SD VI** (*BSLK* 962ff. / *Kolb-Wengert* 587ff.) — the *tertius usus legis* article, which Selnecker himself as FC co-author knew from the inside.

³⁹ **The Antinomian controversy.** — Selnecker's structuring of *seventeen* Antinomian arguments (§§8) unfolds a controversy already two generations old at the time of Pars II. The *first Antinomian controversy* (1527–1540) was Luther-vs-**Johann Agricola** (*Eisleben,* c. 1494–1566), who from *Wittenberg 1527* through the *Torgau Articles of 1537* argued that penitence should be preached from the Gospel alone, not from the Law. Luther's *Disputationes contra Antinomos* (WA 39/I:342–584; six disputation-cycles 1537–1540) provides the paradigm rebuttal-format on which Selnecker's Ch. 8 depends. The *second Antinomian controversy* (1556–1577) was **Andreas Poach** and **Anton Otho** vs the *Wittenberg-Melanchthonian* line, resolved in **Formula of Concord Article VI** *tertius usus legis* — of which Selnecker was co-author. Cf. **FC SD VI. 1ff.** (*BSLK* 962 / *Kolb-Wengert* 587).

## From the Proem *De Evangelio* — §8 Refutation of Antinomian Arguments

⁴⁰ **Argument I — Luke 24:47** *"repentance and remission of sins should be preached in his name."* — The paradigm Antinomian proof-text. Luther's *First Antinomian Disputation* (WA 39/I:349ff.) addresses this argument at length; Selnecker's *matter-vs-form* distinction (*materia poenitentiae ex lege, forma poenitentiae salutaris ex Evangelio*) is Luther's exact formulation in the *Disputation V* of 1538.

⁴¹ **Argument III — John 16:8ff.** *"He will convict the world of sin, of righteousness, and of judgement."* — Selnecker's exegesis (that *sin* here means principally *unbelief in the Son*) is Cyril-of-Alexandrian and Augustine, ***In Ioannem*** *Tract. 95** (PL 35:1871–1875; NPNF I.7:369–373). It is also the FC SD III/V/VI-adjacent line taken by Chemnitz in *Enchiridion* (Chemnitz 1569 Wittenberg edition).

⁴² **"D. Philippi"** — Philip Melanchthon (1497–1560). Selnecker regularly refers to Melanchthon simply as *Philippus* or *D. Philippus,* per the affectionate Wittenberg-generation convention. The *"in cruce Christi simul quasi suspensi"* formulation is Melanchthonian and appears in the *Loci Præcipui Theologici* (1559) *De Peccato Originis* (CR 21:685 / MSA II/1:296).

⁴³ **βιάζεται** (*biazetai*) at Matthew 11:12 — the *"suffers violence"* verb — is much-disputed exegetically. Selnecker follows the *middle-voice* reading (*"is taken by force"* actively) preferred by Luther in the *Auslegung der zehn Gebote* (WA 6:213ff. / *LW* 44:15ff.) and by Melanchthon in the *Loci* 1559 *De Evangelio.* The alternative *passive* reading (*"suffers violence"* — is under attack) was favoured by Chrysostom and Augustine and is defended in the modern critical editions (**BDAG** *biazomai*, sense 2).

⁴⁴ **Christ's four modes of fulfilling the Law.** — Selnecker's schema is Anselmian in the second mode (*satisfactio vicaria* — sufficient *λύτρον*) and Melanchthonian-FC-III in the first, third, and fourth. Cf. **Chemnitz, *De duabus naturis in Christo*** ch. 30 (Preus tr. 253–258) for the paradigm-Lutheran two-natured *obedientia activa et passiva* framework.

## From the Proem *De Evangelio* — §9 Six Differences between Law and Gospel

⁴⁵ **The *Six Differences* schema.** — Directly inherited from Melanchthon's *Loci Præcipui Theologici* (1559) *De Discrimine Legis et Evangelii* (CR 21:695ff. / MSA II/1:308ff.). Selnecker's ordering — from *notice, matter, form of promises, commiations, effects, objects* — is precisely Melanchthon's. This paradigm Reformation-era distinction is codified confessionally in **Apology Article IV** (Justification) — cf. **Ap. IV. 5** (*BSLK* 158 / *Kolb-Wengert* 121) — and in **Formula of Concord Article V** *De Lege et Evangelio* — cf. **FC SD V. 17ff.** (*BSLK* 953ff. / *Kolb-Wengert* 583ff.).

⁴⁶ ***In iudicio finium regendorum*** — Roman-law technical formula for the *actio finium regundorum* (Justinian, *Institutes* 4.17.6; *Digest* 10.1) — the boundary-regulation praetorian action. Selnecker's metaphor: the Law-Gospel distinction requires the same precision of boundary-marking as does the neighbours' land-boundary case.

⁴⁷ **Psalm 20:3–4** (Vulgate 20:4) — Selnecker's application of the David-quote to the *promise of the Messiah in the New Testament* is Augustinian; cf. **Augustine, *Enarratio in Ps.* 20 (PL 36:167ff.).**

⁴⁸ **Augustine on the Two Testaments.** — Precise Augustinian locus not yet certainly identified; the *vetus prius revelatum, novum posterius revelatum* formulation may derive from **Augustine, *Contra Faustum Manichaeum* XVIII. 6 (PL 42:346)**, or from the *De catechizandis rudibus* (PL 40:317ff.). To be verified against **CCSL** at the audit.

⁴⁹ **The *Christus corpus, Moses umbram* verse.** — Draws on Colossians 2:17 (*umbra futurorum, corpus autem Christi*). The verse-form itself is a *Reformation-era Lutheran mnemonic* attributed variously to Luther and to Melanchthon; the precise source is not identified. Cf. also **Melanchthon, *Loci Præcipui* (1559) *De Sacramentis*** (CR 21:857) for the parallel *umbra/corpus* Sacrament-distinction.

The phrase *saluberrima et maxime necessaria doctrina* is Melanchthonian and appears verbatim at CR 21:735 (*Loci* 1559 *De Iustificatione* opening).

## From Locus I *De Iustificatione*

⁵¹ **Transition passage** (OCR ln 1125–1128, closing the pre-Iustificatio material): *"Now we shall take in hand the most saving and most necessary doctrine of the Justification of man before God, God helping us."* — Selnecker's *sedes-doctrinae* signal for the Locus.

⁵² **Threefold use of *iustitia.*** — This distinction is Melanchthon's, from the *Loci Præcipui Theologici* (1559) *De Iustificatione* opening — cf. **CR** 21:731ff., **MSA** II/1:328. Selnecker inherits it exactly.

⁵³ **Aristotle, *Nicomachean Ethics* V.1.1129b30** — **ἐν δὲ δικαιοσύνῃ συλλήβδην πᾶσ' ἀρετή ἐστι** ("in justice all virtue is comprehended in a word"). — Aristotle attributes the sentence itself to *Theognis* fr. 147 West. The Latin verse-rendering *Iustitia in se se virtutes continet omnes* is a common Renaissance-humanist verse-hexameter; compare **Erasmus, *Adagia* III.5.62.**

⁵⁴ **Aristotle**, *Nicomachean Ethics* V.1 in general — where justice is treated as the *summary virtue* embracing all others.

⁵⁵ ***Iustitia legalis*** — the scholastic category of "legal justice" as elaborated by **Thomas Aquinas, ST II-II q. 58 art. 5–6.** Selnecker's rejection of Philosophy's satisfaction with *external discipline* is Melanchthonian and reflects the *Apology of the Augsburg Confession* Article IV rejection of philosophical righteousness — cf. **Ap. IV. 12–14** (*BSLK* 160 / *Kolb-Wengert* 122).

⁵⁶ **The thief on the cross** — *Luke 23:43.* Selnecker's use of the good thief as the *paradigmatic case* of *sola fide* justification is standard Lutheran doctrinal-preaching, deriving from **Luther, *Lectures on Genesis* on Gen 15:6** (WA 42:562ff. / *LW* 3:19ff.).

⁵⁷ **Selnecker's exposition of Psalm 32.** — Reference to his own earlier work: cross-reference not yet identified with certainty; probable candidate is his *Der ganze Psalter Davids* (Nürnberg 1565, expanded Leipzig 1581) or the *Psalmodia* (Wittenberg 1567). To be verified against **VD16** at the audit.

⁵⁸ ***Forensis vocabulum.*** — Central Melanchthon-inherited term. Selnecker's placement of the forensic character right at the head of the definition is programmatic and interfaces directly with **Formula of Concord Article III** *Solida Declaratio* — of which Selnecker was co-author — which insists on the forensic and imputative character of justification against the Osiandrian *unio essentialis / iustitia essentialis* framework. Cf. **FC SD III. 9ff., 17ff., 30ff., 62–63** — *BSLK* 916ff. / *Kolb-Wengert* 562ff. This is the *sedes doctrinae* passage of the whole Locus.

## From Locus I *De Iustificatione* — §§3-15

⁵⁹ **Bernard of Clairvaux (1090–1153), *In festo Annuntiationis Beatae Mariae Virginis, Sermo I* — *PL* 183:383–390.** Selnecker recites *the whole sermon* verbatim from the Latin editions of Bernard current in the Wittenberg-Reformation milieu (probably the 1552 Basel Herwagen edition of Bernard's *Opera omnia*). The paradigm-Reformation reception of Bernard is thoroughly attested; **Luther** himself frequently cites this very sermon as a *sedes* of the Reformation-era anti-Pelagian doctrine of *the merit of Christ vs. all human works* (WA 5:203ff., *In Ps. 5* enarrationes). Cf. also **Kolb, *Bound Choice, Election, and Wittenberg Theological Method*** (Eerdmans, 2005), Ch. 3, on the Wittenberg reception of Bernard's soteriology.

⁶⁰ **Ambrose, *De Officiis Ministrorum* III.7 (PL 16:150–151)** — *"Gloria vera est testimonium conscientiae, non quale ille Pharisaeus habebat..."* Bernard here paraphrases the Ambrosian topos.

⁶¹ **The Four Sisters allegory** — Bernard's dramatisation of Psalm 85:11 is the paradigm Western-medieval *sororum-in-consilio* meditation. The tradition ultimately derives from **Hugh of St. Victor, *De Sacramentis* I.8.4 (PL 176:307)** and reaches its most influential later development in **the *Meditationes Vitae Christi* of pseudo-Bonaventure** (14th c.). It became a standard *Marian-Annunciation* preaching-topic across the medieval West and passed with the Reformers into the Lutheran Advent-preaching tradition — cf. Luther, *Kirchenpostille* on Advent I (WA 10/I.2:1ff.).

⁶² **The sixfold denial of what justifies before God.** — Selnecker's structuring is Melanchthonian, drawn ultimately from the *Apology of the Augsburg Confession* Article IV. The full Apology-IV structure enumerates *justificatio sine operibus legis* against seven categories: opera ceremonialia, opera moralia, opera electicia, iustitia philosophica, opera renatorum, contritio, ipsa fides considered as work. Cf. **Ap. IV. 15ff.** (*BSLK* 160ff. / *Kolb-Wengert* 122ff.) and — for the specific "even the good works of the reborn" denial — **FC SD III. 25ff.** (*BSLK* 923 / *Kolb-Wengert* 564).

⁶³ **Selnecker's twofold-imputation analytic.** — This distinction (real-inherent vs rational-attributed imputation) is one of the paradigm anti-Trent Reformation-era formulations. It reappears verbatim in **Chemnitz, *Loci theologici* (1591), *De Iustificatione*** and later in **Gerhard, *Loci theologici* (1610-1622) Loc. XVI: De Iustificatione**, which build out the same twofold-analytic in extended anti-Bellarmine polemic. Cf. **Preus, *The Theology of Post-Reformation Lutheranism*** (Concordia, 1970-1972) Vol II, Ch. 2 on the seventeenth-century systematisation.

⁶⁴ **Selnecker's definition of imputation.** — This is Selnecker's own composition and appears (with minor variation) in his *Explicationes Symbolorum* (1572) and *Institutionis Christianae Religionis Pars Prima* (1579) *De Peccato* section on Christ's satisfaction. The *quod nec sumus, nec habemus, nec fecimus* triadic structure is programmatic and echoes the Chemnitz *De duabus naturis* Christological *genus apotelesmaticum* formulation. Cf. **FC SD III. 40-43** (*BSLK* 926ff. / *Kolb-Wengert* 566).

⁶⁵ ***Mercantile commutation*** (*mercatoria quaedam commutatio*) — the *fröhlicher Wechsel* / *admirabile commercium* topos, one of Luther's paradigm-metaphors for justification. See **Luther, *Sermo de duplici iustitia*** (WA 2:145-152 / *LW* 31:297-306), the 1519 sermon which first developed the *fröhlicher Wechsel* imagery. Also **Luther, *Ein Sermon von den guten Werken*** (WA 6:196ff. / *LW* 44:15ff.) on the *marriage-exchange* between Christ and the believing soul. Selnecker retains the Latin *mercatoria commutatio* formulation as the tradition's Latin technical term.

⁶⁶ **Anselm of Canterbury (c. 1033–1109; not 1058 as Selnecker's date — Selnecker follows an earlier bibliographical convention).** The paradigm Reformation-received text is **Anselm, *Cur Deus Homo* (PL 158:359-432)**, with the specific prayer-formulas Selnecker cites drawn from the **Anselm, *Meditationes*** and the ***Orationes*** printed variously in the 16th-c. editions. The two prayer-formulas as Selnecker gives them appear in **Anselm, *Meditatio* III** and **Anselm, *Oratio* 65** — but the Latin printed text Selnecker uses is that of the **1547 Cologne / Basel edition of Anselm's *Opera omnia*.** For the modern critical text, cf. **Schmitt, *S. Anselmi Cantuariensis Archiepiscopi Opera omnia,*** 6 vols (Edinburgh 1946-1961).

⁶⁷ **The extended Anselm-meditation** ("*Terret me vita mea...*") — this specific passage is the paradigm-Anselmian meditation *De miseria humana ante iudicium Dei* and appears in **Anselm, *Meditatio 1 — Ad concitandum timorem*** (PL 158:709-712). Selnecker's Latin follows the received Wittenberg-Reformation text and was much cited by **Luther** in his early Genesis Lectures on Genesis 3-6 (WA 42:145ff. / *LW* 1:194ff.) and by **Melanchthon** in the *Loci Præcipui* on *De Iustitia hominis coram Deo* (CR 21:731ff. / MSA II/1:328ff.).

⁶⁸ **The *Cum sis ipse nocens* verses.** — This is an anonymous late-medieval anti-Reformation distich, quoted by Luther in the *Disputatio de iustificatione* of 1536 (WA 39/I:78ff.) and cited by **Bellarmine, *De iustificatione* II.1** as a paradigm-Catholic objection. Selnecker's Latin transmission of the verses follows the received Reformation-era anti-Tridentine polemical corpus.

⁶⁹ **The *gratia et donum* distinction.** — Paul at Romans 5:15 distinguishes ***ἡ χάρις καὶ ἡ δωρεά*** (*hē charis kai hē dōrea,* the grace and the gift). The Reformation-era Lutheran systematisation of this distinction into *iustificatio (= gratia, imputation)* and *renovatio (= donum, inhabitatio Spiritus)* is paradigmatically set out in **Luther, *Rationis Latomianae confutatio*** of 1521 (WA 8:43ff. / *LW* 32:133ff.) — the *Antilatomus,* which Selnecker knew thoroughly. Cf. also **FC SD III. 32-40** (*BSLK* 924ff. / *Kolb-Wengert* 565ff.) on the *gratia/donum* distinction as it interfaces with the anti-Osiandrian question of *iustitia essentialis.*

⁷⁰ **Osiander controversy.** — **Andreas Osiander** (1498–1552), *Von dem einigen Mittler Jesu Christo und Rechtfertigung des Glaubens* (Königsberg 1551), taught that man is justified not by the imputed obedience of Christ, but by the *indwelling essential righteousness of the divine nature of Christ* (*iustitia essentialis*) infused into the believer. The Osiandrian controversy 1549–1566 was resolved confessionally in **FC SD III** which explicitly rejects the Osiandrian *iustitia essentialis* framework and confirms the imputative-forensic model. Selnecker as FC co-author was directly involved. Cf. **FC SD III. 17-19, 60-63** (*BSLK* 920ff., 940-941 / *Kolb-Wengert* 563, 572).

⁷¹ **Sedes doctrinae de iustificatione.** — The paradigm-Lutheran Reformation-orthodoxy identification of Romans + Galatians as the *sedes* for the justification-doctrine is programmatic and traces to **Melanchthon's *Loci Præcipui Theologici*** (1559) *De Iustificatione* opening (CR 21:731ff. / MSA II/1:328ff.). Selnecker structures the whole exposition as an *analytical commentary* on the two Epistles.

⁷² **The ten arguments from Romans 4.** — Selnecker's numbered-argument schema tracks Melanchthon's *Commentarii in Epistolam Pauli ad Romanos* (1540 Wittenberg; **CR** 15:493-796 / **MSA** V:35-231) on Rom. 4 quite closely. For the modern critical treatment, cf. **Cranfield, *A Critical and Exegetical Commentary on the Epistle to the Romans*** (Edinburgh: T&T Clark, 1975-1979), Vol I, on Rom. 4:1-25 (pp. 224-260); and **Dunn, *Romans 1-8*** (WBC 38A, 1988), pp. 195-229.

⁷³ **Rom. 9 as anti-Pelagian rather than anti-conditional-election.** — Selnecker's reading of Rom. 9 — *the Pauline discourse targets Jewish-election-vs-Gentile-election in the *ordo salutis,* not a supralapsarian double-decree* — is the paradigm-Lutheran FC XI hermeneutic. Selnecker himself was co-author of FC XI *De Praedestinatione,* which explicitly rejects both the Reformed *decretum absolutum* and the Calvinist *supralapsarian* readings of Rom. 9. Cf. **FC SD XI. 4ff., 33-42, 76-90** (*BSLK* 1064ff. / *Kolb-Wengert* 640ff.).

⁷⁴ **Rom. 9:16 — *"Non est volentis..."*** — a paradigm-anti-Pelagian text, the *sedes* for the *sola gratia* strand of the Reformation-era doctrine of grace. Augustine's exegesis at *De praedestinatione sanctorum* 6-9 (PL 44:965ff. / NPNF I.5:498ff.) is the paradigm reference-point, but Selnecker deliberately does *not* follow the Augustinian double-predestinarian reading — retaining instead the Chrysostomean-cooperative reading which the FC XI framework then confirms.

⁷⁵ **The hardening-terminology.** — Selnecker's exhaustive philological analysis of the four hardening-verbs (*qāšāh, ḥāzaq, kābad,* and the LXX renderings *sklērynō / katischyō / barynō*) is a paradigm sixteenth-century humanist-Hebraist procedure and is remarkable for its philological accuracy. Selnecker draws on **Sebastian Münster's *Hebraica Biblia*** (Basel 1534-1535, revised 1546) and **Sanctes Pagninus, *Thesaurus Linguae Sanctae*** (Lyon 1529; revised 1575) for the Hebrew semantic-field analysis.

⁷⁶ **Oecumenius (c. 990–1050 by traditional attribution)** — the Byzantine commentator whose *Catena in Pauli Epistolas* (PG 118:325ff. for Romans) was much used by Reformation-era exegetes as a Greek-Fathers-catena. The specific formulation **ὁ θεὸς σκληρὸν καὶ ἀπειθῆ εἶναι συγχωρεῖ** ("*God concedes him to be hard and disobedient*") appears in Oecumenius on Rom. 9:18 (PG 118:509).

⁷⁷ **Augustine, *De correptione et gratia* 14-15 (PL 44:942-946)** for the *God-willing-to-be-obeyed-or-punished* formulation; and **Augustine, *Enchiridion* 100-104 (PL 40:279-282; CCSL 46:103-105)** for the passage Selnecker cites on the *will-of-God-fulfilled-in-both-ways.*

⁷⁸ **Luther's *Enarrationes in Genesin* on Genesis 26** (WA 43:459ff. / *LW* 5:41ff.) — the ***Swan-Song*** of the aging Luther (1544-1545, published posthumously 1550-1552). The specific *retraction*-passage Selnecker excerpts is at **WA 43:459-463** on Gen. 26:9 and represents the mature Luther's programmatic clarification of the *De Servo Arbitrio* (1525; WA 18:600-787 / *LW* 33) — namely, that the *hidden God / revealed God* distinction must not be used to feed *predestinarian speculation* but always to *drive the anxious conscience to the vocative Christ.* The FC XI *Solida Declaratio* — Selnecker co-authored — canonises this Luther-*Enarratio*-26 hermeneutic as the confessional-Lutheran doctrine of predestination.

⁷⁹ **The vessels-of-wrath / vessels-of-mercy exegesis** — Selnecker's *"God has created no men for destruction"* is the paradigm-Lutheran FC XI reading of Rom. 9:22-23 against the Reformed *reprobation ante praevisa peccata* framework. Cf. **FC SD XI. 76-90** (*BSLK* 1091ff. / *Kolb-Wengert* 649ff.).

⁸⁰ **Selnecker's definition of faith.** — This is Selnecker's own compositional signature; the *firma et certa cognitio divinae erga nos benevolentiae* language is a *Concord-generation* Lutheran technical formulation, appearing verbatim in Chemnitz *Loci* (De Fide justificante) and Selnecker's own *Explicationes Symbolorum* (1572). It is the paradigm formulation against the *fides implicita* / *fides informis* Papist framework.

⁸¹ **The *synergon* controversy.** — Selnecker's careful *ordo/subject/matter*-analytic of the human will in conversion is the Second Antinomian Controversy's *synergism controversy* (1560-1577) working itself out toward FC II *De Libero Arbitrio.* Selnecker was co-author of FC II. Cf. **FC SD II. 44-90** (*BSLK* 890ff. / *Kolb-Wengert* 552ff.) — the *doctrine-of-the-servile-will* article confirming Luther's *De Servo Arbitrio* while resisting both *Manichaeism* (which makes man a stone) and *Melanchthonian synergism* (which makes man a co-cause). Selnecker's *materia rationalis / non-brutum / συνεργὸν = subjectum non otiosum* formulation is the paradigm FC II compromise-language.

⁸² **The *Nil sum, nulla miser* verses** — attributed by Selnecker to *D. Philippus* (Philip Melanchthon), and appearing in **Melanchthon, *Selectarum Declamationum Philippi Melanthonis... liber tertius*** (Strassburg 1546-1553), as part of the *Preces Ecclesiasticae.* The *massam humanam / massam Christi* metaphor here is the paradigm-Melanchthonian *incarnation-solidarity* topos.

⁸³ **Georg Spalatin (1484–1545) — the paradigm-Wittenberg court-preacher and Luther-collaborator.** The *Sola fides hominem caelesti donat honore* verses were composed by Spalatin as an epistolary poem to **Eobanus Hessus** (1488–1540), the paradigm-Wittenberg humanist-poet, and printed in **Eobanus's *Sylvae*** (Frankfurt 1539). Selnecker frequently draws on these Wittenberg-Reformation Latin verses as pedagogical mnemonics; compare his own *Elenchus Praeceptorum* (Leipzig 1580) which uses similar didactic-hexameter forms.

## From Locus I *De Iustificatione* — §§16-22

⁸⁴ **The four reasons for the exclusive *Sola Fide*.** — Selnecker's schema is paradigm-Lutheran and directly parallels **Melanchthon, *Loci Præcipui Theologici* (1559) *De Iustificatione*** (CR 21:741ff. / MSA II/1:340ff.). Cf. also **Chemnitz, *Examen Concilii Tridentini* (1565-1573), Pars I *De Iustificatione*** — the paradigm Reformation-orthodoxy anti-Trent handbook, structured as a rebuttal of Trent Session VI (1547) canon by canon; and **Apology of the Augsburg Confession Article IV** (*BSLK* 158ff. / *Kolb-Wengert* 120ff.) on the same four-reason apparatus. Note also **FC SD III. 32-45** (*BSLK* 924ff. / *Kolb-Wengert* 565ff.).

⁸⁵ **Chrysostom on Luke 8:50** — the *"only believe"* / *πίστιν εἰσάγωμεν* saying appears in **John Chrysostom, *Homilia in Lucam*** — precise locus not certainly identified; the Latin transmission is via the medieval *Catena aurea* of **Thomas Aquinas** on Luke 8 (*S. Thomae Aquinatis Opera*, Parma 1863, XII:99). The paradigm sixteenth-century transmission is through **Erasmus's *Novum Instrumentum*** apparatus on Luke 8:50.

⁸⁶ **Basil the Great (c. 330-379), *Homilia de Humilitate*** — PG 31:525-540; the *sola fide iustificari* saying is at PG 31:532. Reformation-era transmission via the **1532 Basel Herwagen** edition of Basil's *Opera*.

⁸⁷ **Gregory Nazianzen (329-390), *Oratio* — "de taxias"** — precise locus not certainly identified; the Latin transmission is via the **Wittenberg-Reformation patristic catena** and appears in similar form at **Melanchthon, *Loci* 1559 *De Iustificatione*** (CR 21:735) with attribution to Nazianzen's *Orationes.*

⁸⁸ **Photius of Sirmium** — Selnecker here appears to conflate two Photii: **Photius of Constantinople** (c. 810-891), whose *Amphilochia* and homiletic corpus are in PG 101-104, and the earlier **Photinus of Sirmium** (d. 376), the anti-Trinitarian condemned at the Councils of Sirmium 351 and Constantinople 381. The specific Galatians-3 quotation appears in **Photius of Constantinople's *Amphilochia*** (PG 101:1265ff.) rather than in any extant Photinus text; the *Sirmiensis* attribution in Selnecker's 1579 imprint is a Reformation-era bibliographic confusion.

⁸⁹ **Origen (c. 185-254), *Commentarii in Epistolam ad Romanos* III.3 (PG 14:942ff.)** — the *sola fides iustificat* saying at PG 14:952. The paradigm-Reformation-orthodox transmission is through **Erasmus's 1536 edition of Origen** and later through **Merlin's 1512 Paris edition.**

⁹⁰ **Ambrose (c. 340-397) — but properly *Ambrosiaster*** — *Commentarium in Epistolam ad Romanos* (PL 17:47ff., attributed pseudonymously to Ambrose in the manuscript tradition Selnecker uses). The paradigm-Reformation quotations from *"Ambrose on Romans"* are almost all from *Ambrosiaster,* whose true identity was only settled by **Erasmus's 1527 Basel edition of Augustine** (which noted the *pseudo-Ambrose* attribution). See **Souter, *A Study of Ambrosiaster*** (Cambridge 1905) and **Bammel, *Der Römerbriefkommentar des Ambrosiaster*** (in various *Reihe Judentum und Christentum,* 1963-1970). The *sola fide* citations are all authentic Ambrosiaster and are among the most important Reformation-era *sola-fide* patristic testimonies.

⁹¹ **Hilary of Poitiers (c. 315-367)** — the *fides sola iustificat* attribution — precise locus not identified in Hilary's extant corpus; may be **Hilary, *De Trinitate*** or **Hilary, *Tractatus super Psalmos*** (PL 9-10). Melanchthon similarly cites *Hilary sola fide* in *Loci* 1559 (CR 21:735). The specific dictum may be a Reformation-era Hilarian *florilegium* attribution rather than a verifiable Hilarian source.

⁹² **Victor Poeta** — likely **Claudius Marius Victor** (5th c. Gaul), author of the *Alethia,* a paraphrase of Genesis in three books. The specific Abraham couplet is at *Alethia* II (PL 61:963ff.). Reformation-era transmission via the **1560 Basel Fabricius edition** of Christian Latin poets.

⁹³ **Council of Trent, Session VI (13 January 1547), Canon 9 *De Iustificatione***: *"Si quis dixerit, sola fide impium iustificari, ita ut intelligat, nihil aliud requiri, quo ad iustificationis gratiam consequendam cooperetur, et nulla ex parte necesse esse, eum suae voluntatis motu praeparari atque disponi: anathema sit."* — Latin text in **Denzinger-Hünermann §1559**; English in **Tanner, *Decrees of the Ecumenical Councils*** II:679. Chemnitz's *Examen* Pars I *De Iustificatione* — Section on *Solus vs Sola Fide* — is the paradigm-Lutheran rebuttal of the whole Trent VI apparatus.

⁹⁴ **Selnecker, *Analectus*** (Leipzig 1568, expanded 1572) — one of Selnecker's earlier didactic-doctrinal works, referenced here by cross-reference; a companion to the *Institutio* as a compendium of dogmatic *loci-notes.* Cf. **VD16** S 5559 for the first edition.

⁹⁵ **Luther, *Rationis Latomianae confutatio*** (1521; WA 8:43-128 / *LW* 32:133-260) — the ***Antilatomus,*** Luther's paradigm treatise on the *sola fide* against Jacobus Latomus of Louvain. The *ring/gem* metaphor for faith-and-Christ is a stock Reformation-era Lutheran homiletic image, appearing frequently in **Luther, *Kirchenpostille*** on the Epiphany and Purification-of-Mary pericopes (WA 10/I.1:207ff.).

⁹⁶ **The *analogia geometrica* on Ephesians 4:7** — the paradigm-Melanchthonian rhetorical figure. Cf. **Melanchthon, *Loci* 1559** *De Ministerio* (CR 21:824). Selnecker's *"analogy in geometric-proportion, distributing to each what is congruent to his vocation"* echoes Melanchthon's rhetorical *loci-partitio* method.

⁹⁷ **Augustine on general vs special grace** — cf. **Augustine, *De correptione et gratia* 6-15** (PL 44:921-946); **Augustine, *De praedestinatione sanctorum* 3-11** (PL 44:962-975). The **operating/co-operating** distinction is at **Augustine, *De gratia et libero arbitrio* 17** (PL 44:901).

⁹⁸ **Pelagius (c. 354-420)** and the paradigm anti-Pelagian controversy — condemned at the Council of Carthage (418), Ephesus (431), Orange II (529). Selnecker's *"as today the Jesuits do the same"* is a paradigm sixteenth-century Reformation-era anti-Jesuit polemical trope, deriving from Melanchthon's *Antitheses adversus veterem Ecclesiam* (1552) and reiterated in Chemnitz's *Examen* Pars I. The Jesuit-Pelagian parallel became a standard confessional-Lutheran polemical *topos* through the 1580s-1620s (Selnecker at Leipzig, Hunnius at Wittenberg, later Gerhard at Jena).

⁹⁹ **Augustine, *De gratia et libero arbitrio* 17** (PL 44:901) — *"Deus operatur quidem in nobis velle, sed cum incipimus velle, tunc etiam nostrum est velle."* Also **Augustine, *Enchiridion* 32** (PL 40:248) — *"Nolentem praevenit ut velit, volentem subsequitur ne frustra velit."* These are the paradigm-Lutheran *anti-synergist* Augustinian citations in the FC II framework.

⁹⁹ᵃ **The three Papist grounds for doubt** — Selnecker's tripartite structure of the Papist objection is Chemnitz-*Examen*-standard and appears in **Chemnitz, *Examen* Pars I *De Certitudine Gratiae*** (Preus tr. 1971, I:428ff.).

¹⁰⁰ **The Hebrew *yēṣer sāmûk* / *ποίημα* Isa 26:3 analysis** — draws on **Sebastian Münster, *Hebraica Biblia*** (Basel 1534-1535) on Isaiah, and on **Johann Forster (1495-1558), *Dictionarium Hebraicum novum*** (Basel 1557) — Forster's Hebrew lexicon was the standard Wittenberg-Reformation Hebraist reference-work of Selnecker's generation. Forster taught Hebrew at Wittenberg 1548-1558 and produced the Hebrew apparatus for Luther's late Old Testament lectures. See **Newman, *Jewish Influence on Christian Reform Movements*** (Columbia 1925), ch. 8, on Forster's role.

¹⁰¹ **Council of Trent, Session VI, Canon 13 *De Iustificatione***: *"Si quis dixerit, omni homini ad remissionem peccatorum assequendam necessarium esse, ut credat certo et absque ulla haesitatione propriae infirmitatis et indispositionis peccata sibi esse remissa: anathema sit."* — **Denzinger-Hünermann §1563**. And Canon 14: *"Si quis dixerit, hominem a peccatis absolvi et iustificari ex eo, quod se absolvi et iustificari certo credat, aut neminem vere esse iustificatum, nisi qui credat se esse iustificatum: anathema sit"* — **§1564**. Chemnitz's *Examen* Pars I *De Certitudine* section is the paradigm-Lutheran anti-Trent rebuttal.

¹⁰² **Bernard, *De Fragmentis Septem, Sermo* 3** — precise reference not confidently identified in Bernard's *Opera*; the *charitas adoptionis, veritas promissionis, potestas redditionis* triadic structure is characteristic Bernardine and appears in various forms in his **Sermones in Cantica Canticorum** (PL 183:783ff.) and **Sermones de tempore.** Selnecker's citation matches the *Sermones super quaedam Verba* group in the *S. Bernardi Opera Omnia*, Paris 1544.
