# *Explicatio Decalogi* · Exposition of the Decalogue

<!-- p.45 -->

The DECALOGUE, so called from the number of the ten Commandments, is comprised in two Heads or two Tables, which are: *"Thou shalt love the Lord thy God with all thy heart, etc. And thy neighbour as thyself."*

The word *Decalogue* is taken from **Deut. 10**: *"The Lord wrote on the Tables the ten words, which the Lord spake, etc."*

For although the moral Law, as it is commonly named, was before known by nature, yet by the voice of God on mount Sinai, in the year 2454 from the creation of the World — that is to say, reckoning backward from this year 1571, three thousand and eighty years ago[^C1-1] — it was openly promulgated and repeated, for four causes. Of these the **First** is the Darkness (*Caligo*) which came on after the fall, and was thereafter made thicker and more dreadful, and obscured the natural knowledge.

The **Second** is the Testimony which God willed to stand forth, namely, that the natural notions were poured into man by Himself, and that the judgement of conscience which ariseth out of those notions is truly, by divine order, such that gladness accompanieth things rightly done, and torment of mind things ill done.

The **Third** is the Office of the law itself, whereby GOD willed the difference between righteousness and sin to be shown, and withal willed the law to be a Schoolmaster unto Christ, who both hath perfect righteousness according to the law, and, being innocent, hath perfectly paid the penalty for us, that the wrath of God against our sins might be appeased.

The **Fourth** cause is the Certainty of the life to come. For since the law is not fulfilled by us in this life, and yet the law must needs be wholly fulfilled, it is necessary that another life remain, in which nothing shall be wanting, either to obedience or to integrity. The righteousness of the blessed shall be entire. God shall be all in all. The punishment <!-- p.46 --> also of the damned shall be equal to the greatness of their sins, namely death and eternal malediction.

Now as often as we name the Decalogue, let us consider, first, what we understand by the word *Decalogue*. Secondly, since the wisdom of God set forth in the Decalogue cannot be exhausted, let us at least have always in view some admonitions, necessary and brief. Thirdly, let us see how the two Tables of the Decalogue are distinguished. Fourthly, what the Worship of God is.

## *De Primo* · On the First Point: What We Understand by the Word Decalogue

When we name the Decalogue, we understand the wisdom and righteousness in God, a ray whereof hath been poured into the minds of men, agreeing with the mind and will of GOD, and showing what manner of persons men ought to be, and pronouncing those righteous who are such, and condemning all who are not such. For the Law of GOD is the eternal and — considered in itself — immovable wisdom in God, and the Rule of righteousness, discerning things right and not right, willing the right, and with dreadful wrath destroying the contrary; the knowledge of which was implanted in the rational creatures at the creation, and afterward often repeated and declared by the Divine Voice, and promulgated anew on mount Sinai with great Majesty, and confirmed by miracles: that we may know that GOD is, who He is, and what manner of God He is, and that He is judge, avenger and punisher, and bindeth all rational creatures, and willeth all to be conformed to Himself and to have entire obedience according to the whole law, and accuseth, punisheth and condemneth, in this life and after this life with eternal punishments, all in whom there is not entire agreement, and promiseth rewards to the obedient.

## *De Secundo* · On the Second Point: Four Admonitions

Let these four admonitions be held fast. **I.** The Law truly testifieth <!-- p.47 --> that GOD is, and what manner of God He is, and that He is judge and punisheth the guilty. **II.** The Law showeth also to what end the nature of men was created, and what manner of nature it ought to be, seeing that God willeth men to be His image, and willeth our mind and will to agree with the Rule of His own mind and with His most righteous will. **III.** The Law showeth us our misery, sin, the wrath of GOD, the cause of calamities, the cross, and death, seeing that that wisdom and righteousness is lost in which, and unto which, we were created. **IIII.** The Law leadeth us to seek deliverance from all evils, since it is impossible that the whole human race was created unto eternal destruction, and that the law was set forth by the voice of GOD in vain. The Law is therefore a reminder concerning the Mediator, who fulfilleth the law by His own obedience, and turneth the punishment upon Himself, and restoreth in us righteousness and a life conformed to the law, and establisheth the law and purgeth it from errors. Therefore Paul saith: *"The end, or consummation, of the Law is CHRIST, for righteousness to every one that believeth."*

## *De Tertio* · On the Third Point: The Two Tables of the Decalogue

There are two Tables of the Decalogue, as hath been said. The former, containing three Commandments, speaketh of the true acknowledgment (*agnitio*) whereby God is acknowledged, shining Himself in us and communicating Himself to us, and whereby we in turn love Him, rejoice in Him, and are made one with Him; that is, the former Table commandeth the inward and outward works by which we deal immediately with God. For the first Commandment preacheth of the Acknowledgment of God in the mind, and of the motions, or affections, in the will and heart. The second speaketh of the profession or confession of that acknowledgment and of those affections. The third establisheth the whole Ministry, whereby the heavenly doctrine, and the outward worships which God hath instituted, are propagated.

<!-- p.48 -->

The latter Table, containing seven Commandments, showeth what manner of Order there ought to be in all the parts of man, and among men themselves, that there may be conformity with God; that is, the latter Table containeth the works toward men, which, although they are the bonds of human society, yet God most severely requireth this obedience also, and judgeth it to be worship rendered to God. Therefore Christ saith in the Gospel: *"The second is like unto the former"* — not indeed in order, nor in object, nor in the degree of love, since the love of God is prior in order, and worthier in object, and higher in degree than the love of man (for God is to be loved before all, ahead of all things, and above all things); but it is like in affection, and in obligation, and in the consequence of obedience. For as touching the affection, each commandment requireth pure love. Each, next, bindeth either to obedience or to punishment. And as touching the consequence of obedience, John saith: *"He that saith he loveth God, and hateth his neighbour, is a liar."* And since the law is copulative, John addeth: *"He that loveth God, let him love his brother also."* This also is to be added: whatsoever things are done to a neighbour, Christ testifieth that they are done to Himself, saying: *"What ye have done to one of the least of these who believe in me, ye have done to me."* And **Acts 7**:[^C1-2] *"Saul, Saul, why persecutest thou me?"* Now this is the order of the commandments: The fourth commandment instituteth, confirmeth and fortifieth the common society unto which man was created, and ordaineth the governors thereof. The fifth guardeth the bodies and life of individual men. The sixth defendeth marriages. The seventh fortifieth each man's house, dwelling, and the things necessary for the sustenance of life. The eighth defendeth good name, and ordereth the courts of judgement, which are the sinews and the execution of justice. The ninth and tenth add the declaration, that the Law <!-- p.49 --> of God commandeth not only concerning outward works, but also accuseth and condemneth depraved concupiscence.

We retain the customary division of the commandments. For there are those who make four commandments of the first table and six of the second, separating in the first table the commandment of not making idols from the first commandment, and joining together in the second table the commandments which forbid concupiscence, the ninth and tenth, as is evident from **Nazianzen** and **Ambrose**. Now the whole division of the decalogue tendeth to this, that we distinguish immediate obedience toward God from mediate. For although the whole decalogue in general commandeth obedience toward God, yet as the former table demandeth immediate obedience, so the latter demandeth mediate.

## *De Quarto* · On the Fourth Point: What the Worship of God Is

At the outset this rule is to be held: No worship pleaseth God, save that which He Himself hath instituted by His own word, as it is written: *"In vain they do worship me with the commandments of men."* And: *"Whatsoever is not of faith is sin."* Now faith resteth upon a commandment and a manifest word. The use of this rule is threefold. The first establisheth the authority of the lawgiver and supreme Lord, who saith: *"I am the Lord thy God."* But the authority of a superior Lord groweth cheap, if it be free to others also to frame laws which ought to be worships of God. Therefore God saith: *"Walk ye in my commandments."* Likewise: *"Add nothing, and take nothing away."* The second use delivereth us from every error, heathen, pharisaic and Pontifical, namely that we err not in worshipping God, nor heap up superstitions and ***ἐθελοθρησκείας*** (self-chosen worships). The third use maketh us cheerful to perform those things which God requireth, since we are certain of His will and of His help.

This rule being laid down, let the foundation of the worship of God now be <!-- p.50 --> considered, which lieth both in the knowledge of God and in the knowledge of ourselves. The knowledge of God we obtain by the word, and by the testimony which is added to the word; which two conjoined teach us these four things: first, that God is the fountain of all wisdom, righteousness, truth and power; secondly, that all glory is to be ascribed to God; thirdly, that God is by His own nature most ready to help; fourthly, that He willeth all to flee unto Him in any peril whatsoever. But the knowledge of ourselves is to be sought from three things, namely from the end of creation, or the consideration of the image of God unto which man was created; and from the estimation of our present powers; and from the easiness of falling into sin. The end teacheth what our duty is, wherefore we were created, and wherefore we live, to wit, that we should bear the image of God in all holiness, sincere godliness, purity and true righteousness. Our powers show that we cannot perform the duty which is owed. The easiness and frequency of falling admonisheth us of our frailty and sin. This knowledge tendeth to this, that, all matter of glorying in ourselves being cast away, we ascribe all glory to God alone, and acknowledge our weakness and filthiness, and pray that we may be preserved every moment by the hand of God — we who, left to ourselves, are every moment borne headlong, again and again, into new sins.

From these things it can now be shown, in this manner, what the worship of God is: The worship of God is a work commanded by God, done in the acknowledgment of, and trust in, the Son of God, and referred principally to this end, that obedience and honour be rendered to God, that is, a testifying that we truly hold that He alone is truly God whom we thus worship, and that He willeth so to be worshipped.

This definition is taken of the worships commanded to the human race, that is, of *eucharistic* worships. For worship is twofold: *expiatory* or meritorious, that is, the active and passive obedience of the Son of God, by which He alone hath merited for us grace, righteousness, <!-- p.51 --> the gift of the Holy Spirit, life and eternal salvation; and *eucharistic*, which belongeth to all the Sons of God, and of itself meriteth nothing, but of grace, for the Son's sake, hath the promises of the present and of the eternal life. Now in the definition of the worship of God there is shown, first, what is, so to speak, the matter of the worship of God, namely, a work commanded by God, according to the rule delivered above: *"Thou shalt not add anything, nor take away."* Likewise: *"To the law, and to the testimony."* Secondly, it is necessary that faith be added, by which the person also pleaseth God, and which is the worker of the good work, and performeth it of its own accord and gladly, and maketh the true difference between the true worship of God — such as is the sacrifice of Abel, and the alms of Cornelius, and the obedience of all believers — and false worship, such as is the sacrifice of Cain, and the alms of the Pharisee, and the works of all who lack faith. Thirdly, the work commanded, and proceeding from faith, ought to tend principally to the celebration of the glory of God; namely, that he who rendereth worship to God think rightly of the essence, will and majesty of God, and ascribe to Him all glory, wisdom, mercy and righteousness, and obey Him, and celebrate Him with heart, mouth, confession, works and manners, and by his own example invite others to celebrate God, and ascribe to Christ alone the glory of redemption, and the Majesty, and arrogate and claim nothing to himself, or to his own wisdom, fortune and counsels, according to that of Isaiah: *"Every one that calleth upon my name, I have created him for my glory, I have formed him; yea, I have made him."* And: *"Let your light so shine before men, that your Father which is in heaven may be glorified."*

Now I add the solution of a question.

**Whether any outward work also ought to be called worship of God, seeing that all worship ought to be spiritual?**

I answer: Faith joineth together the worship of the soul and the outward working, and showeth that one worship <!-- p.52 --> is inward, which is a motion of the mind and heart agreeing with the law of God; and that another is outward, namely, when the outward action agreeth with the law and will of God. Now all worship ought to be spiritual, whether it be inward or outward — not indeed by reason of the outward object about which a man putteth forth his obedience, even as the apple in paradise did not make the worship spiritual; but by reason of the efficient cause, namely, God Himself; and by reason of faith, which the Spirit worketh unto the glory of God, who is spirit, and from which the worship proceedeth; and by reason of the final cause, or the glory of God. Whatsoever worships, therefore, are done according to the word of God, of faith, unto the glory of God, are spiritual, and are done by those who are sons of God, that is, who believe in Christ, and through faith have access unto the Father, and therefore can rightly worship God, because they have access unto Him, and are Sons.

What things besides seem needful to be said concerning the Decalogue and the Mosaic law — namely, of how many kinds it is, how it is abrogated, whether any man can satisfy the law, what and how manifold the use of the law is, for what causes God appointed the ceremonial and judicial laws, and what are the chief differences of the law and the Gospel (all and each of which are indeed to be diligently examined in the exposition of the Decalogue) — of these we have treated above, in the locus *On the Law* and in other loci. Now we must speak of the commandments, all which must needs be done in the acknowledgment and faith of the Mediator, and concerning which these five things are to be considered: What works are demanded: How they can be done, and please: The promises added to the law: The Antithesis, that is, what sins are forbidden: The threatenings.

## *Primum Praeceptum* · The First Commandment

### *Ego sum Dominus Deus tuus* · I Am the Lord Thy God

These words confirm the authority of the whole law, God being <!-- p.53 --> shown to be its author. And this is the first principle of Theology, which all ministers of the divine word, nay, all Christians, ought always to have in view, namely, that they consider whether *Jehovah* speaketh. For there can be no certainty of doctrine, no faith, no sure hope, unless it be established that the Lord speaketh. Therefore the Prophets and Apostles confirm their calling by the voice of God: *"Thus saith the Lord"*; *"The mouth of the Lord hath spoken it"*; *"Paul, an Apostle of Jesus Christ,"* etc.

Next, these very words show who the true God is, and how He is to be acknowledged, and distinguished from all feigned deities. And it is necessary that this knowledge go before all others. For unless the true God be acknowledged, all the rest that is commanded concerning invocation and the other worships, or concerning the whole of piety toward God and toward men, is undertaken in vain.

Hither pertaineth the whole Doctrine of God, of the revelation (*patefactio*) of God, of the end of man, of the difference of true and false invocation, of the one essence of God, of the three persons, of the two natures in Christ, of the Holy Spirit, etc., of which above.

### *Qui eduxi te de terra Aegypti* · Which Have Brought Thee Out of the Land of Egypt

The manner of acknowledging God consisteth in word and testimony. Of the word we have already heard: *"I am Jehovah thy God."* The testimony is: *"Which have brought thee out of Egypt."* Here the two ways of acknowledging God — namely, the Word, whose principal parts are the law and the Gospel, and likewise the testimonies, that is, all the works of God which God hath in all times wrought in the human race for the confirming of His word — are to be diligently considered, and always had in view, that it may be certain who God is, and what the doctrine and will of the true God is.

<!-- p.54 -->

Now mention is made of the bringing out of Egypt, first, for this cause, that by this work and testimony the power, Majesty, Promise and Truth of the true God may be declared, and that this immense benefit may show how great He is who saith: *"I am Jehovah"*; and how kind and merciful that same One is, who saith: *"The Lord thy God,"* that is, regarding thee and caring for thee, giving thee the word, hearing thee when thou callest upon Him, ready to succour thee in this life and after this life. Next, mention is made of the bringing out of Egypt for this cause also, that the spiritual deliverance and eternal redemption from the captivity, tyranny and bonds of the devil, of death and of hell may be signified to us and impressed upon us, and that Christ, the leader and deliverer, the true Joshua, leading us into the land flowing with milk and honey, that is, into eternal life, may be acknowledged, invoked and celebrated by us.

For this cause, even as the patriarchs, the Fathers and others knew that in invocation they addressed this true God, who had revealed Himself in the promises and in the bringing out of Egypt, and asked, and believed, that they were received and heard for the sake of the promised seed, and separated their invocation from the religions of all other nations, which knew not the promises: so we in the new testament invoke the same true God, and add the testimonies of the sending of the Son into our flesh, and of the deliverance from all evil, and separate our invocation from the muttering and enthusiasm of all Heathen and idolaters, and flee and abhor all worships instituted without the word of God, all doctrine, and all religions that conflict with the word delivered through the prophets, Christ and the Apostles. For we acknowledge, fear, love and worship this one only true God, Jehovah, the father of our Lord Jesus Christ, and the Son Jesus Christ, and the Holy Spirit, who hath revealed Himself unto us, and hath delivered His word, and hath confirmed it by most illustrious testimonies, <!-- p.55 --> namely, the bringing out of Egypt, then the incarnation of the Son, the raising of the dead, the giving of the Holy Spirit, and other miracles.

Let it be diligently considered, moreover, how the authority of the divine law is confirmed, namely, in three ways. 1. From power: *"I am Jehovah."* 2. From the fatherly promise: *"Thy God and thy Lord."* 3. From the benefit: *"I brought thee out of Egypt."*

What manner of name *Jehovah* is, and of how great moment, we have said elsewhere. He alone is ***αὐτοφυὴς καὶ παντοδύναμος*** (self-existent and almighty), and the one only lawgiver, instituting true worships. He testifieth that He is our God, and promiseth us His presence, His working, His help, His omnipotence and His mercy. For a testimony of this, He performeth in very deed that which He hath promised, and bodily leadeth the Jewish people out of Egypt, and spiritually delivereth the whole human race from the captivity of death, of sin and of hell through the Son, and giveth us the Holy Spirit and eternal Life.

### *Non habebis Deos alienos coram me* · Thou Shalt Have No Other Gods Before Me

For the sake of teaching, this rule for children is wont to be delivered: that the proposition or sentence of a commandment is twofold, namely, affirmative and negative, so that by antithesis the one may illustrate the other. First, therefore, it is shown what virtues are required; then, what sins are forbidden; that is, what is to be done, and pleaseth God, and what is not to be done, and pleaseth not God. So the first commandment commandeth that we have the true God; then, that we have not strange Gods.

In the affirmative sentence, which willeth that we have the true God, these chief virtues are contained:

<!-- p.56 -->

- The true acknowledgment of God.
- Faith.
- Love of God.
- Hope.
- Fear of God.
- Humility.
- Patience.
- Perseverance.

**The true acknowledgment of God** (*vera agnitio Dei*) is so to acknowledge the essence and will of God as He hath revealed Himself by word and testimonies, and to distinguish God from feigned deities, and not to think otherwise of God, and of all and each of the articles of faith, than as He Himself hath delivered in the prophetic and Apostolic writings.

**Faith** is the knowledge (*notitia*) of the whole Doctrine delivered unto us by God, and the assent whereby we embrace the whole doctrine, and in it the promise of reconciliation, or of the imputation of righteousness to be given for Christ's sake; and it is trust resting in Christ the mediator according to the promise, and certainly holding that to oneself sins are remitted, that one pleaseth God, is loved of God, heard, defended, and received unto eternal life, for the sake of Christ the mediator, freely, not for one's own worthiness. Now it is to be considered that Faith is a virtue both legal and Evangelical; and that, understood correlatively, as when we say that we are justified by faith alone, it pertaineth properly to the preaching of the Gospel; but that in general it is, and is called, a work of the first commandment. For absolutely it pertaineth to the law, inasmuch as it is a quality. But relatively, inasmuch as it apprehendeth the merit of the Son, and applieth unto man the obedience and benefits of the Son, so that man is said to live, to be justified and to be saved by his faith, faith pertaineth only to the Gospel.

**Love of God** is, the mercy of God <!-- p.57 --> promised for the Son's sake having been apprehended through faith, to subject oneself to God, and to keep His commandments with a certain gladness that resteth in Him.

**Hope** is the sure expectation of eternal life for the sake of the Son of God, according to the promise expressed in the Gospel; and it is the expectation of the mitigation of punishments or calamities in this life, according to the Counsel of God. Now faith and hope differ in three ways: in their objects, in their office, and in their subjects. For as touching the objects, faith receiveth a present benefit, or present reconciliation; but hope expecteth a future good, or future deliverance: which in the schools they are wont to express neatly, when they say that faith looketh upon *the word of the thing* (*verbum rei*), but hope upon *the thing of the word* (*rem verbi*). The word of the thing is the promises. The thing of the word is the good things promised, spiritual and bodily. Next, the office of faith is to apprehend the promise, and to apply Christ unto us. This neither hope nor the other virtues do. And on account of this office, which faith alone performeth, we say in the Church: That we are justified by faith alone. But in their subjects faith and hope differ thus: that the former is in the mind knowledge and assent, and in the will trust; but the latter is only in the will and heart. Yet it sometimes cometh to pass that these words — trust, faith, hope, expectation — are used without distinction, because they are neighbouring affections.

**Filial Fear of God** is to tremble at the thought of the wrath of God against sin, and through fear of the divine judgement to flee sin, and reverently to subject oneself to God, and withal to be raised up by trust in mercy, lest in terror we fall away from God, and to draw near unto God, and to rest in God as in a father. But *servile* fear is terror without faith, fleeing God, and raging against God, as shall be said a little after in the negatives.

**Humility** is truly to fear God, and to acknowledge one's own uncleanness and infirmity, and yet in faith to obey one's calling, and to expect help and deliverance from God, and not <!-- p.58 --> to seek after greater things than accord with one's calling, nor to ascribe prosperous outcomes to one's own powers, nor to despise others, through whom God is able to accomplish things even more profitable.

**Patience** is to obey God in bearing adversities, so that we do nothing against the commandments of God, nor are angry with God, but by faith acknowledge that He is propitious unto us, and expect help, mitigation or deliverance, and by this faith and hope assuage our grief.

**Perseverance** is to abide in the true acknowledgment of God and in faith, constantly and perpetually, even to the last breath of life.

He therefore who hath these virtues hath God, according to the first commandment, with mind, affection, will, and all his powers. In the mind is the true knowledge of God; in the affection is faith, love, hope, fear. And from the pure judgement of the mind and the pure affection of the soul is born the readiness of the will to command all the powers, that they render obedience to God.

Now since the law commandeth that these virtues be perfect in us, and free from sin, while we know that in this depravation human nature resisteth the law of God, it is rightly and necessarily asked, how the works of this commandment, and of the other commandments also of the whole decalogue, can be done, and are done. Now concerning this very question it hath been said, in the doctrine of good works, after this manner: that the outward works of this commandment can be understood and done even by the unregenerate, that is, that a man can learn and profess the true doctrine with an outward profession, and flee idols in outward gesture; but that the inward obedience cannot be begun and done otherwise, than if first each man acknowledge his own weakness, and hate and flee sins, and flee for refuge to Christ, the mediator and expiator of sin, and, having heard the promise of reconciliation, believe that his sins are remitted him, and that he is received for the mediator's sake, freely, and, <!-- p.59 --> having obtained remission of sins by faith, the Holy Spirit being given, return to this commandment, and begin to render new obedience to God. For these are the beginnings of obedience, and begun obedience pleaseth God in the reconciled for the sake of the mediator, who is as it were a covering shade, and covereth the uncleanness that remaineth in us, according to that saying: *"Blessed are they whose sins are covered."*

### *Antithesis* · The Antithesis

The affirmative sentence having been expounded, let the prohibition now be recited: *"Thou shalt have no other gods before me."* Now other gods, or strange gods (*Dii alieni*), are all feigned idols, namely, opinion and imagination concerning God outside the word, and every worship instituted without the word of God, and whatsoever a man feigneth, from which he hopeth for help without the true and filial fear of the true God: for which cause St. Paul nameth covetousness idolatry.

In this antithesis and negative are comprehended all the sins that conflict with the first commandment. Thus with the acknowledgment of God conflict ignorance of God, and all errors concerning God, Epicurean and Stoic, Academic and others. With faith conflict doubtings concerning God, and concerning providence, and concerning the word; likewise distrust, despair, confidence or presumption, trust in human defences, idolatry, superstition, magic. With the love of God conflict ***ἀταξία*** (disorder) of the affections in man, depraved inclinations, hatred of God, indignation against the judgement of God, especially in afflictions, and stupor. With hope conflict despair and presumption. With the fear of God conflict security, superstition, Epicurean contempt of God. With humility conflict hypocrisy, pride, ambition, boasting, self-love (*philautia*), envy, emulation, ***ὕβρις*** (insolence). With patience conflict impatience, murmuring, hypocritical endurance, hardening, stupor. <!-- p.60 --> With perseverance conflict apostasy, and obstinacy in impious opinions and superstitions.

But that all and each of these may be treated, there is need of a special labour of sermons. Let students, however, have at hand the *Rules of Life* written by **D. Chytraeus**.[^C1-3] Let students also consider the six degrees of sins against the first commandment, in which all these are included. The **first** degree is the madness of atheists, who either deny that there is a God, or deny that God is the creator of the human race, or deny His providence and judgement, and ascribe all things to second causes, or strengthen doubt. The **second** degree is that of idolaters, who worship as God something which is not God, or adorn and attend creatures with divine worship, or adore statues, or invoke saints, or set other things before themselves as the mark of their refuge, as the covetous trust in their riches; or who ascribe to their own good works, maimed things and filth, the power of justifying and saving; or who in other ways trust in self-chosen worships, that for the sake of these God willeth to be propitious, as are idolatrous masses, superstitious fasts, self-chosen ceremonies, and the ***ἐθελοθρησκείαι*** (self-chosen worships) of Monks, Canons and the like idolaters, who lack the word of God. The **third** degree is that of all blasphemers and heretics, Mahometans, Arians, Nestorians, Eutychians and the like, who will not so acknowledge God as He hath revealed Himself, the Son being sent, in His word delivered unto us through the prophets and Apostles. The **fourth** degree is that of Magicians, and of all who consult Magicians; likewise of impious consecrations, as of oil, salt and other things. The **fifth** is that of hypocrites, that is, of the secure, the proud, the impatient, and those who admire themselves. The **sixth** is that of apostates, or of all those who in any manner whatsoever — whether by doubt and distrust, or by defection, or by the devising of new worships, <!-- p.61 --> or in other ways — neglect the Gospel, and do not with firm Faith assent to the divine promise, so as to give unto God the glory of truth, but will have sense go before faith.

Let it suffice to have noted these things concerning the virtues and vices of which the first commandment treateth. But let two rules be added. The former is this: The first commandment is to be included thereafter in all the commandments that follow, in which regard is always to be had to the first commandment. As: Obey thy parents — namely, that thou mayest obey God. The latter rule is: The first commandment is the supreme law, and the governess of the other commandments. And the authority of the first commandment is to be preferred before a lower commandment, as Abraham preferreth the slaying of his Son, which was commanded him, before the lower commandment. And all the godly prefer the first commandment before the command of a magistrate who commandeth them to receive the idolatrous worships of Masses.

These things being finished, we shall here add a few things for the sake of students.

1. Of the causes for which, in the Psalms and elsewhere, mention is often made of the bringing out of Egypt, which came to pass in the year of the world 2453, 1510 years before Christ was born Man: in this year of Salvation 1571 it is 3080 years.
2. Of idolatry, and the origin of idols.
3. Of images, whether they are to be utterly taken away, and of the threefold use of images.
4. Of the threatening and the promise added to the first commandment.

### *De Primo* · On the First Point: Why Mention Is So Often Made of the Bringing Out of Egypt

The **first cause** is the Majesty and dignity of the History itself, to which, after the incarnation and Resurrection of Christ, no History in this life is to be compared. Many and great miracles are done in Egypt, and all the firstborn are slain in one night <!-- p.62 --> by the Angel of God; by which, nevertheless, the heart of Pharaoh is not bent to render willing obedience to God. Six hundred thousand men, the women and children not being reckoned in, go forth out of Egypt, whose number had grown to so great a sum in the space of four hundred years. For Jacob the Patriarch had brought in only seventy persons. The Red sea giveth way on either side, and is dried up, and, gathered together on either hand, standeth as a wall, until the people pass through for the space of twenty miles, and Pharaoh with his men is drowned.

The **second cause** is the revelation of God Himself, who by these miracles showeth these five things. 1. That He Himself is the only true God, to whom all things are subject, who hath created and sustaineth all things, as He saith: *"I am Jehovah thy God, which have brought thee out of the land of Egypt."* 2. That the doctrine which God Himself delivered to this people is certain and true, and doth not only sound of itself, but hath also been confirmed by mighty testimonies of miracles, and that therefore all other opinions and religions which conflict with this doctrine are feigned and vain. 3. That He is a most free agent, and is able to change the nature of things, and to do what He willeth, against the accustomed order of nature and second causes. 4. That the promises of God concerning the defence of His own, and deliverance out of perils, are true, certain and immovable. 5. That the threatenings of punishments divinely set forth do also certainly come to pass, and that God at length punisheth and overthroweth the ungodly, as He destroyed Pharaoh.

The **third cause** is, that the whole church and all the godly in all times may be stirred up and confirmed in these things. 1. That they may acknowledge this true God, and believe His word and promises, who hath revealed Himself in the sending of the Son, in the bringing out of Egypt, and in other miracles. 2. That they may certainly hold that the Church and themselves are the care of God, and that they are received, heard and helped, <!-- p.63 --> although they be destitute of all second causes. 3. That they may give thanks to God for the defence and deliverance of the godly, and celebrate Him with godly praises. 4. That in all perils they may betake themselves unto God, and seek help from Him, and know that they are not by their own powers sufficient or fit to drive away perils, but that help is from God. 5. That they may instruct others also, and invite them to the fear of God and to faith, and hand down to posterity the true doctrine confirmed by so many miracles. 6. That they may live in the fear of God and in true humility, knowing that God seeth and heareth all things, and punisheth sins. 7. That they may be patient in afflictions, when they are pressed, and ascribe unto God the praise of truth in the promises of deliverances and in the threatenings of punishments.

The **fourth cause** lieth in the types, of which there are many. For that bringing out of Egypt was a type of the true deliverance and bringing out from the house of bondage, that is, from sin, the curse of the law and death, through the red sea, that is, through the blood of the Son of God, into the promised land of the heavenly and eternal life. Next, it was a type of Baptism, as Paul showeth, **1 Cor. 10**: *"All our fathers passed through the sea, and were all baptized unto Moses in the cloud and in the sea"*; that is, they were received with everlasting testimonies, and were baptized by the ministry of Moses, the cloud covering the people, and the waves standing in the sea. Whence **Epiphanius** saith: *"From Moses and the sea began the type of Baptism."* Afterward it was also a type and image of the perils, and of the deliverance and preservation, of the church of all times. For the godly are always going forth out of Egypt, that is, they leave the kingdom of the Devil and of the world, and follow the Son of God. But while they do this, they come into the wilderness, and are pressed with many miseries, with want, contempt, hatreds, perils, persecutions. Yet, the Son of God leading and governing them, through the midst of the sea, through forests, through rocks, through fires (although they be drowned, hanged, stoned, <!-- p.64 --> burned), they come safe and sound into the haven of life, of gladness and of eternal glory.

### *De Secundo, id est, de idololatria, et origine idolorum* · On the Second Point, That Is, of Idolatry and the Origin of Idols

An idol (from ***εἴδομαι***, "I seem, and am made like, and bear an appearance before me", whence also ***εἶδος***, *species* and *idea*) is properly a likeness and image, or a shadowy effigy, as is said in the verses of **Sophocles** concerning the weak and wretched nature of man:[^C1-4]

> ***ὁρῶ γὰρ ἡμᾶς οὐδὲν ὄντας ἄλλο, πλὴν***  
> ***εἴδωλ᾽ ὅσοι περ ζῶμεν ἢ κούφην σκιάν.***  

> As many of us as in this world draw the breath of life,  
> Dust and a fleeting shadow, and phantoms are we.  

> ***τοιαῦτα τοίνυν εἰσορῶν, ὑπέρκοπον,***  
> ***μηδέν ποτ᾽ εἴπῃς αὐτὸς εἰς θεοὺς ἔπος.***  
> ***μὴ δ᾽ ὄγκον ἄρῃ, μηδέν᾽ εἴ τινος πλέον***  
> ***ἢ χειρὶ βρίθεις ἢ μακροῦ πλούτου βάθει.***  
> ***ὡς ἡμέρα κλίνει τε κἀνάγει πάλιν***  
> ***ἅπαντα τἀνθρώπεια. τοὺς δὲ σώφρονας***  
> ***θεοὶ φιλοῦσι, καὶ στυγοῦσι τοὺς κακούς.***  

For **Suidas** citeth these verses, which our **D. Wolf**[^C1-5] hath rendered thus: *"I see that we are nothing else but phantoms (that is, the effigy of a form, vainer than the vainest things), whosoever of us live, or a light shadow. Beholding such things, therefore, never speak thou any lofty word against God; nor be thou in any wise puffed up, if anywhere thou excellest others either in hand or in abundance of great wealth: for a day boweth down, and bringeth back again, all human things; but the modest God loveth, and hateth the wicked."*

The Greeks distinguish ***εἴδωλα*** and ***ὁμοιώματα***. The former they say are ***τῶν οὐκ ὑφεστώτων μιμήματα***, effigies of things that do not subsist, as are Tritons, Sphinxes, Centaurs, Mars, Pallas, Neptune, Juno, and the like Gods of the nations, of whom David saith, **Ps. 96**: *"All the gods of the nations are idols, but the Lord made the heavens."* Idols, *Ililim*, *Al Elim*, that is, not Gods, *Götzen*.[^C1-6] <!-- p.65 --> And Paul saith that an idol is nothing in the world, **1 Cor. 8**, that is, that it is a certain feigned and invented thing, under which no reality lieth, and that it neither profiteth its worshippers nor harmeth its despisers. But ***ὁμοιώματα*** they say are ***τῶν ὑφεστώτων εἰκάσματα***, likenesses which are of things that exist, as the images of particular men, or of beasts.

Now since the heathen worshipped images as deities, it therefore prevailed, by a certain catachresis, that every perverse worship of God was called idolatry, as it were ***τῶν εἰδώλων λατρεία*** (the worship of idols). For ***λατρεία*** is worship; which word the Grammarians derive from the particle ***λα***, "greatly", and ***τρεῖν***, "to fear" or "to tremble".

Idolatry therefore is, of one's own counsel, without the express word of God, either to devise new or feigned Gods, or to ascribe unto creatures the honour due to God, or to bind God to Statues and Images fashioned by the hands of men, or to choose worships without the word of God, and to trust in them, that for the sake of them God is propitious.

This description showeth that there are four species of idolatry. The **first** is to feign something to be God which is not God, as to worship Statues as Deities, or to devise divers Deities, as the Heathen feigned Jupiter, Neptune, Pluto and other Gods, to wit, thirty thousand idols, and among these three hundred Jupiters. And it is well known that there was among the Heathen a threefold order of Gods, of which the first comprehendeth the heavenly Gods, out of whom twelve were named the chief and select Gods, concerning whom the Verses of **Ennius** are extant:

> Juno, Vesta, Minerva, Ceres, Diana, Venus, Mars,  
> Mercury, Jove, Neptune, Vulcan, Apollo.  

The second order contained the *Medioximi*,[^C1-7] that is, Heroic and eminent men, whose souls they supposed to live partly in the air, partly <!-- p.66 --> on the earth, ***ἠέρα ἑσσαμένους, πάντῃ φοιτῶντας ἐπ᾽ αἶαν*** (clothed in mist, roaming everywhere over the earth), as **Hesiod** saith.[^C1-8] The third, Pluto with his Tartarean retinue.

Hither are to be referred those also who turn outward signs, sacrifices and Sacraments into Gods, as did the Chaldeans and Persians, who adored Fire, and the Papists, who carry about the Bread, which they affirm to be God.

The **second** species is to ascribe divine honour unto creatures, either rational, as to invoke the Saints; or irrational, and these either animate, as to worship Storks, Cats, Bulls, Serpents; or inanimate, as the covetous man is an idolater, **Eph. 5**.

The **third** species is to bind God to things which He hath not set forth by His word, as is the adoration of statues, in which either Christ or the Saints are thought to be found, as is said in the verses of the Venetians:

> Lo, what the image teacheth is God, yet is it not God Himself:  
> Look thou on this, but worship in thy mind that which thou discernest in it.  

The **fourth** species is to choose worships without the word of God, as necessary to salvation, even as the Pontificials have feigned Monastic works, Masses, Fasts and other ceremonies.

The efficient causes of idolatry are chiefly two: the Devil blinding the minds of men, and the blindness of the mind of man departing from the word of God.

The impelling causes are: 1. Distrust toward God. 2. Impatience of delay in afflictions. 3. Excessive trust in human defences, in one's own wisdom, in ***ἐθελοθρησκείας*** and self-chosen worships. 4. Security, not earnestly fearing the wrath and judgement of God. 5. ***κακοζηλία*** (perverse emulation) and the imitation of other nations. 6. The affections, as flattery, as shall be said below; even as Solomon, led by the love of Heathen women, worshipped Idols.

### *Successus eorum, qui colunt Idola* · The Outcome of Those Who Worship Idols

The effects are a great mass of sins and shameful deeds, which are recounted in the first Chapter to the **Romans**, and of which it will have to be spoken in order elsewhere.

Now the doctrine concerning the origin of Idols can be easily delivered and truly shown out of **Lactantius**, who in book 1, chap. 15, of the *Divine Institutes* saith thus: *"Since it is established that Saturn, Jupiter and the other Gods were men, it is not obscure by what means they began to be named Gods. For if there were no Kings before Saturn or Uranus, on account of the fewness of men, who lived a rustic life without a Ruler, there is no doubt but that in those times men began to extol the King himself, and his whole race, with the highest praises and with new honours, so that they even called them Gods, whether on account of the wonder of their virtue, or, as men are wont, in flattery of present power, or on account of the benefits by which they had been brought to civility. Afterward the Kings themselves, since they had been dear to those whose life they had set in order, left behind them, when dead, a great longing for themselves: and so men fashioned likenesses of them, that they might have some solace from the contemplation of the images; and going further, through love of their desert they began to honour the memory of the departed, that they might seem to render thanks to those who had deserved well, and might allure their successors to the desire of ruling well; which **Cicero** teacheth in the books* On the Nature of the Gods, *saying: 'Now the common custom of life hath taken this upon itself, to raise up to heaven, in fame and in goodwill, men who excelled in benefits. Hence Hercules, hence Castor, hence Pollux, hence Aesculapius, hence Liber.' And in another place:[^C1-9] 'And in very many cities it may be understood that, for the sake of sharpening virtue, or that every best man might the more willingly face peril for the Commonwealth, the memory of brave men was consecrated with the honour of the immortal Gods.'"*

<!-- p.68 -->

Thus far **Lactantius**, who also addeth these things: *"The Poets also came in, and, with Songs composed to give pleasure, lifted them up to heaven: which evil took its rise from the Greeks, whose levity, furnished with the faculty and abundance of speaking, it is incredible how great clouds of lies it hath raised. And so, admiring them, they were the first both to take up their sacred rites, and to hand them on to all nations. On account of this vanity the Sibyl thus rebuketh them:"*[^C1-10]

> ***Ἑλλὰς δὴ τί πέποιθας ἐπ᾽ ἀνδράσιν ἡγεμόνεσσι,***  
> ***Πρὸς τί δὲ δῶρα μάταια καταφθιμένοις ἀνατίθης;***  
> ***θύεις εἰδώλοις, τίς σοι πλάνην βάλεν ἐν νῷ;***  
> ***ὥς τέ σε τάδε ποιεῖν μεγάλοιο θεοῖο προσώπου λειπομένου.***  

That is:

> *"Greece, why trustest thou in men that are Princes? To what end dost thou set vain gifts before the dead? Thou sacrificest to Idols: who hath cast error into thy mind, that thou shouldest do these things, forsaking the face of the great GOD?"*

But let Students read the fifteenth Chapter of **Lactantius** already cited, and some that follow; and in book 2 of the *Divine Institutes*, Chap. 2, what was the first cause of fashioning Images; and in Chap. 4, that idols have nothing of majesty and reverence, and some that follow; to which let them join the hortatory discourse of **Justin Martyr** to the gentiles, and the oration of **Clement of Alexandria** exhorting the Greeks, or the Gentiles, and **Cyprian** *On Idols*.

Now it is evident from all Histories and Writings that the origin of images and likenesses was at the first more innocent, both among the Gentiles and among Christians. For at the first friends and subjects, languishing with an immoderate longing for their friends and lords, sought some solace in images, and in a manner set them before their own eyes. Gratitude, and the tenderness of love, excused the deed. But the crafty Demon straightway espied weapons fit for his own devices, <!-- p.69 --> and drove the minds of the foolish to this, that while they were held by a longing for men, they forgot God; and while they proclaimed the benefits of men, they obscured the immense benefits of God; and while they wished to be grateful toward servants, they were found ungrateful toward God. Satan, therefore, perceiving a spark put to the tinder, kindled a mighty fire, devising idolatry, in such wise that even to a lifeless thing the adoration which is owed to God was rendered, in memory of departed friends. This pleased the powerful, who judged that it was of very great moment for the confirming of their own authority. Whence also the Devil made those things which men had rendered august by a more elegant and more precious material to be venerable and dreadful, through the opinion of a present divinity, by deceitful oracles and the juggleries of miracles. To these things was added the tyranny of Kings, which destroyed those who reproved, and drove their subjects to impiety. Then those who came after, who saw their Fathers numbered among the Gods on account of certain eminent good deeds, being ambitious, lest they should be accounted less than those, readily admitted the same honour even while living, being inflamed by the counsels of flatterers — nay, they even demanded it.
