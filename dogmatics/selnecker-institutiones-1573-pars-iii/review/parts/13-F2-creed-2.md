### What is Conscience?

Conscience is a practical syllogism (*syllogismus practicus*) in the understanding, in which the major proposition is the Law of God, or whatsoever word of God commanding us anything; and the minor and the conclusion are the application, approving things rightly done, or condemning the offence: which approval is followed in the heart by gladness, and the condemnation by grief, according to the natural order established by God, who willeth that in the reasonable creature there be this knowledge of his judgement, and that the execution be added thereto, that it may be a testimony concerning himself, shewing that there is a God, and that he commandeth things just, and forbiddeth things unjust, and punisheth.[^F2-1]

### What is Contingency?

It is the issue of that thing which hath had causes that of their own nature were able to act otherwise.

Here let the schoolmasters expound unto the younger sort the degrees of necessity, and refute the Stoic fate (*Stoicum fatum*): concerning which to discourse here in a particular writing would be too prolix.

## *De Secundo Articulo Symboli Apostolici* · On the Second Article of the Apostles' Creed

### What is contained in the second Article of the Creed?

The doctrine of the person, that is, of the eternal divinity, and of the <!-- p.252 --> assumed humanity of Christ, and of the office of Christ, and of faith in him, which consisteth in the acknowledgement of the person and office of Christ.

### Distribute the second Article into its members.

1. *I believe in Jesus Christ, his only Son, our Lord.* — The divinity and the benefits of Christ.

The history of the humanity of Christ hath seven parts, in a threefold distinction, after this manner:

**1. Things past.**

1. The Conception and Nativity of Christ.
2. The Passion, Crucifixion, Death and Burial.
3. The Descent into hell.
4. The Resurrection from the dead.
5. The Ascension into heaven.

**2. Things present.**

6. The being in heaven, or, the Session at the right hand of the Father Almighty.

**3. Things to come.**

7. The Coming unto the universal judgement.

For that form shall be seen which the Son united unto himself by the mystery of the Incarnation: that same form shall be judge, which stood under a judge; that same shall judge, which was judged; that the ungodly may see his glory, against whose meekness they raged; and such a judge shall appear as may be seen both of them whom he is to crown, and of them whom he is to condemn.

### What are the testimonies proving the Divinity of the Son?

There are five principal ones.

The first, the voice of God himself, or the Holy Scripture. **Jer. 33**: *"Jehovah our righteousness."* **Isa. 9**: *"The mighty God."* **Ps. 68**: *"Jehovah <!-- p.253 --> is his name."* **John 1**: ***ὁ λόγος*** (the Word) *"was God."* **Rom. 9**: *"Christ, who is over all, God."*

The second is the worship to be rendered unto Christ by Angels and men. **Heb. 2**: *"All the angels of God shall worship him."*[^F2-2]

The third: the works of Christ. **John 1**: *"All things were made by him."* He quickeneth, he is ***καρδιογνώστης*** (the knower of hearts), he heareth prayer.

The fourth: the confession and consolation of the Church and of all the godly.

The fifth: the office of Redemption, or the function of the Mediator, which belongeth not to a bare creature, ***ἢ ψιλῷ ἀνθρώπῳ*** (or to a mere man).

### JESUS — what doth it signify?

It signifieth a Saviour, *Ein Heyland*.

### What doth the name of Jesus teach?

First, it teacheth that the Son of God, begotten of the eternal Father, is very God and very man, the Saviour of the world, the Fountain and the Way of salvation.

Secondly, it teacheth that the whole world without this Saviour is lost, and condemned in its sins.

Thirdly, it teacheth that from this Jesus alone righteousness, salvation and eternal life are to be sought and looked for, and that no works and ***παθήματα*** (sufferings), or merits, can save us, beside the Merit of this Jesus.

Fourthly, it teacheth that this name of Jesus is to be set against despair, the greatness of sin, unworthiness, particularity,[^F2-3] the might and powers of the Devil, and all evils.

Fifthly, this Name setteth before us the sum and compend of the whole Gospel, concerning sin, concerning condemnation, concerning the remission of sin, concerning salvation in the Name of Jesus, in which Repentance and Remission of sins must be preached.

### Christ — what doth it signify?

It signifieth the same as *Messias* among the Hebrews, that is, the Anointed. <!-- p.254 --> For this name sheweth how he is the Saviour, and by what means the course of salvation is wrought and brought to perfection.

### Wherefore is the Son of God named Messias, or Christ?

For three causes. 1. Because he is a Prophet. 2. Because he is the High Priest. 3. Because he is a King. For Prophets, Priests and Kings in the Old Testament were anointed for a type of the true Messias.

He is a Prophet, both because of old he himself by his Spirit revealed unto the Prophets the promise of reconciliation, and because he also set forth the same unto men by his own voice, according to that saying: *"No man hath seen God at any time; the Son, which is in the bosom of the Father, he hath revealed him unto us."*

He is the High Priest by reason of the sacrifice whereby he appeaseth the Father, and by reason of the intercession whereby he is Mediator of God and of men, himself God and man. For he performeth the four offices of a priest, in teaching, in sacrificing himself, in praying, and in blessing.

He is a King: 1. Because he delivereth us from the power of the Devil and of death. 2. Because he reigneth in us by his Spirit. 3. Because he defendeth the Church, gathered by the ministry of the Gospel, against the gates of hell.

### Wherefore is it added, the only Son of God?

1. That the divine nature of the Son of God may be described. 2. That the person of the Son may be distinguished from the person of the Father. 3. That this only-begotten and eternal Son may be discerned from the adoptive sons, who are made sons of God by grace, when they believe in this Son.

### Why is Christ named our Lord?

For four causes. 1. By the right of creation and of universal governance, whereby he himself, with the Father and the Holy Ghost, is <!-- p.255 --> Lord of all things, and the Monarch and Head of his Church. 2. By the right of Redemption. **1 Cor. 6**. For we belong of right unto him by whom we were redeemed. 3. In respect of defence, whereby as Emmanuel he is ever present with his Church, and guardeth her. 4. In respect of the divine power, which as God he hath eternal, and as man hath given unto him and received, in heaven, in earth, and above all powers and creatures, sitting at the Right Hand of the Father according to both his natures, Lord of all things.

## *De Incarnatione Christi* · On the Incarnation of Christ

### Which person of the Trinity assumed the human nature?

The second person, namely the Son, was made man, as it is written: *"The Word was made flesh."*

### Wherefore was the middle person in the Trinity made flesh?

There are seven causes. The first is, because it became him by whom are all things to be made the author of salvation. **Heb. 2**. The Creator is the re-creator; the former, the reformer; the maker, the re-maker; the preparer, the repairer. Whose is the ***πλάσις*** (the forming), his also is the ***ἀνάπλασις*** (the forming anew); and whose is the ***κτίσις*** (the creation), his also is the ***ἀνάκτισις*** (the creation anew). The second cause is, because it was fitting that that person in whom the life of man had been, even before the fall, should be sent unto the office of redemption, that mankind, delivered from death, might be brought again unto life through him in whom was life, and the light of men.

The third cause is, because it was meet that he who in the divinity was the Son of God the Father should in the manhood become the son of a human mother, lest there should be believed to be two Sons, but that there might be one Son, who should bestow upon us also, who were the children of wrath, this power, that we should become through him and for his sake sons of God, brethren and joint-heirs with Christ by a free adoption, ***κεχαριτωμένοι ἐν τῷ ἠγαπημένῳ*** (made accepted in the beloved).

<!-- p.256 -->

The fourth. It was meet, seeing God willed to restore man unto his own image, that that person should be made incarnate who is himself the image of the invisible God, and the express image of the substance of God.

The fifth. The Son is full of the grace and love of the Father. But we have need of the grace of God and of the love or mercy of God. The Son therefore, having assumed flesh, becometh our Reconciler, of whose fulness we receive grace for grace, and for whose sake we are loved.

The sixth. It was meet that the middle person in the Trinity should be made the Mediator between God and men.

The seventh. The Son of God is the ***λόγος*** (the Word) of the Father. But we have need of a Guide speaking with us, and shewing us the Father. This therefore the Son of God doth, coming into the flesh.

**Augustine**, *On the Spirit and the Soul*:[^F2-4] *"Wretched man that I am, how greatly ought I to love my God, who made me when I was not, redeemed me when I had perished. For I was not, and of nothing he made me; not a stone, not a tree, not any one of the beasts, but he willed me to be a man; he gave me to live, to feel, to discern; he redeemed me out of bondage, he called me by his own name, that his memorial might be ever with me; he anointed me with the oil of gladness, wherewith he himself was anointed, that I might be anointed of the Anointed, and of Christ be called a Christian."*

**Bernard**: *"Plainly now God cannot despise us: for the Son of God is our brother and our flesh; seeing that natural use so holdeth, that all flesh loveth flesh like unto itself,"* etc.

### Why is it meet that the Mediator be alike God and man?

There are four causes. 1. The justice of God. 2. Necessity. 3. The condition of a Mediator. 4. Human frailty.

<!-- p.257 -->

The justice of God required that either the whole of mankind should perish, or that, as one man by his own guilt had been the author of sin and of death, so some one in mankind should expiate sin and pay the penalty. Therefore the Son of God willed to be made man.

The necessity is, that no created power alone could have sustained the wrath of God, and in so great a magnitude of sorrows have truly rendered unto God the praise of justice, appeased the wrath of God, destroyed the works of the Devil, beheld the groanings of hearts. Therefore the Mediator is also God.

The condition of a Mediator is to be joined unto the parties at variance, unto God and unto man. It behoved therefore the Mediator of God and of men, by reason of his ***οἰκείωσιν*** (kinship) unto both, to bring both back into friendship and concord, and to bring it to pass that God should receive man, and that man should be restored unto God.

Human frailty required such a Mediator, of whose power and will it should not doubt, and unto whom it should dare to draw near, and to look unto him in all straits, being assured that he hath paid an equivalent price for sins, hath overcome death, restoreth life, maketh continual intercession for the Church, and is always and everywhere present with his Church, and keepeth and protecteth her, heareth, helpeth, and entereth into the Holy of Holies, drawing near immediately unto the Father, and is our Priest and Intercessor, according to both his natures, the Divine, Almighty, and the human, assumed into the unity of the person. For to be Mediator is not only an action of the Divine nature, but also of the human in Christ.

**Argument.**

> No man is Mediator of himself, or intercessor with himself.  
> <!-- p.258 --> Therefore Christ according to the Divine nature is not Mediator, because he would be intercessor with himself.  

**I answer.** These things are true in the same respect, and where there is but one person. But it behoveth to distinguish between the Divinity sending, and the Son sent. The Son, inasmuch as he is sent, is Mediator and Priest.

### Is the work of Redemption to be ascribed unto either nature in Christ apart, or unto the whole person?

The work of Redemption belongeth neither unto the Divine nature apart, nor unto the human, but unto the whole person, for five causes.

The first cause is the personal union. For if the divine nature alone had willed to accomplish the work of Redemption, it would not have assumed the mass of human nature; and the human nature alone could not have of itself faculties and powers so great as that the work of Redemption should be accomplished by them.

The second cause is the comparing of the testimonies. *"Christ was manifested, that he might destroy the works of the devil."* *"This is his name whereby they shall call him, Jehovah our righteousness."* Likewise: *"Thou hast looked upon me in the form of a man, who is in the Highest, Jehovah the Lord."* These are to be referred unto the whole person, not unto the natures apart.

The third is the prerogative of Christ the Priest, of which the Epistle to the **Hebrews** preacheth: *"The law maketh priests which have infirmity; but the word of the oath, which was since the law, maketh the Son, who is consecrated for evermore"*; who also assumed the human nature, that he might have somewhat to offer.

The fourth cause is the office of the Mediator, which agreeth unto the whole person.

The fifth is the confession of the whole Church, as we say in the **Nicene Creed**: *"Who for us men, and for our <!-- p.259 --> salvation, came down from heaven, and was made man."* And **Cyril**: *"He is the Mediator of God and of men, because in him God and man are joined together."*

### Did Christ assume our nature truly, or either a different one, or only in part?

The Son of God assumed a human nature true, entire, or whole, consubstantial with us, having all the conditions, powers and appetites proper unto the nature and well ordered; not corrupted, but without sin, undefiled and holy; in which nevertheless the infirmities that entered into our nature as punishments on account of sin he willingly, of an affection of compassion, not of a necessity of his condition, assumed without fault for our sakes, that he might be made a Sacrifice for us.

This doctrine is to be held against the ***δόκησιν καὶ φαντασίαν*** (the semblance and phantasm) of the Manichees; and against the ***ἀψυχίαν*** (the want of a soul) of the Arians; and the ***ἀλογίαν*** (the want of a reasonable mind) of the Apollinarists; and the ***μίαν θέλησιν καὶ δύναμιν*** (the one will and power) of the Monothelites; and against the ***σύγχυσιν τῶν φύσεων*** (the confusion of the natures) brought in by the Eutychians. For the Son of God assumed our nature entire, consisting of a reasonable soul and a body, without sin, and united it unto himself by the hypostatic union, ***ἀσυγχύτως, ἀτρέπτως, ἀδιαιρέτως, καὶ ἀχωρίστως*** (without confusion, without change, without division, and without separation), that there might be one ***ὑφιστάμενον ἐν δυσὶ φύσεσι*** (subsisting one in two natures).

### For what causes did the Son of God assume the human nature?

The ends were four. For first, the Son of God had respect unto the will of the Father, which was to be obeyed. Secondly, unto the Scriptures, to be made certain and confirmed. Thirdly, unto the human nature, that he might redeem it from the wrath of God, from condemnation <!-- p.260 --> and from eternal death, and in himself first repair and restore it, and that thereafter from him, as from the head, healing, sanctification, or renewal, might be derived unto us, who are his members. Fourthly, unto the Devil, to bruise whom Christ came into the flesh.

### Wherefore was he conceived of the Holy Ghost, and born of a Virgin?

The Angel expresseth the cause, **Luke 1**: *"That holy thing which shall be born of thee shall be called the Son of God."* For therefore was the Son of God born of the Holy Ghost, without the seed of man, of a pure and unspotted ever-Virgin, that we may know that he was made man without sin, which all other men, born of carnal propagation, bring with them into this world.

**Argument.**

> He that conceiveth is the father of him that is conceived.  
> The Son of God is a man conceived of the Holy Ghost.  
> Therefore the Holy Ghost is the Father of Christ.  

**I answer:** The Son of God was conceived of the Holy Ghost, that is, not of the substance, but by the operation and efficacy of the Holy Ghost, as the Angel answereth the Virgin: *"The Holy Ghost shall come upon thee, and the power of the Highest shall overshadow thee."*

### Why is express mention made of the Virgin Mary, the mother of Christ?

1. For the certainty of the history.

2. For the predictions now fulfilled, as: *"The seed of the woman shall bruise the serpent's head."* *"Behold, a virgin shall be with child, and shall bring forth."* *"A woman shall compass a man."*

<!-- p.261 -->

3. For the promises made unto David, of whose lineage the Virgin Mary was sprung, **Ps. 132**: *"Of the fruit of thy body will I set upon thy throne."*

4. For the signification of the spiritual regeneration, of which **Augustine** saith: *"It behoved our Head, by a signal miracle, to be born according to the body of a Virgin, that he might signify that his members should be born according to the Spirit of the Virgin Church"*; *"not of the will of the flesh, nor of man, but of God,"* **John 1**.

### What doth the Scripture name the Exinanition, Phil. 2?

1. The ***ἐνανθρώπησιν*** (the becoming man) itself, and the form of a servant assumed.

2. The infirmities with which our nature is pressed down on account of sin, and which the Son of God also took upon him without sin, as his Passion and death do testify.

3. The hiding of the divine power, which, although it was always present, and dwelt bodily in the flesh of Christ, yet in the state of humiliation rested, and did not manifestly put itself forth in the human nature, and that only for a time. Whence **Ambrose** godly and excellently saith: *"Christ sat at the right hand of the Father, but he would not publish that glory before the resurrection."*

### *Precatio Anselmi* · A Prayer of Anselm

We adore thee, the precious price of our redemption, the peace offering, who alone by the inestimable sweetness of thy savour didst incline the Father, which dwelleth on high, to have respect unto the lowly, and didst render him appeasable unto the children of wrath. Thy mercies, O Christ, we proclaim; the memory of thy sweetness we <!-- p.262 --> abundantly utter; unto thee, O Christ, we offer the sacrifice of praise, for the multitude of thy goodness, which thou hast shewed unto us, a naughty seed, wicked and lost. For when we were yet thine enemies, O Lord, and the ancient death exercised an unjust dominion over all flesh, unto which all the seed of Adam was subject by the law of the first fault, thou didst remember thy most plenteous mercy, and didst look forth from thy high habitation into this vale of weeping and of misery. Thou sawest, O Lord, the affliction of thy people, and, touched inwardly with the grief of charity, thou didst set thyself to think upon us thoughts of peace and of redemption. And truly, whereas thou wast the Son of God, very God, coeternal and consubstantial with God the Father and the Holy Ghost, dwelling in the light which no man can approach unto, and upholding all things by the word of thy power, thou didst not disdain to bow down thy highness into this prison-house of our mortality, that thou mightest both taste and swallow up our misery, and restore us unto glory. It was a small thing unto thy charity, O Lord Jesus, to appoint a Cherub or a Seraph, or one of the Angels, for the finishing of the work of our salvation; thou thyself didst vouchsafe to come unto us by the commandment of the Father, whose exceeding great charity we have proved in thee. For thou camest, not by changing of place, but by shewing forth thy presence through the flesh, descending from the royal throne of thy high glory into a maiden lowly, and in her own eyes an abject handmaid, in whose most holy womb the unspeakable power of the Holy Ghost alone made thee both to be conceived, and to be born in the true nature of manhood, in such wise that the occasion of the Nativity did violate neither the Majesty of the Divinity in thee, nor the integrity of the virginity in her.

### What is the personal, or hypostatic, union?

The personal union of the two natures in Christ is not a transmutation, or a confusion, either of the natures or of the properties <!-- p.263 --> among themselves, but is an ineffable coupling, whereby the second person of the Divinity, or the hypostasis ***τοῦ λόγου*** (of the Word), which from eternity subsisteth in the divine nature alone, did in the fulness of time assume a human nature entire, sanctified by the Holy Ghost in the womb of the Virgin, and by this assumption made it inseparably and perpetually his own, in such wise that, the difference of the natures being preserved, and the essential properties of them being preserved, there is constituted of the coupling of these two natures one ***ὑφιστάμενον*** (one that subsisteth), and one person in Christ, whether he be named God or man, always is and abideth, namely one Christ incarnate, who now consisteth not of the divine nature only, as from eternity before the incarnation, but now both the divine and the human nature do constitute one Christ incarnate, in whom those two natures are coupled not by a bare touching only, but the ***λόγος*** (the Word), assuming the human nature, shineth whole in the whole of it, and the human nature, united unto the Word, is as it were kindled with light, and exalted by an incomprehensible and unspeakable Majesty above every name.

### Did the Son of God assume only a certain mass out of mankind, void of life, or did he couple unto himself a living human nature?

By no means did the Son of God assume only a human mass destitute of life, but an entire human nature, consisting of life, or a reasonable soul, and of a human body; which mass of the assumed human nature, nevertheless, did not subsist by itself before the union, but in the very conception was made the Son of God's own, in the unity not of nature, but of person; not of a human person, but of a divine; not of the assumed, but of the assuming; nor of any person whatsoever, but of the Word alone.

**Hugh** [of St Victor]: *"From the time that God assumed man, he assumed the whole, <!-- p.264 --> namely flesh and soul, that is, the nature of man, not the person of man, but man into person. For that flesh and soul, before they were united unto the Word into person, were not united between themselves unto a person. For the union was a union into one, of the Word, of the flesh, and of the soul; not first the Word and the flesh, nor first the Word and the soul, nor first the soul and the flesh, but at once the Word and the soul and the flesh. Neither did the Word begin to be a person when he began to be man, but he so assumed man, that man should begin to be person, and no other person than he was who took him. Therefore the Word, a person, took upon him man, not a person, but a nature, that he who took, and that which he took, might be one person in the Trinity."*

**Argument.**

Whatsoever consisteth of a reasonable soul and a human body, that is a person, which is named man. Christ the man, ***σώματι περιγραπτός*** (circumscribed in body) and walking upon the earth, consisteth of a reasonable soul and a human body. Therefore he is a person, even as touching his human nature; and by consequence it is not rightly said that the human nature in Christ had not the character of a person (*rationem personae*).

**I answer** unto the major. The major pertaineth unto the usual and regular predications, and is true universally in the order of nature, after the usual manner, without any exception, save this one unusual example of the incarnation of the Son of God, which is the mystery of mysteries, beyond the course of things, a wonderful exchange (*admirabile commercium*), in which ***τὸ ἄλλο καὶ ἄλλο*** (one thing and another thing) do meet together, and yet there is not ***ἄλλος καὶ ἄλλος*** (one person and another person), but there is one ***ὑπόστασις*** (hypostasis), one Christ, ***εἷς ἐν προσώπῳ, καὶ ὅλος, εἷς ἐν ὀνόματι*** (one in person, and whole, one in name), one God and man, ***θεάνθρωπος*** (God-man), ***ὅλος ἐν τῷ πράττειν*** (whole in the working), so that each nature in the one Christ doth, with communication of the other, that which is proper unto each. And although <!-- p.265 --> the assumed human nature is not a brute thing, lifeless, idle, or ***ἄεργος*** (without working), but a certain living thing, understanding and effectual, yet it hath not the character of a person, but is assumed into the person of the Son of God, and there is one Christ, God and man. Thou askest, How? I know not how to answer: the mind faileth, the tongue is silent, not of men only, but of Angels also.

### Wherefore did Christ will that his assumed nature should not have the character of a person of its own?

1. Because he himself, subsisting in the divine nature, was from eternity a person, and indeed a most perfect ***ὑφιστάμενον*** (one that subsisteth), unto whose perfection nothing is wanting.

2. Because he willed to shew his boundless charity towards us, in that he coupled our nature unto himself by the straitest bond of the hypostatic union, and exalted it in himself unto the Right Hand of God.

3. He willeth to be acknowledged, worshipped, and called upon by us not only in his divine nature, but also in the assumed human nature; which if it be separated from Christ, the whole Christ is not had, because the human nature now pertaineth unto the very substance of Christ the Saviour, and the divine nature is not now the whole Christ without the assumed human nature, but the whole person of Christ now consisteth of the divine and the human nature.

4. He willed to reject and condemn every ***τετράδα*** (quaternity), such as the Quaternists feigned, and such as they devise at this day who consider the humanity of Christ in the abstract only, and make it subject unto the rules of natural philosophy, according to that saying: *"Every spirit that dissolveth Christ is not of God."* For Christ with his own flesh is one of the Trinity, ***εἷς θεὸς ἀμφοτέρωθεν*** (one God, of both), not that the human nature was thus mingled with the Divine <!-- p.266 --> nature, or that the Word advanced into flesh, but that the human, mortal and weak flesh and nature advanced into the divine glory, the Lord himself saying of the Son of man assumed by him: *"Now is the Son of man glorified, and God is glorified in him. God hath glorified him in himself,"* **John 13**.

5. He willed that our consolation should be firm and lively, and our invocation sure, who know that Christ, God, is our brother, and that our flesh and blood is in the Trinity at the right hand of the divine Power and Majesty.

### What is the communication of idioms?

That is called an idiom (*idioma*) which is a property agreeing unto one nature.

The communication of idioms (*communicatio idiomatum*) is, when the idioms, that is, the properties of one nature, are communicated and attributed unto the other, not in the abstract, but in the concrete, or in the person.

Abstract terms signify the natures and forms considered by themselves, as: divinity, divine nature, humanity, human nature.

Concrete terms signify the subjects themselves together with their natures and forms, as: God, man, that is, the person, or ***τὸ ὑφιστάμενον*** (that which subsisteth).

### How many are the kinds of the communication of idioms?

For the sake of teaching and of learning we say that there are three. The first is, when those things which are proper unto one nature in Christ are attributed unto the person in the concrete, with a declaration added, according to which nature, that the essential properties of each nature may remain entire; as: God was born of a virgin; the Son of God suffered, was crucified, died. The Lord of glory was crucified. God redeemed the Church with his own blood. The Son of Mary is the creator of heaven and earth. But in the abstract it is not said: <!-- p.267 --> The Divinity was born, suffered, died. The human nature died for us (because this phrase, with the little words "for us" added, comprehendeth the whole work of Redemption, which belongeth unto the person). The humanity created heaven and earth.

The second is, when each nature in Christ worketh with communication of the other, as it cometh to pass in the offices of Christ; as, that Christ is mediator, redeemer, justifier, and saviour; quickener, propitiator, High Priest, priest, King, shepherd, physician, head of the Church. These idioms are not of one nature only, but of both, and therefore of the whole person, without distinction according to which nature, but simply according to both natures.

The third kind is, when it is discoursed concerning the human nature in Christ, not in the abstract, outside the person of Christ, but in the ***λόγῳ*** (the Word) himself; namely, that beside and beyond the natural properties very many things, which surpass and exceed the common and natural condition of human nature, have in very deed been communicated and given unto it, which it hath not of itself or according to itself, but hath received by the hypostatic union with the divinity from the ***λόγῳ*** himself, not outside the ***λόγον***, but in him. For it is a blasphemous speech, and worthy of eternal torments, to say that Christ in his human nature received nothing, and hath nothing, that surpasseth the human condition and property, on the ground that the human nature is a creature in all things like unto us. But of these things elsewhere. And there is extant the book *On the Two Natures in Christ*, written by **D. Chemnitz**,[^F2-5] unto the diligent reading whereof we exhort them that are studious of godliness, especially in these tempests of contentions.

But let the rules be before our eyes. **Augustine's**: *"Whatsoever Christ the man hath not in his human nature by nature, he hath by grace,"* to wit, of the ***λόγου*** (the Word) assuming.

**Hilary's**: *"That which is of time hath received the glory of that brightness which is without time."*

<!-- p.268 -->

**Leo's**: *"Whatsoever Christ received in time, he received according to the man, upon whom those things which he had not are conferred. For according to the power of the Word, the Son also hath, without difference, all things that the Father hath."*

**Athanasius's**: *"When the Scripture speaketh of the Glorification of Christ, it speaketh of the flesh, which received glory."* The same: *"The Most High is not exalted, but the flesh of the Most High is exalted."*

**Cyril's**: *"To have received glory, power, and a kingdom over all things, is to be referred unto the conditions of the humanity."*

**Ambrose's**: *"The magnificence which the Son of God had eternally by nature, the Son of the Virgin was to receive in time by grace, and to have eternally."*

**Cyril's**: *"As man he receiveth by grace from the Deity the things which as God he hath by nature."*

When therefore the Son of God saith: *"All power is given unto me in heaven and in earth"*; *"All things are delivered unto me of my Father"*; likewise: *"God hath made him both Lord and Christ"*; *"He hath put all things under his feet"*; unto the Son of man he gave power, honour, a kingdom over all peoples, tribes and tongues; he ascended into the heavens, that he may sit at the right hand of the Majesty of God, in the throne of his Father, that he may reign over the house of Jacob for ever; *"God hath anointed him with the oil of gladness above his fellows"*; God giveth not the Spirit by measure unto Christ; God hath given him authority to judge, because he is the Son of man; the Father hath given all things into his hands; God hath exalted him by his right hand, and made him to sit at his own right hand in the heavenly places above all things; he hath lifted him up unto the highest height; *"In him dwelleth all the fulness of the Godhead"* ***σωματικῶς*** (bodily), etc. — when, I say, either the Son of God himself or the Holy Scriptures do so speak, they always understand the human nature in Christ, unto which so great Majesty and Glory hath been given in Christ, not of itself, but communicated by the Son of God really, not in title only, or verbally (because the union is not <!-- p.269 --> verbal), nor yet by itself or apart, outside the person of Christ, in the abstract, by any reasoning or vain imagination whatsoever, but as inseparably united with the Deity of the Word in one person; so that this rule is universal and unmoved: Unto Christ according to the humanity all things are delivered, all excellency, all glory, all power and working, nothing excepted, save him only which did put all things under him, **1 Cor. 15**.

### Those excellent gifts which Christ received according to his human nature — are they created gifts, or are they divine, as Omnipotence, Omniscience, etc.?

There have been bestowed upon the human nature of Christ certain created gifts, most excellent and most perfect, which both in number and in degrees do very far surpass the measure and power of all other creatures, so that the human nature in Christ may be an instrument sufficiently disposed and fit, by which the Divinity ***τοῦ λόγου*** (of the Word) may exercise and accomplish the works of its Divine Majesty.

But these created gifts do by no means attain unto, nor express, the eminence of those things which the Scripture delivereth that the human nature in Christ hath received, as: to sit at the right hand of God; to have life in himself, and to be quickening, because he is the Son of man; to receive all power in heaven and in earth; to be higher than all heavens; to have the whole fulness of the Divinity dwelling personally in his body. All these things do shew a divine Majesty, Glory and Power more clearly than the noonday sun.

**On the contrary:**

> Essential idioms are not communicated.  
> The Majesty and power of God are essential idioms.  
> <!-- p.270 --> Therefore they are not communicated unto the human nature in Christ.  

**I answer** unto the major. They are not communicated, to wit, essentially, and by a natural communication; neither are they communicated outside the Trinity unto any thing or creature; neither is there made an equalling of the natures. For Christ the man, even in glory, is less than the Father according to the humanity, which, although it be lifted up above all creatures, yet is under God the Creator as a creature, and is not changed into the nature of the Deity. But the communication, which is made in very deed, not in title only, or verbally, or dialectically, or naturally (*physice*), but really, above nature (*hyperphysice*), and after a Divine manner, is thus expressed out of the Holy Scriptures: The Divinity ***τοῦ λόγου*** (of the Word), in the union, doth not pour over, out of itself into the assumed nature, essential attributes of the Divinity, altogether like and equal unto the Divine nature, which, separated from the Divinity, should formally inhere in the human nature; but the whole fulness of the Divinity dwelleth in the assumed nature, and is as it were laid up therein, not simply and absolutely, but in such wise that all the glory and the whole Majesty of the Divinity ***τοῦ λόγου*** shineth in the assumed humanity, and putteth itself forth through it, when and howsoever it will; and the whole fulness of Divine grace and truth is in the assumed humanity, and floweth forth thence, and not from elsewhere, unto the elect; and all the virtue and all the power of the Divinity ***τοῦ λόγου*** dwelleth in the assumed nature, not in an absolute manner, but in such wise that the works of its Divine power, which aforetime it accomplished by itself alone, and could do the same now also, yet now, after the incarnation, it willeth to exercise and accomplish, those especially which pertain unto the office of the Messias, in its assumed human nature, with it, and through it, as through its nearest and most proper Instrument, according as it shall seem good unto it.

This dignity of the human nature is the greatest, which is now united unto the second person in the Trinity itself, and receiveth from it all Divine Power and Glory, which it assuredly hath, <!-- p.271 --> not according to itself, but in Christ and from Christ, although the manner in which so great a thing can come to pass be unknown unto all human reason.

### What thinkest thou of Ubiquity?

This word is monstrous, and feigned by the Sacramentarians themselves. And the thing itself also is monstrous. For we do not extend, spread abroad, or diffuse the Body of Christ unto infinity, because the Body of Christ is a true Body. But we retain and call upon the whole Christ, God and man, and do not separate the human nature from him, nor shut it up in a certain place, but we hold that a mere and only natural Locality is an empty figment, pernicious unto our Faith and Salvation. But we believe the Word of Christ, and hold two Rules, and the rest, which remain to be learned, we reserve unto the heavenly and true Academy. The Rules are these:

1. Christ can be, not only in his Divine nature, but also in his humanity, and with his Body and Blood, wheresoever he willeth to be, whensoever and howsoever, even ***ὑπερφυσικῶς*** (above nature), and contrary to the nature and property of a natural Body. For he is Almighty, not bound unto the Rules of natural philosophy, and yet he retaineth the truth of his Body, in that manner which is known unto himself alone.

2. The Body of Christ is in very deed present there, where Christ himself hath expressly said that it is, and whereunto he hath bound himself by his Word, although this seem absurd and contrary unto human reason, and unto the course and property of natural things; as in the Supper, at one and the same time in places most diverse and most remote. For Christ is true, and a keeper of his Testament; and he that doth not with most simple faith believe him in the manifest Institution and attestation of the last Will which he himself hath left unto us, <!-- p.272 --> maketh Christ a liar, and taketh from him Omnipotence and Truth: which how horrible, perilous and pernicious it is, we shall see in the Last Judgement, forasmuch as at this day, in so great security, pride, confidence and rage, very few study to take counsel of their own Consciences and to consider the Majesty of the Last Judgement.

But of these things thus far, and perchance more at large than ought to have been done in Catechetical Questions. But the certainty of the heavenly Truth will excuse me, and the contradiction that must be opposed unto the Devilish Ravings of them who imprison Jesus Christ, their Lord and Brother, in the natural Chains of the Properties of a human Body, and will not gladly allow Power and Glory unto his Flesh sitting at the Right Hand of God, nor grant and leave unto it inviolate that which the whole Trinity with one accord hath given, bestowed, granted, attributed, imparted unto it. O the malice, ingratitude, envy, blasphemy, perfidy, and Apostasy, for which answer must be made unto the Son of man himself, our Lord Jesus Christ, howsoever it be now tricked out, cloaked, excused, as men are wont who care little for the Glory of their God and Brother, our Lord Jesus Christ, the Almighty and the Judge of all men; whom if those Worldly, Courtly and Politic, or Philosophical, Disputers do fear, worship and rightly call upon, why do they not leave unto us Christ whole and untorn? But if they set him aside, make light of him, and look upon him as a bare Man, or at the least as weak in his Flesh, why do they lead us about with their Impostures, and do not say openly that they acknowledge not Christ our Brother in that manner in which the Praise and Glory of Omnipotence, and the Worship of Invocation, are due unto him, even as Man?

## *De Passione Christi* · On the Passion of Christ

*Suffered under Pontius Pilate. Crucified. Dead. And Buried.*

### What is the most conspicuous Act of the humiliation of the Son of God?

The Passion, the Crucifixion, the Death, the Burial.

### What is the Passion of Christ?

It is the wonderful and unspeakable obedience which Christ our Lord, knowing, willing and glad, rendered in bearing the most bitter torments of soul and of body, that by this true reverence and subjection an equivalent price might be paid unto the Divine justice for the sins of the whole of mankind, and that the Father, appeased by this propitiatory Sacrifice, might receive them that flee unto him in trust of this Mediator and sacrifice, and, they being delivered from the curse of the Law, from the tyranny of the Devil, and from eternal death, might for the Son's merit and intercession freely bestow upon them righteousness and eternal Life.

### What is a propitiatory Sacrifice, or ***ἱλαστικόν***?

It is the work of our Lord Jesus Christ alone, appeasing the wrath of God for others, and making satisfaction for the guilt, and for the eternal punishment, due unto mankind on account of sin. **John 17**: *"For their sakes I sanctify myself, that they also might be sanctified for ever."*[^F2-6]

<!-- p.274 -->

### What things are required unto the perfection of a propitiatory Sacrifice?

Five. 1. God, unto whom the offering is to be made. 2. The Priest, the Son of God himself made man, who offereth. 3. The thing which is offered, the same Son of God, the sacrifice and the lamb. 4. The thing for which it is offered, namely mankind. 5. The Covenant and Testament, and the effect of the Sacrifice, which is saving unto all that believe, unto eternal life.

### How can the work of one man be sufficient for the sins of the whole world?

This the hypostatic Union of the divine and human nature doth shew. For by this Union is to be esteemed the worth of the merit and Passion of Christ, which is of infinite value, because he is not man only, but God also.

### Whether doth the Passion belong unto the person, or unto the human nature only?

The Passion belongeth unto the person, not in the divine, but in the human nature, according to that saying of **Peter**: *"Christ hath suffered in the flesh."* For whatsoever of reproach and of wrong was laid upon the flesh of Christ, or is yet laid upon it by his enemies, all that hath fallen and doth fall to the reproach of the eternal Son of God.

The human nature is likened unto Iron, the Divine unto Fire, so that there is made a fiery iron (*ferrum ignitum*). For so hath Antiquity always spoken. Now if any man shall essay to touch and to handle the Iron, he shall feel the force, not of the iron, but of the fire; which thing all they shall really prove, who are either manifest blasphemers and revilers of the Son of God, or diminishers of the Divine Majesty communicated unto Christ the man.
