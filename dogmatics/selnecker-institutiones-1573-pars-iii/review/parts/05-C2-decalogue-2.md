In the same manner in the Church, men seized with longing for the Apostles and Martyrs would have images made for themselves representing their countenances; whereby Satan snatched a handle for the setting up of idolatry and the invocation of Saints, and confirmed it with devilish miracles. Hence the churches were adorned with images of the Saints, and also with the bucklers and shields of noble families: sometimes with good intent, which — superstition being absent, and every cause or occasion of idolatry — we in no wise disapprove, so far as true and godly histories are concerned; sometimes after the heathen manner, and with an ambition not to be borne. Let our verses, such as they are, be kept in mind:

<!-- p.70 -->

> *Unde sit Idolis et origo et noxius usus,*  
> *Quaeritur, et res est expedienda brevi.*  
> *Fecit avaritia interdum, sed maxima causa*  
> *Assentatio erat, nonque ferendus amor.*  
> *Iam desiderium defuncti ardebat, et atra*  
> *Accumulans flammas saepe tyrannis erat.*  
> *Res Satanae grata ista fuit, nil gratius illi,*  
> *Quam perversa suo more superstitio.*  
> *Hinc genus humanum ruit in facta impia semper,*  
> *Hinc sunt tot passim Numina, totque Dei.*  
> *Errat mens hominum, nimiumque incerta vagatur,*  
> *Ludit in hac Satanas, et furit insidiis.*  
> *Sed tibi Christe pio grates tibi dicimus ore,*  
> *Qui monstras verum, voce docente, Deum.*  
> *Iam non mens errat, iam nostra oratio Patrem*  
> *Aeternum alloquitur, novit, honorat, amat.*  
> *Ethnica turba vale, fictorum turba Deorum,*  
> *Patroni Monachis nota valete cohors.*  
> *Qui pater est Christi, Dominum iam novimus unum,*  
> *Divino flatu pectora nostra calent.*  

> Whence idols have both their beginning and their hurtful use  
> is asked; and the matter is shortly to be dispatched.  
> Covetousness wrought it at times; but the greatest cause  
> was flattery, and a love not to be borne.  
> Now longing for the dead burned hot; and black  
> tyranny was often there, heaping up the flames.  
> That thing was pleasing to Satan; nothing pleaseth him more  
> than superstition, perverse after its wont.  
> Hence the race of men doth ever rush into ungodly deeds;  
> hence are there everywhere so many Powers, so many gods.  
> The mind of men strayeth, and wandereth all too uncertain;  
> in it Satan sporteth, and rageth with his snares.  
> But to thee, O Christ, to thee we give thanks with godly mouth,  
> who by thy teaching voice dost show the true God.  
> Now the mind strayeth not; now our prayer speaketh  
> to the eternal Father, knoweth, honoureth, loveth him.  
> Farewell, thou heathen throng, thou throng of feigned gods;  
> farewell, ye Patrons, a company well known to the monks.  
> Him who is the Father of Christ we now know as the one Lord;  
> our breasts are warm with the breath of God.  

Moreover, two causes are set down in the sacred writings on account of which idols were fashioned.[^C2-1] **The former** is the inordinate and excessive affection of men toward their departed kinsfolk, for whom they made images for their own consolation, while they looked upon the images of the dead. Whence it is written of **Ninus**, king of the Assyrians, that when his father Belus was dead he made an image of Belus, and showed it so great reverence that he spared offenders and such as had grievously fallen when they fled to his father's image, and there set up a sanctuary (*asylon*) for all the guilty and the wicked. So it came to pass that men began to bestow divine worship and honours upon that image. And after his example other mighty men began <!-- p.71 --> to set up images to the dead whom they had loved, and showed them like reverence; and as the fashioning of idols had been begun from Belus, so also from his name arose the naming of the others, as *Bel*, *Baal*, that is, Lord; *Baal Phaeor*, the god of the Moabites, so called from "opening", whom afterwards they named Priapus, either by a change of the name *Paeor*, or from *Pri* and *ab*, as it were "the father of fruitfulness"; *Baal Zebub*, Lord of Flies, the god of the Ekronites, etc. In the same way also fathers made images for their first-born, if they had departed out of this life; of which matter **Epiphanius**, Tom. 1, lib. 1, speaketh after this manner: *"Reu begat Serug. And idolatry began among men, yet not as yet in stones or wood, or silver and gold, or other matter, but only in colours and paintings. And Serug begat Nahor, Nahor begat Terah. Hence began* ***ἀνδριαντοπλασία***, *the art of forming and carving images out of any matter whatsoever; and for a punishment his son Haran died unto Terah himself, whereas before no son had died a natural death before his father."*

**Lactantius** also speaketh of this cause of the feigning of images, lib. 2, cap. 2 of the *Divine Institutes*, saying: *"The whole device of feigning likenesses was therefore found out by men, that the memory might be kept of those who had either been taken away by death or parted by absence."*

**The latter cause** is ambition. For the mighty and tyrants, willing to be honoured not only in their presence but also in their absence, commanded their images to be made, which they sent into far places, that they might be worshipped; as **Josephus** relateth of Caius Caesar, that he sent his image through the Roman empire to be adored, and commanded altars and temples to be set up to himself. This wicked purpose the Jews withstood, and, a tumult having arisen, slew many of the citizens of Alexandria; for which cause ambassadors were sent to Caius, among whom was **Philo**, the author of this book,[^C2-2] of whom, when he was cast out of the antechamber of Caius, there is this notable saying <!-- p.72 --> to his sorrowful companions: *"Ye ought to be of good cheer, with whom Caius is wroth; for the help of God must needs be at hand, where the help of man ceaseth."*

There have been many kings in all times who would have divine honours given to themselves. This impiety began in Egypt, and was afterwards spread abroad among well-nigh all nations. Alexander the Great would be called, believed and held a god and the son of Jupiter; although, being taught by his own wound, when he saw the blood, he confessed that it was not that humour of the gods, or ***ἰχῶρα*** (ichor).[^C2-11] Herod would have the name and honour of God ascribed to himself, and at the last said to his flatterers: *"I, your god, am now constrained to change life for death."* **Acts 12**; **Josephus**, *Antiq.* lib. 19, cap. 7. Domitian commanded that he should be called the Lord and God of his subjects. So great is the vanity of the mind of man when it is destitute of the knowledge of the true God and puffed up with the momentary success of perishing things.

### *De Tertio, id est, de imaginibus et harum usu* · On the Third Point, that is, on Images and Their Use

It is wont to be asked concerning images, whether it be altogether unlawful to have them, either in houses or in churches, seeing it is expressly written: *"Thou shalt not make unto thee any graven image."* I answer: All idolatrous images are forbidden, and the office of the Magistrate requireth that they be taken away, as did King Asa, **1 Kings 15**;[^C2-3] Hezekiah, **2 Kings 18**; Josiah, **2 Kings 23**; Jehoshaphat, **2 Chron. 17**; Darius the Mede, Constantine, Valentinian, Theodosius, and other godly princes. Of idolatrous images, therefore — to which there is joined an execrable superstition, feigning the use of them to be necessary, or adoring and worshipping statues, or binding God to them — nothing is said in this question. Neither is anything said of those tasteless and too childish and old-wifely images, to say no worse, in which <!-- p.73 --> God the Father is painted aged, an old man, bearded, hoary, with bleared eyes, holding the Son all bloody, and having a Dove beside him. But we speak of those pictures and images which are remembrances and monuments of a true history, as of the incarnation, nativity, temptation, crucifixion, death, resurrection and ascension of Christ. These and the like images, as remembrances, are not forbidden, so long as they are kept without superstition. But if there be a running together to them by idolaters, it is the Magistrate's part to remove them, as Hezekiah broke in pieces the Brazen Serpent, which before had always been suffered, when it was worshipped as a divine Power. For this cause **Luther** saith: *"Although I have no great love for images, and could wish that they were not set up in the church — not that I regard this only, that they be adored, which I think happeneth but seldom, but that a trust in the work is placed in their costliness and show, as though in that work some service were rendered unto God, while in the mean time the cost is lost, and all that is spent upon them ought to be spent to better uses for the need of the brethren — yet images lightly painted, in a private house, I cannot condemn,"* etc. For they are, as is commonly wont to be said, the books of the laity, for the Catechumens.

Let the younger sort know that this commandment (*"Thou shalt not make unto thee any graven image"*) is partly moral, partly ceremonial. The moral part is unchangeable, and doth earnestly forbid the adoration and worship of statues, and pertaineth to all and to each. But the ceremony bound the Jewish people, which was tied to the Temple at Jerusalem, wherein it was lawful to place neither statues nor images.

There are those who altogether condemn images of what sort soever: not only the Popish, as the image of Peter bearing the Keys in his hands, by which picture the Roman Pontiffs would signify their primacy; or the image of James, whereby the pilgrimage to Compostella is confirmed; or the image <!-- p.74 --> of the Blessed Virgin showing her breasts to the Son seated upon the rainbow, whereby the intercession of Mary is confirmed; or the image of Pope Gregory celebrating Mass for the living and the dead, etc.; but the others also, which we have said to be remembrances of true and chief histories, they utterly reject and disapprove. These men, to be sure, reprove the abuse with a godly mind, and consider the inconstancy and lightness of the common people, prone to idolatrous worship. But let us hold fast the simple and true judgement concerning images which we have briefly set forth, and which is profitable for the joining together of the godly Churches — which consisteth not in the keeping or the taking away of indifferent ceremonies, but in consent concerning doctrine and the lawful use of the Sacraments.

Moreover, concerning the use of images let the younger sort observe only this, that the use of images is threefold: the **Typical**, the **Idolatrous** or superstitious, and the **Political**. The *typical* use is that whereby some other real thing was shown beforehand, as by a figure and sign, or represented and shadowed forth, as the Brazen Serpent in the wilderness. This type hath now ceased. The *idolatrous* use, or rather abuse, of images is when we give to idols the outward worship which we owe to God, as that saying of the monks witnesseth: *"We, to the honour of the invisible God, worship and adore visible images."* But let us know that, whereas the worship of God is twofold — the *inward*, which ariseth from faith and is named ***σέβασμα*** (reverence), and the *outward*, which is called ***λατρεία*** and ***προσκύνησις*** (service and adoration) — judgement must be made and something certain determined of both kinds of worship according to the will of God expressed in the Word. Now it is plainly written: *"Thou shalt not make unto thee any graven image"*; or, as **Nazianzen** expoundeth it, ***μὴ στήσῃς ἴνδαλμα κενὸν καὶ ἄπνοον εἰκῶ*** (thou shalt not set up an empty likeness and a breathless image). But of these things it hath been spoken above. The *political* use is that which serveth either to the better understanding of the descriptions of things and to the keeping of things in memory (this one may call the *Historical* use, <!-- p.75 --> of which it is said that images are the books of the unlearned); or to adornment, wherewith no danger is mingled, and which doth not hinder or mar the true and principal adornment of churches, namely, Sermons, Hymns, Prayer and the Communion; or to a remembrance of the thing which is represented by the image, with a political end, as the image of Caesar upon the coin putteth in mind both that the coin is Caesar's and that political obedience is owed to him. But if abuse and superstition be added, as when men abuse the images left in churches, the Magistrate is to be admonished of his office, that they follow Hezekiah, who, when he saw the Brazen Serpent of Moses worshipped and adored by the Israelites, broke and shattered it, and called it *Nehushtan*, that is, a thing of brass, because it had nothing of divine power or godhead, **4 Kings 18 [2 Kings 18]**.

### *De Quarto, videlicet de comminatione et promissione addita primo praecepto* · On the Fourth Point, namely on the Threat and the Promise Added to the First Commandment

First let the general threat be considered, **Deut. 27**: *"Cursed is every one that continueth not in all things which are written in the book of the law."* The general promise also is this, **Levit. 18**: *"He that doeth these things shall live in them."* Then to the First Commandment there is added a peculiar threat and promise, comprehended in these words, **Exod. 20**:

> *I am the Lord God, strong, jealous, visiting the iniquity of the fathers unto the third and fourth generation of them that hate me:*
>
> *And showing mercy unto thousands, to them that love me and keep my words.*

Let us know, therefore, that the breaking of the Decalogue is punished with eternal death, unless remission be made through and for the sake of the Son, the Mediator.

<!-- p.76 -->

Let us know also that temporal punishments are added, unto the third and fourth generation, as the examples of Saul, of David also, and of Solomon, and of others in all times do witness. And although it is written: *"The son shall not bear the iniquity of the father,"* which is altogether true of eternal punishments, yet temporal punishments range through many who tread in the footsteps of their fathers. For they are excepted whom the Gospel exempteth, namely, they that repent, or they that hate not God, that is, who love God with the whole heart and obey his commandments.

The promise showeth that grace doth abound above sin. For as in the threat mention is made of the third and fourth generation, so in the promise God maketh mention of many thousands.

Now in the promises of the Law let us consider these four things.

**First**, how the rewards come to pass, seeing the promises are conditional and the Law is not satisfied. For the rewards come to pass for obedience even begun, because God approveth this for Christ's sake.

**Secondly**, seeing the godly are subject to the cross, what things can be called true rewards. Here there is need of the doctrine of the Gospel, which interpreteth the Law and teacheth concerning the cross: namely, that in this life the godly are afflicted, and yet both have in this life spiritual rewards and certain bodily ones, and shall have after this life the true rewards, as it is written: *"Godliness hath promise of the life that now is and of that which is to come."* Likewise: *"Great is your reward in heaven."*

**Thirdly**, in what order bodily good things are to be asked, which God certainly willeth to be asked and looked for; yet so, that we prescribe not to him the manner or the time, but patiently bear the cross and the delay of help, and keep the degrees, that we make more account of the glory of God <!-- p.77 --> than of our own profit, and know that the salvation of the soul is to be preferred before our own will and profit, and that we are always heard of God, if not according to our will, yet unto our salvation.

**Fourthly**, let us consider that by the expectation of rewards faith is exercised, which determineth that good things are given us of God and do not befall by chance; and determineth withal that our obedience pleaseth God, not for its own worthiness, but for Christ's sake; and that God willeth to make good his promise, and to be our debtor, because he hath promised to them who are reconciled by faith for Christ's sake. These things it is profitable to know in general concerning the threats and promises in the whole Decalogue.

## *Secundum Praeceptum* · The Second Commandment

The order of the commandments is worthy of consideration. The First Commandment teacheth of the inward worship of the mind, of the knowledge of God, and of the motions of the will and heart whereby we ought to draw near unto God and to rest in him.

But the Second Commandment preacheth of the outward confession of the mouth, and showeth of what sort our speech ought to be, which God willeth to spring from godly motions of the heart. For to this end is the benefit of tongue and speech given to men, that they may praise God and teach others concerning God.

*The Name of God* is the term for the very Word of God, whereby God hath revealed himself, and whereby he willeth to be known and called upon; and whatsoever is proclaimed concerning God out of the revealed Word.

*To take the Name of God in vain* signifieth to use the Name or Word of God irreverently and profanely, without the honour and veneration that is due.

But how the Name of God is to be used rightly, godly and holily, is shown by the *affirmative*, that is, the order of virtues which <!-- p.78 --> the Second Commandment setteth forth. And how the Name of God is taken in vain is shown by the *Antithesis*, or the negative, and the abuse. The virtues of the Second Commandment are:

> The true invocation of God.  
> Thanksgiving.  
> True preaching.  
> Confession.  
> The oath.  

**The invocation of God** is a virtue which asketh of the true God, revealed in the promise of the Gospel and in the sending of the Son, good things eternal and present, and, striving against doubt, doth certainly determine that we are heard and obtain the good things desired, according to his promises, for the sake of Christ the Mediator.

Now although the commandments of the Decalogue are distinct, and invocation is a work of the First Commandment so far as concerneth the true motion of the heart (for in the First Commandment it is spoken of the inward invocation, which is made by the thought of the mind, and by faith in the will, and by the heart fleeing unto God, and asking and expecting good things of him), yet God requireth also the outward invocation and the express voice, which uttereth those motions of the heart and agreeth with them; that there may be a conformity of the whole man with God, and that the motions of the heart may be stirred up and grow continually and become more fervent, and others be confirmed, instructed and invited, and withal that the devils may be affrighted and flee when they hear the invocation of the true God — which indeed alone is the true amulet that driveth away the Devil and all hurts. For this cause the Son of God saith: *"When ye pray, say."* For he requireth the express voice.

Let the solution, therefore, of a common argument be known to the boys:

> The commandments of the Decalogue are distinct.  
> Invocation is a work of the First Commandment.  
> Therefore it is not rightly referred to the Second Commandment.  

<!-- p.79 -->

I answer to the major: The commandments of the Decalogue are distinct, yet so that the First Commandment is always the fountain and rule, wherein all the other commandments and their virtues ought to be included. For out of the true knowledge of God, the fear of God, faith and love, that is, out of the godly motions of the heart, prayer and the other virtues ought to spring.

To the minor the answer is now easy: The First Commandment preacheth of the inward invocation, the Second of the outward.

**Thanksgiving** is to confess with mind and voice that good things are not offered us by chance, nor by second causes only, but that God is truly the author of all benefits; and by this our confession to invite others to the knowledge and invocation of the true God, that they also may certainly determine that the godly are cared for of God and are heard, and may therefore with a true heart submit themselves unto God and praise him.

**True preaching** is to teach faithfully and purely the true doctrine concerning the essence and will of God, revealed in the Law and the Gospel, whether in the public ministry or elsewhere according to a man's place and calling, and skilfully and constantly to refute the corruptions of the true meaning.

**Confession** is a constant and perpetual will, before God and men, to profess the true doctrine of the Gospel, and not to cast it away for terrors, hatreds, punishments or other perils; to this end, that God may be obeyed, and that the true knowledge of God be not quenched on earth.

To confession some refer also the rule concerning **good report**, or a good name, which is, namely, a testimony ordained of God, of the conscience judging rightly, and of others judging rightly, and approving deeds rightly done, and disapproving those who judge otherwise. For God willeth the difference of virtues and vices to be understood, and willeth evil examples <!-- p.80 --> to be shunned and punished, and others to be invited to the study of godliness and to deeds rightly done.

**An oath** is an invocation of the true God, whereby we ask that he be the witness of our speech and punish them that deceive.

But as to that which is asked, whether it be lawful for Christians to swear, seeing Christ saith: *"I say unto you, Swear not at all"* — to this let the true answer be held, namely, that it is lawful for Christians to swear, when the oath is either ***ἔπακτον***, that is, tendered by a lawful Magistrate, to whom obedience is owed (especially seeing all honest political works are granted to Christians), or is undertaken at the constraint of one's calling, that we may defend either our own or others' good name and life.

This judgement is confirmed, first, by testimonies, as the Law saith: *"Thou shalt swear by the name of the Lord"*; and **Jerem. 12**: *"They shall swear, The Lord liveth"*; then by examples: of God himself, as, *"As I live"*; of Christ, *"Verily, verily"*; of Paul, **2 Corinth. 1**: *"I call God for a record upon my soul."* And in **Hebr. 6** it is said: *"An oath is an end of all strife."* And all these things now show that the commandment of Christ which forbiddeth to swear is to be understood of all oaths which break forth privately, without the command of authority, and likewise without the constraint of a calling, and are made by human lightness that considereth not the greatness of an oath. For to swear lightly, idly and without cause is an abuse of the Divine Name, because the Name of God is invoked without reverence.

This abuse Christ willeth to take away; therefore he addeth: *"Whatsoever is more cometh of evil,"* that is, of irreverence. Others answer that Christ forbiddeth the unlawful ways of swearing: *"Neither by heaven, nor by the earth."* For seeing an oath is an invocation, the honour of God is not to be transferred to creatures.

### *Antithesis* · The Antithesis

<!-- p.81 -->

Briefly, the vices or sins which fight against the Second Commandment may be rehearsed thus. With **the true invocation of God** there fight the omitting of invocation, and profane security; the invocation that is heathenish, Turkish, Jewish, idolatrous; the invocation of dead men, of saints and of creatures.

With **thanksgiving** there fight ingratitude, and a feigned and hypocritical gratitude, such as is that of the Pharisee, **Luke 18**, unbelieving and arrogant.

With **true preaching** there fight all corruptions of doctrine, heresies, negligence in teaching, and curiosity in handling unprofitable or unnecessary matters.

With **confession** there fight apostasy, or falling away or denial, the casting away of the truth; likewise unjust silence; and also ungodly stubbornness in fanatical opinions.

With **the oath** there fight sophistical and slanderous interpretations of oaths; pretexts of the glory and Name of God; ungodly oaths; unlawful vows, as those of Jephthah, of Herod, and of monks, canons and the like, who promise things dishonest and forbidden of God, or such wherein ungodly opinions cleave, which hurt the glory of Christ and transfer the benefit of Christ to works devised by men; likewise all perjuries, all blasphemies, and all abuses of the Divine Name. And let these sayings be held as rules:

*An oath ought not to be a bond of iniquity. In promises which cannot be performed without sin, the covenants are to be rescinded.*

*All ungodly vows and deeds, contrary to the commandment of God, are void.* That oaths which fight not against the manifest Word of God, howsoever they be extorted, are to be kept even to an enemy, godliness and civil honesty do persuade. There is an example in the Gibeonites, **Joshua 9**. Concerning perjuries the little verses are well known:

<!-- p.82 -->

> *Ah miser, et si quis primo periuria celat,*  
> *Sera tamen tacitis poena venit pedibus.*  

> Ah, wretched man! though one at first conceal his perjuries,  
> yet punishment cometh late, on silent feet.[^C2-4]  

### *De comminatione et promissione secundi praecepti* · On the Threat and the Promise of the Second Commandment

The threat is in these words: *"God will not hold him guiltless, whosoever shall take his Name in vain"*; that is, God doth horribly punish the abuse and profanation of his Name with present and eternal punishments, as the dreadful examples of Cerinthus, Arius, Nestorius, Monetarius [Müntzer], Carlstadt and others in all ages do witness. And the promise, often repeated, is: *"Them that honour me I will honour,"* **1 Sam. 2**. **Deut. 28**: *"Blessed shalt thou be in the city, and blessed shalt thou be in the field; blessed shall be the fruit of thy body,"* etc.

## *Tertium Praeceptum* · The Third Commandment

Seeing the Church is gathered by the voice of Doctrine, there is need of an assembly, and of signs, which are the sinews of the assembly. Rightly therefore, after it hath been spoken in the First Commandment of the conformity of the mind and will with God, and in the Second Commandment of the conformity of speech, it is now spoken in the Third of the institution and preservation of the ministry of teaching in the Church. For the law of the Sabbath comprehendeth the whole ministry.

Rightly also is this admonition set before: that of the commandments of the Decalogue some are *absolute*, namely, those which require the obedience of the heart privately, and which can exist in men who are alone and separated from the common society of men, as are faith, the fear of God, and most of the other virtues of the First and Second Commandments; others are *relative*, which cannot be exercised outside the society of men, as the Third and Fourth Commandments.

Of the Sabbath, that is, of rest and ceasing, we shall say a few things. <!-- p.83 --> The first: why God blessed the seventh day and appointed it to sacred uses. The second is concerning the ceremonial Sabbath, which was repeated and confirmed in the Third Commandment of the Decalogue. The third, concerning the symbolical signification and type of the ceremonial Sabbath.

**1.** God therefore chose the seventh day for sacred uses, because on that day he rested from every work of creation; that is, he made no new kind in nature whose matter or likeness had not gone before. He willed also to show that man, whom he had made last after all the other creatures, was the highest and final work, wherein God took delight and wherein he rested. And he willed by this very sanctification of the Sabbath to institute a certain worship, wherein mankind, even in its innocency — that is, even if Adam had not fallen — should proclaim the goodness of God and render services pleasing unto God, whereas other things were to be cared for on the remaining days. This sanctification of the Sabbath was also to be a notable testimony of another life, or of the eternal rest, into which man, even before the fall, should have been translated when a certain time was past. In this manner we see that the Sabbath was sanctified of God for four causes: 1. for rest; 2. for the excellency of man; 3. for the institution of a certain worship; 4. for a testimony of immortality.

From the sanctification of this day it hath come to pass that the number seven hath always been called sacred both among the Jews and among the heathen, and, as experience witnesseth, hath had and still hath a peculiar virtue in many things, in the changes and vicissitudes of kingdoms, of diseases, of life, of condition and estate. Hence are the climacteric years, hence the critical days, hence the periods of kingdoms, hence also the changes of private life, and many other things, of which wise men have often disputed, **Macrobius**, **Gellius** and others. And **Pythagoras** was wont to call the number seven the vehicle of human life. <!-- p.84 --> And experience witnesseth that commonly in the seventh year of a man's age, and in the same year as it is repeated from time to time and observed, something of change and fortune doth befall. But I break off these things, and remember the saying of David: *"Commit thy way unto God, and he shall bring it to pass."*

**2.** The ceremonial Sabbath, or the pedagogical and Mosaic Sabbath, was nothing else than a remembrance of the former sanctified Sabbath, and a worship of God performed on the seventh day, and an admonition concerning the Sabbath to come, as we shall say a little after. For God willed his people to be put in mind of the rest of God in the works of creation, and of the sanctification of the seventh day; and he willed the outward works of the body to be laid aside and deferred which hinder the ministry and holy works, such as are: that the ministry be celebrated, that others be taught concerning God, that the Sacraments be administered, etc.

Now seeing this commandment and institution of the Mosaic Sabbath is mixed — partly *moral*, as touching the genus, or the work itself; partly *ceremonial*, as touching the species, or the certain day — and the ceremonial laws delivered by Moses are abolished, and we Christians have no commandment concerning a certain day, therefore we know that the special or ceremonial part is abrogated by reason of Christian liberty, the genus being retained, which is moral, perpetual and unchangeable. For this cause the seventh day hath been transferred by Christians to the day following, which we name ***κυριακόν***, or the Lord's day, for a remembrance of the glorious Resurrection of the Son of God.

Here let the difference of the commandments of God be observed. Some commandments are named *Catholic*, which bind all and each either to obedience or to punishment. Others are *particular*, which were given only to one nation or to one man. Concerning these, three rules are delivered. **The first**: No commandment is so particular but that it doth in some measure pertain to all. **The second**: Whatsoever is <!-- p.85 --> joined to the particular commandments of God out of outward circumstances — place, time, person, number, order and the like — all that is to be left to those to whom those things were particularly commanded. **The third**: Whatsoever remaineth of faith and charity, that is to be transferred and applied to us all and each. Therefore the observation of the Sabbath, so far as it is ceremonial, is left to the Jews, that is, it concerneth not us; but so far as it requireth faith and charity, so far it pertaineth unto us.

Here let the boys learn the solution of a school argument. Paul saith, **Coloss. 2**: *"Let no man judge you in respect of an holyday."* Therefore the commandment of the Sabbath doth no longer bind us. I answer to the antecedent: Paul speaketh of the ceremony and of the observation of outward circumstances; he speaketh not of the genus, or of the principal meaning of the commandment and its final cause, which is natural and unchangeable.

Likewise: *Idleness begetteth vices. The Sabbath is idleness. Therefore it is pernicious.* I answer to the major: Idleness that is dishonest and illiberal, and not sanctified, breedeth vices, and is the occasion of many evils. To the minor: The Sabbath requireth rest together with sanctification. For the ceasing from labours on the seventh day was instituted, not for slothfulness or immoderate feastings, but for sanctification. Because the common people omit the sanctification, their rest becometh to them an occasion of offences.

There is also to be shunned a superstitious resting[^C2-5] or observation of the Sabbath, such as the Jews feign, and which Christ refuteth, **Matth. 12**, with many arguments, of which it is spoken elsewhere. Forty years ago, it is reported, it happened at Magdeburg that a Jew fell into a sewer on the Sabbath day. The Jews, because of the observation of the seventh day, asked of the Christians that they would draw the Jew out, for that it was now their Sabbath, and they ought not to labour on that day. The Christians answered: *"Keep ye <!-- p.86 --> your Sabbath; to-morrow we shall keep ours, neither will we suffer that on the Lord's day such a servile work be done either by you or by us."* So the Jew was drawn out only on the third day.

**3.** The signification of the Sabbath regardeth either the time past, or the present, or the time to come. If the time **past** be considered, the Sabbath was a type of the rest of the day from the works of creation, and a remembrance of the sanctification of the seventh day and of all created things. It was also a remembrance of the rest of the people of Israel brought out of Egypt, as the Epistle to the **Hebrews, chap. 4**, showeth out of **Psalm 95**: *"I sware in my wrath, They shall not enter into my rest."* To us it is a remembrance of the Sabbath wherein the Son of God rested in the sepulchre the whole seventh day, which we therefore name the *Sabbath of redemption*.

If the time **present** be considered, the Sabbath is a figure of the rest of consciences from sin and the wrath of God, from the curse of the Law and from hell, through and for the sake of Christ. Next, it is a sign of the sanctification whereby God sanctifieth us and chooseth us to be his dwelling-places and temples. Then, the Sabbath admonisheth us of our life, and of the study of godliness, of holy conversation and patience, and of the meditation of the heavenly and eternal rest.

If the time **to come** be considered, the Sabbath is a type of the consummation of the world and of the eternal Sabbath, whereof it is written, **Isa. 66**: *"There shall be Sabbath out of Sabbath."* And **Heb. 3 and 4**: *"There remaineth a Sabbath for the people,"* namely, when the people of God shall have rest from the labour of the present miseries in this life, and from the temptations and bondage of the devil. Hither pertaineth the saying of Elias concerning the six thousands of years of the world.[^C2-6] And **Irenaeus** speaketh after this manner: *"In as many days as the world was made, in so many thousands of years is it consummated. For one day of the Lord is as a thousand years. Now in six days were the things finished which <!-- p.87 --> were made; therefore the consummation of them is the six-thousandth year."*[^C2-7] Thus much of the Sabbath.

Now from the things which have been said it is easily manifest what is to be determined concerning the **five kinds of Sabbath**. For five kinds are rehearsed. **The first** is the *Sabbath of creation*, whereof it is written, **Genes. 2**: *"In six days God made heaven and earth, and rested on the seventh day from all his work, and blessed it"*; that is, God made no other bodily nature more excellent than man, nor more worlds, but rested in man as in his last and highest work. **The second** Sabbath is the *ceremonial* or pedagogical, as we said before, which the Jews by divine commandment kept on the seventh day, which is commonly named the day of Saturn, in memory of the creation of all things. But the Christians — first the Apostles, and then their disciples — in memory of the Resurrection of Christ, which came to pass on the Lord's day, so called from that same Resurrection, that is, on the day of the Sun, which is the first day of the week, and that they might show an example of Christian liberty in abrogating the ceremonies pertaining to the Mosaic polity, changed the seventh day and chose the first day, which is the day of the Sun, or the Lord's day, abolishing at the same time the rites of sacrifices which were in use among the Jews. For thus saith **Ignatius**: ***μετὰ τὸ σαββατίσαι*** (that is, after the Jewish Sabbath now abrogated) ***ἑορταζέτω πᾶς φιλόχριστος τὴν κυριακήν, τὴν ἀναστάσιμον, τὴν βασιλίδα, τὴν ὕπατον πασῶν τῶν ἡμερῶν· ἣν περιμένων ὁ προφήτης ἔλεγεν εἰς τὸ τέλος, ὑπὲρ τῆς ὀγδόης· ἐν ᾗ καὶ ἡ ζωὴ ἡμῶν ἀνέτειλε, καὶ τοῦ θανάτου γέγονε νίκη ἐν Χριστῷ*** (after the keeping of the Sabbath, let every lover of Christ keep festival on the Lord's day, the day of the Resurrection, the queen, the chief of all days; looking for which the prophet said, "Unto the end, for the eighth"; on which also our life arose, and the victory over death was wrought in Christ).[^C2-8]

Here let the younger sort learn the solution of common arguments:

> The Mosaic ordinances are abolished.  
> The commandment of the Sabbath is a Mosaic ordinance.  
> Therefore the commandment of the Sabbath is abolished.  

<!-- p.88 -->

I answer: The major is true of positive law, or of the ceremony or species; but it is not true of the genus, that is, so far as a certain moral thing is understood, whereby all men are bound to godliness. The minor: The commandment of the Sabbath is a Mosaic ordinance or ceremony as to the species, that is, as to the observation of a certain day and the manner of ceasing. For this species is abolished. But the same commandment is natural or moral as touching the genus, as hath been said before.

Likewise:

> The species is abolished in the Third Commandment.  
> The ceasing or rest on the seventh day is a species, or ceremony of positive law.  
> Therefore it is abolished.  

I answer in the same manner to the minor: The manner of ceasing is a ceremony of positive law, which is abolished. But the genus, which is to omit servile works that draw away from the ministry, is moral, and bindeth all men and ages.

Moreover, let the things which were said above in the *Institutio* concerning the Mosaic sacrifices, and likewise concerning Christian liberty, be repeated in this place. Let the causes also be considered for which the polity of Israel was established. For the Mosaic polity was established with this purpose, that there might be a certain place and a certain stock wherein the Messiah should be born, should teach, should bring forth testimonies, should offer sacrifice and be raised again. And God willed this people to be seen and to be separated from other nations; therefore he assigned to it certain rites. And that polity endured 1582 years, from the Going out of Egypt even to the last destruction of Jerusalem wrought by Titus. And together with the Mosaic polity the ceremonial and judicial laws were extinguished, because these were in very deed the sinews of that polity, or rather the polity itself; and, as touching the species, which is of positive law, they do in no wise bind other nations, as is manifest from the Acts of the Apostles, <!-- p.89 --> **chap. 15**, and from the Epistle to the Galatians.

Further, **the third** Sabbath is the *spiritual*, or the Sabbath of the soul, which is to be given to the study of the heavenly doctrine and to cease from sins or evil works, as it is written, **Exod. 31**: *"Ye shall keep the sabbath; for it is holy unto you."* For to sanctify the Sabbath is to dedicate soul, body and all actions unto God, and to meditate upon and to spread abroad the Kingdom of God and his benefits, and to follow after a holy conversation and life, and to rest in Christ, patiently bearing miseries, until God shall have translated us into the glory of his Kingdom. We sanctify the Sabbath, therefore, in these ways:

1. When we earnestly meditate upon and handle the Word of God.
2. When we rightly use the ceremonies.
3. When we further godliness according to our calling.
4. When we help the members of the Church with every kind of good office.
5. When, living holily, we patiently await our calling away out of this miserable life to the eternal Sabbath.

For this cause there is added to the Third Commandment a peculiar and emphatical word: *"Remember."* And the Greeks have prefixed to **Psalm 38** the title: *"For a remembrance, before the Sabbath"*; that is, in order that sins against conscience may be acknowledged and shunned, and a man may live in continual humility and patience, there is need of remembrance, that is, of a diligent and godly study of the heavenly Doctrine.

**The fourth**, the *Great Sabbath*, is the wonderful Rest of the Son of God in his Passion, emptying himself, and resting in the sepulchre the whole seventh day. And this is the Sabbath of Redemption, which some take of the whole Passion, or rather of the whole humiliation of Christ even unto the death of the cross, rendering obedience to the Father — especially seeing **Irenaeus** saith that Christ suffered, was crucified and died, *the Word resting*,[^C2-9] <!-- p.90 --> that is, not putting forth his power; but some understand it only of the rest in the sepulchre, which came to pass on the seventh day, which was the day of the Great Sabbath. Now seeing both judgements are true, we may distinguish this Sabbath into the *Sabbath of the whole exinanition*, and the *Sabbath of the sepulchre*, where Christ rested from his Passion, when he had finished the work of the redemption of mankind by his obedience. But the exinanition (*exinanitio*, the emptying) doth not signify an absence, a failing, a nakedness or an emptiness of the fulness of the Godhead, which dwelt in Christ bodily from the very moment of his conception; but it regardeth the use or exercise, namely, that the Godhead, as it were covered with human infirmity, did not always put itself forth in the human nature and through it.

This I am minded to add by the way: that there are certain men of an ill judgement who, when they speak of this Sabbath of the rest of the Son of God in his Passion, do thence infer that, as the exinanition came to pass *the Word resting*, so the exaltation also is to be understood, not of the human nature in Christ, but of the whole person according to both natures; so that the saying, *"All power is given unto me,"* is not to be taken of the man Christ, but that word, *"is given,"* is to be expounded in this manner: *"is restored unto me."* This opinion is utterly false. For the exinanition doth not signify a separation, or a casting away,[^C2-10] or an absence of the eternal power, but signifieth a certain forbearing of its exercise in the human nature. And the exaltation and glorification are to be taken of the human nature. *"God hath highly exalted Christ, and given him a name which is above every name,"* saith Paul. *"Now these things are to be understood of the temple, which is his body,"* saith **Athanasius**. *"For the Most High is not exalted, but the flesh of the Most High is exalted."* ***καὶ τὸ δεσποτικὸν σῶμα τῇ θείᾳ δόξῃ δεδοξασμένον παρὰ τῶν ἐπουρανίων προσκυνεῖται δυνάμεων*** (and the Lord's body, glorified with the divine glory, is worshipped by the heavenly powers), saith **Theodoret**. And **Oecolampadius** himself, upon these words (*"He was made higher than the heavens"*), speaketh thus: *"Let these things be understood, not of the Godhead, <!-- p.91 --> because God is not exalted, but of the humanity of Christ."* The same is affirmed by the Sacramentarians themselves, and they cannot deny the manifest truth, as **Wolfgang Musculus** and **Bullinger**, upon the place **Matth. 11**: *"All things are delivered unto me of the Father."* For that those things are to be taken ***ἀνθρωπίνως*** (after the manner of a man) of the glorified body of Christ, godly antiquity doth always witness. And these things I note by the way for this reason, that we may learn to flee the snares of those who, when they speak of the Sabbath of the rest of the Son of God in his Passion, do suppose the Word so to have rested that they understand a loss of power for a time, which was afterwards restored to Christ after the Resurrection; and this they do only to the end that they may lessen the power of Christ the Man. But of these things elsewhere.

**The fifth** Sabbath is the *eternal*, which is promised to all and each that believe in the Son of God, namely, when they are raised again from death, when God shall be all in all.

These things being dispatched, the works of the Third Commandment are now to be considered, which are to be taken from a comparing of them that teach and them that hear.

But first let the degrees of ministers in the Church be held.

**A Prophet in the Old Testament** is a person immediately called of God to teach and to set forth the Gospel, having a testimony that he doth not err, and withal sent to govern certain political counsels; as Moses, Samuel, Elijah, Elisha, Isaiah, Jeremiah, etc.

**A Prophet in the New Testament** is a minister raised up of God, and mediately called to teach, but immediately to restore and to set forth the doctrine when it is fallen; having a testimony that he doth not err, namely, if he depart not from the voice of the Prophets and Apostles. For departing from this he erreth. Such a prophet was **Athanasius**, likewise **Augustine**, and in our age **Luther**.

**An Apostle** is a person immediately called of God to teach <!-- p.92 --> the Gospel, having a testimony that he doth not err; yet not sent to rule the counsels of political government. And he hath a universal calling, that he may teach in any place whatsoever. Wherefore it is necessary to hear the voice of the Apostles as a voice sounding from heaven.

**An Evangelist** is a person mediately called of God to teach the Gospel in many places and to establish Churches.

**A Bishop or Superintendent** is a Pastor, and a person mediately called of God, through men — the magistrate or the Church — to the ministry of the Gospel, and to the public ministration of the Sacraments, and to the discharging of the other parts of the ministry in a certain place; setting forth the doctrine delivered by the Prophets and Apostles, from which when he departeth, he straightway erreth.

**A Doctor** is a person mediately called to teach the Gospel, or to expound the prophetical and apostolical books, whether in the church, or in the school, or in the household; not to the ministration of the Sacraments, as the pastor, nor to the chief government of the Church. And such in old time were the Catechists.

The word *ministers* putteth us in mind of the difference between ecclesiastical and political government. *"The kings of the Gentiles exercise lordship; but ye shall not be so,"* saith the Son of God, **Luke 22**. Let the ministry therefore be a ministry, not a lordship. And notable is the saying of **Chrysostom**: *"Whosoever shall desire primacy on earth shall find confusion in heaven; neither shall he be reckoned among the servants of Christ who shall treat of primacy."*

Now the duties of ministers, or of them that teach, are these three: knowledge, constancy and diligence; which three are comprehended in the word *faithfulness*. A teacher must know the true doctrine delivered of God, that he may be able faithfully and rightly to set it before others. Let him be constant also in the acknowledged truth, and diligent in all the parts of the ministry, of which there are six:

1. The sermon, or true preaching.
2. <!-- p.93 -->The lawful administration of the Sacraments.
3. The absolution of them that repent.
4. The visitation and consolation of the afflicted.
5. The excommunication of the wicked.
6. Godliness of life, and honest manners.
