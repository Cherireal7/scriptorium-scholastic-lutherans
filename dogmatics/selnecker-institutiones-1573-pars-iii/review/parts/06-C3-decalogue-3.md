The offices of hearers and learners are six.

1. Diligence in learning, hearing, trusting, and living godly.
2. Reverence toward the ministers.
3. Obedience in those things which pertain to the ministry.
4. ***ἐπιείκεια*** (equity, forbearance) in pardoning the infirmities of godly ministers.
5. The contribution of their substance for the support of them that teach.
6. The godly observance of the public ceremonies.

### Question: Whether the Maccabees, by fighting, violated the Sabbath?

A rule is to be held: The necessary offices of love are to be preferred before ceremony, yet so that ceremony be not utterly abolished. For it is written: *"I will have mercy rather than sacrifice."* In the case, therefore, where the rest of the sabbath cannot be kept together with the necessary defence of the people — in which not only the bodies of the citizens but the ministry itself is to be defended — the magistrate doth rightly in that he fighteth for sacred things and religion, *"both alone and with many,"* as it is said in the old oath.[^C3-1] Nay, the defence of the ministry is a work of the sabbath. And this dispensation is not an abrogation of the law, but a confirmation of it. And when in a case of necessity this office is neglected and set below ceremonies, there is assuredly sin. We add this also: that no offices which are not of evil example are to be blamed, provided only that we render unto the public ministry its own honour.

<!-- p.94 -->

But the pretext of many is to be reproved, even of Princes, who oftentimes, in cases not necessary, drive their subjects, countrymen and townsmen, upon feast days to servile labours, huntings, buildings, and the like. Such men consider not what that word *Memento*, "Remember," meaneth; and at the last they perish miserably, as examples testify. **King Alfonso**, when his captains urged an assault and inroad upon the enemy on the Day of Christ's Nativity, answered better, saying: *"I set more by this Day than by any victory."*

### Another question: What is to be thought concerning Ecclesiastical ordinances

A rule is to be held: *"In vain do they worship me with the commandments of men"*; that is, no work is a worship of God which God hath not commanded by his word. Let the distinction therefore be held between rites divinely commanded — as in the New Testament are Baptism, the Lord's Supper, and all the sacrifices or good works of believers — and rites instituted in the Church by human authority for the sake of good order and discipline. For these ceremonies instituted by men are not worship of God, neither do they merit the forgiveness of sins nor other rewards, but have an end that is merely political, namely, that they bring about order, or ***εὐταξίαν*** (good order), in this common life, and serve the ministry. And as honest discipline is to be maintained, so also, for discipline's sake, such ceremonies are not to be violated, if the opinion of worship, of merit, and of rewards be absent.

### Another question, concerning oblation

That oblation is a work of the third commandment there is no doubt, seeing it pertaineth to the preservation of the ministry, toward the support of whose ministers, and of others who are truly poor, every man ought to contribute something. And this custom was always from the beginning in the <!-- p.95 --> Church, even among the Fathers before the law, namely, that in the public assembly collections should be made for the support of the ministers and of the poor. Thence also is the name ***συμβολῆς***, *symbolae* (a contribution), and *Collecta*, the Collect, which word hath remained in the Church for a prayer, because the alms that had been collected were set forth in the assembly, and the minister gave thanks to God for his goodness and for his sustaining of the Church, and prayed for blessing upon the benefactors. Hence also it appeareth that the word *Missa*, the Mass, arose, which among the Hebrews signifieth a contribution, as **Deut. 16**: *"Thou shalt offer* Missah*,"* that is, *"a contribution or freewill oblation of thine hand, according to the blessing of the Lord thy God."*[^C3-2] And ***ἀγάπη*** signifieth alms, by which name they sometimes, with some licence, called the *synaxis* (the assembly) itself, wherein the alms were distributed. Now the example flowed from the people of Israel, among whom they that were about to sacrifice brought a victim and gave a part unto the priests, according to that saying, **Exod. 23**: *"Thou shalt not appear empty before me."* The end of this contribution was thankfulness toward the ministry, and help to be rendered to the poor. Such an oblation, made to this end, pleaseth God at all times. For we signify thereby that we owe reverence to the ministry, and thanks and wages to the ministers. And if superstition be absent, and the opinion of worship and of peculiar honour, that oblation cannot but please God. Today the thing is everywhere neglected, in so great security and unthankfulness of the world, although the name of *Collect* hath remained in the prayer.

### *De festis diebus* · Of feast days

The Jews had every year three solemn feasts: the Passover, in memory of the deliverance from the bondage of the Egyptians; Pentecost, in memory of the law given upon mount Sion;[^C3-3] and the Feast of Tabernacles (*Scenopegia*), in memory of the people's dwelling in the wilderness forty years in tents; that so by this ceremony they might call to remembrance the whole benefit of the deliverance out of <!-- p.96 --> Egypt, of which mention was made above in the exposition of the first commandment. In the room of these three Jewish feasts the Church hath put the Passover (Easter), in memory of the Lord's Resurrection; and Pentecost, in remembrance of the confirmation of the Gospel by the visible giving of the Holy Ghost; and the Nativity of the Lord, in memory of the Saviour given unto us.

And these feasts are observed, that the history may be learned in order, and the benefits weighed, that we may rightly use them to the glory of God and our own salvation.

There are added also certain feasts of the Saints, not idolatrous nor superstitious, but such as set forth the history of the Church and the benefits of God, and move the minds of the godly to thanksgiving, and instruct us, and prescribe unto us examples of repentance, faith, and godliness, and stir up in us the desire of attaining unto the fellowship of the blessed.

Upon these feast days these works, or virtues, are required of a Christian man:

1. To shun the hindrances of the feast days: ***τὰς ἑορτὰς μὴ ἀτιμάζειν*** (not to dishonour the feasts).
2. Reverently to be present at the sacred assemblies.
3. Godly to meditate upon the sum of the doctrine that is set forth.
4. To use the ceremonies gravely and religiously.
5. To live holily.
6. To instruct, admonish, and reprove the household and others.
7. To exercise the works of charity, to visit the sick, to comfort the sorrowful, to help the needy.

### *Antithesis* · The Antithesis

They offend against the third Commandment, first, among them that teach, when they either teach false things and corrupt the doctrine, as do all heretics, Anabaptists, Donatists, Enthusiasts; or teach negligently; or altogether neither teach nor administer the sacra<!-- p.97 -->ments, as do the belly-Bishops, ***κακὰ θηρία, γαστέρες ἀργαί*** (evil beasts, idle bellies), and the Mass-priests (*Sacrificuli*), who deprave the true use of the Sacraments, and read Masses for gain's sake, and transform the Lord's Supper into a sacrifice. Many do away with[^C3-4] the ministry and the spoken word, as the Anabaptists, the Enthusiasts, and the Stenckfeldians.[^C3-5] Many bind[^C3-6] the dignity of the word and the efficacy of the Spirit to the dignity of the ministers, as the Donatists. And the crop of errors is infinite. Next, in the magistracy all those sin against the third commandment who take no care that their subjects be rightly taught, and who do not abolish idolatrous worships, the invocation of Saints, the adoration of images, and idolatrous Masses; likewise they who neglect the schools and the studies of the youth; likewise they who burden their subjects with labours not necessary upon feast days. Lastly, among the people they sin who come not together to the public assemblies in the Church, who are absent without probable cause, who negligently learn the doctrine of the Church, who neglect the Sacraments, who draw others away by their evil example, who bestow the feast days upon games, shows, huntings, journeys not necessary, banquets, and delights; who render not due reverence to the ministers of the Gospel, who find fault with them that teach, who slander honest ceremonies, who blame things rightly said and done, who contribute nothing to the support of the ministers, who are touched with no care of the sick, the sorrowful, and the needy, and are busied with no works of charity.

### *De comminatione et promissione tertii praecepti* · Of the threatening and the promise of the third commandment

The general threatening and promise is in **Luke 10**: *"He that despiseth you despiseth me. He that heareth you heareth me."* The special one is expressed in **Jeremiah 17**: *"If ye shall hallow the sabbath, Kings and Princes shall enter in by the gates, and the city shall be inhabited <!-- p.98 --> for ever. But if ye shall not hallow the sabbath, I will kindle a fire in the gates of Jerusalem, and it shall devour the houses, and shall not be quenched."* And in **Haggai** the first: *"Because ye neglect my house, the heavens give no dew, and the earth is made barren."* Hither pertain also these sayings: *"Seek ye first the Kingdom of God."* *"Whosoever shall give a drink of water unto one of these in my name, he shall not lose his reward."*

## *De Secunda Tabula* · Of the Second Table

What the difference is between the first and the second table, and how the works of the second table become worship of God, hath been said above. Now we shall speak of the fourth commandment: *"Honour thy father and thy mother,"* etc.

## *Quartum Praeceptum* · The Fourth Commandment

Whereas in the first table, through the several commandments, the order hath been appointed how God is to be acknowledged, called upon, and celebrated, now the fourth commandment, in the second table, nameth the place where those worships ought to be rendered, namely, in human society. Of this society, therefore, as before in the first table God was *immediately* the head, so now governors are appointed *mediately* by God; among whom parents deservedly hold the first place, who ought to be the rule of the other governors — nay, from which order the whole life of men in every estate draweth its beginning.

We shall speak briefly of these three things: 1. Of honour. 2. Of the name of father and mother, and of the office of superiors and inferiors. 3. Of the promise and threatening that are added.

### *De Honore* · Of Honour

Honour in general is the approving of another person or thing, ascribing unto it excellency, and reverently submitting oneself unto it, on <!-- p.99 --> account of some divine good — that is, either on account of the will of God who commandeth, or on account of wisdom and gifts of excellency, or on account of virtue and deserts — and declaring this opinion, or reverence, by outward signs.

The foundation of honour is some divine good in a person or in some thing. The degrees are: an honourable opinion, and a true subjection of mind, and the outward offices.

Rightly therefore and briefly it is wont thus to be said: Honour is the reverence whereby we venerate the dignity of our neighbour, which he either beareth by his office, or hath by the peculiar gifts of God.

For honour requireth not only the outward gesture — as are to rise up, to uncover the head, to give place, and the like, which make the difference between barbarians and them that are not barbarians, and yet very often deceive, and contain in them hypocrisy, yea poison, as experience witnesseth, and yet, if they proceed from a sincere and good mind, are worthy of all praise — but honour requireth a right mind and judgement, and an obedient will: as, if a man be said to honour his parents, he hath this testimony, that with true reverence he acknowledgeth God to be the author of this ordinance, and with true goodwill submitteth himself unto them, and from the heart rendereth the offices that are due, and is a fair judge of the faults of his parents, and not only willingly beareth their infirmities, but also, so far as may be done with faith and conscience unhurt, lightly and moderately excuseth and covereth them, as Shem and Japheth covered their father lying unwarily.

### *De appellatione Patris et Matris* · Of the name of Father and Mother

Under this name are comprehended not only the parents themselves — whose order and dignity is nevertheless set before the rest as the foundation and fountain — but all others to whom God hath committed the offices of parents, as are Preceptors, Guardians, Masters of households, Ministers of the word, and Magistrates. And <!-- p.100 --> as, ***συνεκδοχικῶς*** (by synecdoche), under the name of Parents all Superiors are signified, so under the name of children all Inferiors are denoted: Scholars, Wards, Household servants, Hearers, and subjects.

The virtues of this commandment, pertaining to the higher and the lower orders, are these.

1. *Pietas*, dutiful affection, or *philostorgia*, is the virtue which rendereth unto all that are joined by blood, but especially unto parents and children, due love, honour, and the offices that are due.

2. *Iustitia universalis*, universal justice, is the obedience owed unto God toward all honest laws in civil society.

3. *Iustitia distributiva*, distributive justice, is the virtue which discerneth the degrees of persons and offices in life, and keepeth the agreement of persons and offices, and assigneth to fit men apt functions, and a becoming honour and place.

4. *Sedulitas*, diligence, is the virtue which acknowledgeth and understandeth every man's own function or vocation, and diligently considereth the end of the vocation, and useth the means pertaining to that end, and faithfully and constantly, for God's sake and the common welfare, performeth the labours that are proper and necessary to the vocation.

5. *Gratitudo*, thankfulness, is a virtue comprehending truth, which acknowledgeth and professeth that it hath received a benefit from another; and comprehending therewith justice, which keepeth equality, and in turn rendereth unto them that have deserved well the thanks that are due for the benefit and for the goodwill of the mind, and other offices, according to the measure of its ability.

6. *Reverentia*, reverence, is a virtue rendering unto others their own place and a becoming honour, and acknowledging the gifts of GOD in others and highly esteeming them, and acknowledging its own infirmity, and submitting itself unto them, and declaring this subjection by outward tokens.

7. *Gravitas*, gravity, is the virtue which performeth the necessary offices <!-- p.101 --> of the vocation constantly, that is, after one and the same manner, and ruleth all actions and gestures, that they may agree with the order of nature, of persons, of places, and of times.

8. *Modestia*, modesty, is a virtue acknowledging its own infirmity, and not lifting itself above its strength, nor undertaking any thing outside its vocation, nor despising others, but in the fear of GOD and in faith diligently doing the necessary offices of the vocation after one and the same manner, according to right reason, and governing all the motions of mind and body, that they may agree with the order of nature, of the laws, of persons, of places, and of times.

9. *Civilitas morum*, civility of manners, is the virtue which ruleth the speech, the gestures, the carriage of the body, and all the actions, that they may agree with the order of nature, of times, of persons, of places, and of the other circumstances.

10. The desire and pursuit of a good name, or of glory, is the virtue which seeketh GOD for the witness and judge of its actions, and hath a good conscience, and highly esteemeth other good men who judge rightly, and resteth in their judgements — a glory always answering to true godliness toward God, and to eminent virtue, and to good deserts toward the Church and the Commonwealth.

These virtues having been rehearsed in general, now in the fourth commandment the *Oeconomia*, the household, and the *Politia*, the civil state, are set in order.

The household, the private government of house and family, having GOD for its author and preserver, consisteth as it were of three yokes: whereof the first is that of parents and children, likewise of preceptors and scholars; the second is that of husbands and wives; the third is that of Masters and servants.

The offices of parents are three: ***παιδεία***, ***νουθεσία***, and nourishment, **Eph. 6**. ***Παιδεία*** comprehendeth doctrine and discipline; ***νουθεσία***, godliness, invocation, and the knowledge of the heavenly doctrine; nourishment, bringing up and defence.

<!-- p.102 -->

**Daniel 13**: *"The parents of Susanna, being righteous, instructed their daughter in the law of Moses."* **Ephes. 6**: *"Provoke not your children to wrath, but bring them up in the discipline and admonition of the Lord."* **Col. 3**.

The offices of children are: love and reverence for GOD's sake, submitting itself unto the parents, and striving to be unto them a delight and an ornament; obedience; thankfulness in rendering the ***θρεπτηρίοις*** (the recompense for nurture), or ***ἐν τῷ ἀντιπελαργεῖν*** (in cherishing them in return, as the stork doth); patience, and ***ἐπιείκεια*** (forbearance) that beareth with the infirmities and blemishes of parents. **Sirach 3**: *"Honour thy parents in word, in deed, and in patience."*

The offices of Preceptors are: doctrine, containing ***σύνεσιν*** and ***δύναμιν ἑρμηνευτικήν*** (understanding, and the power of expounding), and discipline.

The offices of Scholars are: the love of letters, reverence of their Preceptors, diligence, obedience, and thankfulness. And hither pertaineth the *Cabala* out of the paradigms of **Donatus**, whom we read to have been the Preceptor of **Jerome**.[^C3-7] For the offices of Preceptors are portrayed in the conjugations. For the first rule ought to be the *love* of God and of the Scholars, toward whom the preceptor ought to be affected as a father toward his sons. And he that doth this findeth that he in turn *is loved* of God, and of the Scholars, and of other good men, and goeth forward into the field as a *Teacher*; and while he *teacheth* others, he instructeth himself: which that he may happily perform, he studieth beforehand, getteth knowledge of the matters, diligently *readeth*, and searcheth out those things which are to be set forth; and the matters aptly conceived in his mind by much reading and repeating he aptly and easily putteth into the mouths of the boys, as it were meat well chewed beforehand; and he in turn *is read*, that is, is sought after by others, and understood. Yet he is not ***αὐτοδίδακτος*** (self-taught), nor doth he stubbornly stand upon his own conceived opinion alone, but *heareth* also the judgements and opinions of others, gladly conferreth with his Colleagues, and by examining also requireth again of the Scholars, as a Catechist, the things which they have heard, and by his presence sharpeneth the diligence and attention of the hearers. While he performeth this, he <!-- p.103 --> himself in turn *is heard* by others, and his authority groweth by loving God, by teaching his hearers, by reading good Writers, and by hearing others who excel in judgement. But amid these things it is ever ***οἰστέον καὶ ἐλπιστέον*** (to be borne, and to be hoped): many injuries are to be *borne*, revilings to be endured, certain boorish manners of the Scholars to be gently corrected; and the little Verse is by all means to be considered:

> Be thou an ass, whomever hard fortune hath made an ass;  
> He that can calmly bear his lot, the same is wise.  

LIKEWISE:

> Be patient, if thou wilt be accounted wise and holy;  
> After the tears of the sowing, the harvest hath its joys.  

AND:

> For let the Ruler of the School be stout of heart, as Hector,  
> Patient as Noah, and knowing as the Sibyl.  

Many indeed have repented them of the vain and barren Chair, as a certain man hath written;[^C3-8] but it is to be considered what the vocation is, which assuredly is not to be forsaken, neither must we give way to the unthankfulness and the injuries of many. For it is rightly said:

> He that is wise doth in doubtful matters ask a mind  
> Of the divine Majesty, and doth what need requireth,  
> And committeth the issue unto God, who governeth all things.  

LIKEWISE:

> Do thine own, the things that are thine, which thy true function requireth;  
> All the rest commend thou unto God alone.  

AND:

> If thou art wise, let it be thine one care *to be* that which thou art.  

He therefore that showeth that patience which becometh us all who are Christians, the same in turn *is borne with* by God and men, and obtaineth pardon of his own blemishes and errors, seeing no man is ***ἀναμάρτητος*** (without fault); and now, his mind being strengthened and as it were overgrown with a hardened skin, he doth of his own accord the things that are to be done, look<!-- p.104 -->ing unto the glory of God, and the increase of the Church, and the profit of his country; and in serving others he is consumed: which thing boys were wont of old to say of wax Candles: *"It giveth light to others; it is worn away by use; but God restoreth."* And this now is the true Schoolmaster, worthy of praise and honour.

But seeing we have briefly discoursed of the offices of Preceptors — although out of the same paradigms the offices of Scholars also may be considered, who ought to love GOD and their Preceptors, and to cherish their studies, to repeat the things heard, to ask often, to keep the things asked, to teach the things kept, to hear others, to bear labours, and gladly and of their own accord to be present at sermons and lectures — yet now it pleaseth us to gather the offices of Scholars out of the paradigms of the declensions.

Let the learners therefore have, seek, and choose a fit *Master*, a Preceptor or Pedagogue, by whom they may be rightly instructed, publicly and privately, in doctrine and in the culture of manners. Let the boy be given to the *Muses*, that is, let him love GOD and call upon him, let him watch over his studies, and be tenacious of labours, as they of old said:

> To rise early, to pray to God, to study diligently,  
> To hasten to School, to observe the Preceptor,  
> To repeat with one's schoolfellows.  

Let him also bear many things — poverty, vexations, chastisements; let him not seek slothful, delicate, idle, and soft days. ***ἡ πενία τὴν σοφίαν ἔλαχε*** (poverty hath obtained wisdom for her portion).

> Much hath the boy borne and done; he hath sweated and been a-cold.[^C3-9]  
> Let him learn to serve, who would come unto great things.  
> He is worthy of flesh-meat, who well cheweth his pot-herbs.  

Let him also be a lover of religion, and studious of godliness in his life, gait, manners, gesture, and apparel; neither let him carry himself after the fashion of soldiers and men of war, but let him show forth his profession outwardly also. In this manner GOD will bless him with happiness and <!-- p.105 --> success of his labours, with plentiful fruit in his vocation and in the propagation of true doctrine, as it is wont to be said in the Verses:

> If thou learn rightly, thou shalt feed on roasted capons;  
> But if ill, thou shalt be fed with bran from the swine's ladle.  

AND:

> Your labour in the Lord shall not be vain.  
> ***ἐξ ἔργων ἄνδρες πολύμηλοί τ᾽ ἀφνειοί τε.***  
> (By works do men become rich in flocks and wealthy.)[^C3-10]  

But of these things thus far. Now the offices of husbands and wives are: 1. Love mutual, sweet, and sincere. 2. Conjugal faith. 3. The fellowship and sharing of goods and of hardships. 4. Generation, or the conjugal duty. 5. Defence. 6. ***ἐπιείκεια*** (forbearance), pardoning certain infirmities.

The offices of Masters are: 1. Godly to exercise the household by instructing, admonishing, reproving. 2. Not to despise servants as vile and abject chattels, but gently and lovingly to cherish them as fellow-servants of God. 3. Not to burden the servants with overmuch labours. 4. To render unto servants ***τὰ αὐτά*** (the same things), or that which is just and equal, as it is written: *"Thou shalt not muzzle the mouth of the ox that treadeth out the corn."* And: *"The Hireling is worthy of his hire."*

The offices of servants are: 1. To fear God. 2. To maintain simplicity, ingenuousness, truth, and candour. 3. To obey their Masters in all godly and honest actions. 4. To labour faithfully.

Thus far of the Household. But in the Civil State, when inquiry is made of the offices of the Magistrate, these six offices are rightly reckoned: 1. Let the Magistrate be the nursing-father of Churches and Schools. 2. Let him be the keeper of the whole Decalogue, of the first and of the second Table. 3. Let him be also the keeper of his own positive laws. 4. Let him be the executor of the Decalogue and of his own laws. 5. Let him be the keeper of the peace. 6. Let him be the father, and nourisher, and cherisher of the land and of the subjects.

The offices of subjects are: 1. To submit themselves unto the Magistrate <!-- p.106 --> for God's sake. 2. Reverently to love and fear the Magistrate. 3. To obey the laws divine and positive. 4. Not to refuse the services of warfare, both of the gown and of the field, for the defence of the public tranquillity, according to each man's vocation and measure. 5. To contribute tributes for the preservation of the government. 6. To bear the public burdens, if no atrocious injury be added. 7. To cover the blemishes of the Magistrate, when they are not enormous, and patiently to bear them. 8. To pray for the Magistrate.

The offices of Judges are: to fear God, diligently to take knowledge of the cause, to hear both parties, to be skilled in the law, to judge according to the laws, to be equal toward all according to one rule, to shun ***δωροφαγίαν*** (the devouring of gifts, that is, bribes).

In all and each of these degrees the bound of obedience is to be considered. For so far are Superiors to be obeyed, whether they be parents or Magistrates, as their commandments are not repugnant to the word of God and the law of nature. For then that saying hath place: *"We ought to obey God rather than men."* And **Luke 14**: *"If any man will come unto me, and hateth not his father and mother"* (namely, when they command him not to come unto Christ, or enjoin any thing against God), *"he cannot be my Disciple."*

### *Antithesis* · The Antithesis

Against *philostorgia* are repugnant ***ἀστοργία*** (want of natural affection), indulgence, the undutifulness and unthankfulness of children, and a superstitious and ungodly obedience.

Against universal justice are repugnant tyranny, injustice, wilful omission in government, contumacy, contempt of the laws and of the Magistrate, sedition, and the observing of ungodly laws.

Against distributive justice are repugnant the tyrannical confounding of degrees, the disturbing of offices, spite, hatred, sloth, ***πολυπραγμοσύνη*** (meddling in other men's matters), envy, and emulation.

<!-- p.107 -->

Against diligence are repugnant ***πολυπραγμοσύνη*** and sloth.

Against thankfulness, flattery and unthankfulness.

Against reverence are repugnant pride and fawning.

Against gravity, ***πολυπραγμοσύνη***, lightness, sloth.

Against modesty are repugnant pride, arrogance, vanity, boasting, emulation, ***πολυπραγμοσύνη***, ambition, lightness, shamelessness, rudeness, obscenity, scurrility in apparel, laughter[?],[^C3-11] and gait, in speech and gesture, etc.

After the same manner, to speak summarily, there are repugnant to the fourth commandment: in Parents and the Magistrate, tyranny and indulgence; in children and subjects, ***ἀστοργία***, contumacy, sedition, ungodly servitude; in every man's vocation, sloth and curiosity.

In the Antithesis is also to be reproved the ***κακοζηλία*** (perverse imitation) of the Papists, who, in imitation of the civil power, set up kingdoms in the Church, using this argument:

> Unto the Ecclesiastical power no less reverence is owed than unto the civil.  
> But the violation of the civil laws is a mortal sin.  
> Therefore the violation of traditions in the Church is also a mortal sin.  

I answer: I grant the major concerning reverence, as God himself hath ordained it. But he hath so ordained it, that in all things which are of divine right obedience be rendered unto the Ministers. But human ordinances, or traditions, must be without the opinion of worship and of necessity, as it is written: *"Let no man judge you in meat, in drink, or in the turns of feast days."* Next, the example of the civil laws is unlike. For God hath armed the civil power with authority to make laws of its own, not repugnant to the divine right. But the Gospel establisheth this liberty, that hu<!-- p.108 -->man traditions in the Church be understood without the opinion of worship and of necessity.

Let the solution of this argument also be held:

> No man can command under a penalty which he cannot execute.  
> The Magistrate cannot execute eternal penalties.  
> Therefore the edicts of the Magistrate do not bind unto eternal penalties.  

I answer to the major. The major is true, if it be taken of human authority alone. But the Magistrate is the instrument of God, who by this ordinance preserveth in mankind discipline and the bonds of civil society. God therefore executeth the penalty which he himself hath established; and the edicts of the magistrate bind unto eternal penalties, not on account of human authority, but on account of God their author.

### *De promissione et comminatione addita quarto praecepto* · Of the promise and threatening added to the fourth commandment

The peculiar promise is: *"That it may be well with thee, and that thou mayest live long"* — so far as is expedient for the good and salvation of thy soul. The threatening is in **Proverbs 30**: *"The eye that mocketh at his father, and refuseth to obey his mother, the ravens shall pick it out."* And it is commonly said: *"He obeyeth the hangman, who refuseth to obey his parent."* And **Romans 13**: *"He that resisteth the power, resisteth the ordinance of God, and bringeth judgement upon himself."* Likewise: *"He that shall take the sword shall perish by the sword."*

### *Quaestio de termino vitae cuiuslibet hominis* · A question concerning the term of every man's life

We answer that the measure of the term is to be sought, not from any Stoical and fatal necessity, but from the plain Word of God. Now the Word of God setteth before us the providence of God, grounded in the divine promises and threatenings. The promises are extant in **Exod. 20**: *"Honour thy parents, that thou mayest be long-<!-- p.109 -->lived,"* that is, that all things may fall out prosperously unto thee. **Proverbs 9**: *"By me thy days shall be multiplied, and the years of life shall be added unto thee."* **Proverbs 10**: *"The fear of the Lord shall add days, and the years of the ungodly shall be shortened."* Unto King Hezekiah life is prolonged, **Isa. 38**. Upon David's supplication the pestilence and the slaughter cease, **2 Sam. 24**. Unto the Ninevites, when they repent, life is granted, **Jonah 3**. The threatenings are extant everywhere, as **Ps. 54 [55]**: *"Men of blood shall not live out half their days."* **Levit. 20**: *"The wicked shall be cut off from the people."* **Proverbs 10**: *"He that curseth his parents shall be put out."* And most true is that which is wont to be said:

> Many perish by the sword, but surfeiting destroyeth more,  
> And plungeth them into destruction before their day.  

And David himself prayeth, **Ps. 102**: *"Take me not away in the midst of my days, neither cut off my days."* That the sick are relieved by the prayers of the godly **James** testifieth, **chap. 5**. And it is certain that the term of every man's life is either prolonged or shortened by God, according to the promises and threatenings.

Let us therefore flee the Stoical opinions concerning an unavoidable and fatal term of life: which if it were unavoidable, of necessity the fault also, for which many are haled to punishment, would be unavoidable, and so the cause and fountain of sin would be cast upon God. But this is both blasphemous and false, and hurteth manners, and hindereth the exercises of godliness.

Let us therefore look unto the promises and threatenings of God; let us fear him, and pray that he himself would rule us by his Spirit, and that he himself would be our life, and the length of our days, **Deut. 30**.

But what shall we answer to the place of **Job**, in the **fourteenth chapter**: *"The days of man are short; the number of his Months is with thee; thou hast appointed his bounds, which cannot be passed"*?

The true answer is, that the whole is granted according to the divine promises <!-- p.110 --> and threatenings which we have in the Word of God. But we ought not to judge otherwise than according to the Word, which revealeth unto us the will of God.

As touching the untimely decease of many godly persons, we often behold with exceeding great grief that there are suddenly taken out of this life the young plants of the Church, boys and youths of the best hope, and others who, by human and Physical reckoning, as we at least judge, might have continued longer, and have served and profited the Church and the Commonwealth by their wholesome labour. Now although we justly grieve — not for their sake, who are gently called forth out of this life, of whom it is written: *"Precious is the death of the saints"*; likewise: *"Blessed are they that die in the Lord"*; but for our own sake, who are day by day bereft of the best wits — yet let us know that the will of God is to be obeyed. Let us hold fast also the saying and consolation: ***δίκαιος ἐὰν φθάσῃ τελευτῆσαι, ἐν ἀναπαύσει ἔσται*** (the righteous man, though he be overtaken early by death, shall be in rest). We know that they of old said: *"Whom God loveth, the same dieth young."* But it is to be added that he who dieth, whensoever he dieth, is in rest, that is, falleth asleep, and resteth in the bosom of Abraham, and enjoyeth the sight and converse of God. Let us therefore hold the causes why oftentimes our children, and others, depart and are called away in the flower of their age.

The first cause is, that boys, youths, and the like may the sooner come unto rest and gladness and life eternal, and not be long detained in hope, but be speedily translated unto the thing itself and the possession of the eternal good things.

And hither may also most fitly be referred the sentence of Christ, **Matt.** the **tenth**: *"Suffer the little ones to come unto me, and forbid them not, for of such is the Kingdom of God."*

The second cause is, that they who are snatched away — or rather sent before us and called forth into the heavenly life — may be no more subject to the peril of seduction, as it is said: *"The righteous is taken away, lest wickedness should pervert him."* For it is easily wont to come to pass that a man, even of the <!-- p.111 --> godly and good, being seduced and deceived, falleth into errors or into heinous sins. God, foreseeing this, that it may not come to pass, calleth us forth beforehand, and preventeth the peril, lest both we ourselves be deceived, and lest we be able to deceive others.

The third cause is, that, as soon as may be according to the will of God, the godly may be delivered from all evil, and may remove out of this workhouse, and this grinding-mill of all calamities, unto the heavenly and eternal fatherland, and may escape the evils to come, as it is written in **Isaiah** the **fifty-sixth**: *"The righteous dieth, and no man considereth it: the righteous is gathered, before the evil arise; and he that hath lived uprightly cometh unto peace, and resteth in his bed."* Most gravely saith **Luther** in the exposition of **1 Corinth. 15**: *"This is true, that the more godly men are, the sooner they die. For if God is to preserve any godly man, that he may live long, there must needs be present a peculiar virtue and power, greater and stronger than is the power either of men or of Satan."*

Now although it is altogether true that it is best if we die early in the acknowledgement and confession of the Son of God, yet this also is to be known, that the prolonging of life is to be asked of God, if so be that the Church of God and the Commonwealth have need of our labour. And there are three chief causes for which a longer life is to be asked of God.

The first, that we ourselves may learn the true doctrine, and get unto ourselves the knowledge of the things necessary unto salvation, and profitable unto human life.

The second is, that we may be able to turn the doctrine which we have learned to use in our whole life, and in all our actions, to the praise and glory of God, and to the profit and amendment of our neighbour.

The third is, that we may provide for posterity also, and endeavour to propagate the true doctrine unto them that come after by our voice, <!-- p.112 --> labours, and writings. So King Hezekiah asketh of God the prolonging of his life, that he may the longer serve and govern his own, **Isa. 38**. So it is reported of **blessed Martin**, that he prayed: *"Lord, if I am necessary unto thy people, I refuse not the labour: thy will be done."*[^C3-12] And **Paul** saith unto the **Philipp. 2**: *"What I shall choose, I know not: I am straitened of two, having a desire to be dissolved and to be with Christ, which is far better by much; but to abide in the flesh is more necessary for your sakes, for your furtherance and joy of faith."*

But in these and the like things it must be committed unto the will of God, whatsoever indeed come to pass; and it is to be known that we are the Lord's, whether we live or die, **Rom. 14**. And **Deuteron. 30**: *"He is thy life, and the length of thy days."* Likewise **Job 12**: *"In the hand of the Lord is the soul of every living thing, and the Spirit of all flesh of man."* And **Ps. 31**: *"In thy hands is my time."*

Moreover, although we know that shortness of life is a punishment wherewith certain offences are punished — as unto David the son born of Bathsheba dieth, **2 Sam. 12** — yet let us know this also, that often the godly, not defiled with manifest sins that are against conscience, are snatched away into life, and taken out of all peril of evils, as we daily see examples. Let us know also that sins are forgiven unto them that turn themselves unto God, and that punishments are mitigated, and that nothing standeth in the way of the salvation of them that believe in the Son of God and flee sins.

## *De V. Praecepto* · Of the Fifth Commandment

Society having been established in the fourth commandment, now in most fitting order the care of protecting the life of men is enjoined; and therefore there is straightway added: *"Thou shalt not kill."* And we shall consider these things:

1. What are the virtues of this commandment.
2. <!-- p.113 -->What are the sins against this commandment.
3. Of public and private revenge.
4. Of war.
5. Of zeal.
6. Of the promises and threatenings.

### *De operibus quinti praecepti* · Of the works of the fifth commandment

There pertain unto this commandment many virtues, whereof the chief are these.

*Iustitia particularis*, particular justice, is a virtue keeping equality with other men, and rendering to every man his own, and hurting no man's body, good name, or goods, by force or by fraud.

*Iustitia distributiva*, distributive justice, is the virtue whereby the lawful Magistrate punisheth them that are defiled with outward crimes repugnant to the precepts of the Decalogue, according to the quality of the offence.

*Fortitudo bellica*, fortitude in war, is a virtue that is the champion of justice, undertaking perils and labours for the defence of the Church, of the fatherland, of honest discipline, of wives, of children, and keeping the mean between fearfulness and rashness.

*Fortitudo togata*, fortitude of the gown, is the constancy of a mind persevering in an honest purpose rightly undertaken according to vocation, and enduring injuries for an honest cause, and with a lofty mind despising pleasures, honours, bribes, riches, detraction, and envy, for virtue's sake — so, namely, that on account of these hindrances it cast not away its honest purpose.

*Patientia philosophica*, philosophical patience, is a virtue obeying right reason in the bearing of injuries and adversities, and moderating grief, that it oppress not nature, and that it do nothing against the judgement of right reason and against the virtues.

*Mansuetudo*, meekness, is the virtue which mitigateth severe justice, either by punishing more mildly, or by bearing with infirmities in others <!-- p.114 --> which the laws do not punish; and it turneth things doubtfully done to the better part, and covereth secret offences, and healeth certain errors before contumacy.

*Aequitas*, equity, or ***ἐπιείκεια***, is the virtue which for probable causes mitigateth the strictness of the law (*summum ius*) in some circumstance, especially in that case whereof the law doth not principally speak.

AMONG PRIVATE MEN, however, that lenity also is called equity which is occupied in bearing with the infirmities of others, and in covering their errors, yea, also in healing manifest offences before contumacy.

*Misericordia*, mercy, is the virtue which is touched with grief at another's calamity, especially that of good and innocent men, or of men led astray, and with probable reason lighteneth punishments, and bringeth help to the miserable: either for justice' sake, as when cruelty is used against the innocent; or for goodness' sake, relaxing somewhat of the strictness of the law, as God hath mercy upon mankind; or because we also, in common society, have need of the mercy of others.

The study of concord, or ***φιλησυχία*** (love of quietness), is the virtue which thinketh humbly of itself, bridleth and moderateth anger, beareth with the infirmities of others, pardoneth even true injuries, and maintaineth concord, and taketh heed that it contend not about any matter not greatly necessary, lest the public tranquillity of the Church and the Commonwealth be troubled.

*Amicitia*, friendship, is a virtue rendering true goodwill for true goodwill, and performing certain mutual offices according to the measure of its ability, with a liberal heart, for virtue's sake.

*Fidelitas*, faithfulness, is the virtue which, in true mutual goodwill and in all the offices owed to friendship, keepeth constancy in prosperity and in adversity.

*Candor*, candour, is the virtue which, without dissembling and false colours, openly showeth the meaning of the mind, and approveth the intentions of others and the things rightly <!-- p.115 --> said and done, and neither suspecteth nor speaketh amiss of the intentions of others without probable reason, and wisheth well to the good, and rejoiceth in their prosperity.

*Humanitas*, humanity, is the coupling together of many virtues of the second Table, namely, of justice, courtesy, meekness, equity, beneficence, affability, and the like, in which there is the greatest sweetness and moderation.

*Clementia*, clemency, is a virtue doing good to the good, and discerning them from the evil, and moderating the punishments of those who are curable.

*Zelus*, zeal, or *Nemesis*, is a burning love of the glory of God, of justice, of godliness, of chastity, and of the other virtues, and a burning indignation against all filthiness and heinous deeds whereby the glory of GOD is defaced, and a burning desire of driving away and punishing the wicked.

### *De peccatis contra quintum praeceptum. Antithesis* · Of the sins against the fifth commandment. The Antithesis

Against particular justice are repugnant injury, wantonness, naughtiness. Against distributive justice are repugnant cruelty, indulgence, inequality, or ***προσωποληψία*** (respect of persons). Against fortitude in war are repugnant fearfulness, desertion, shameful flight, rashness. Against the fortitude of the gown are repugnant softness of mind, lightness and inconstancy, unjust stubbornness, and Stoical frowardness. Against patience are repugnant loathing of things present, impatience, wrathfulness. Against meekness, equity, and mercy are repugnant cruelty, wrathfulness, the desire of revenge, envy or spite, lightness, indulgence, slackness. Against the study of concord are repugnant slackness, and ***φιλονεικία***, or contentiousness, pride, ambition, wrathfulness, insolence, obstinacy. Against friendship are repugnant enmity, hatred, dissembling, the painted goodwill of courtiers and of others. Against <!-- p.116 --> faithfulness is repugnant perfidy, today most usual. Against candour are repugnant suspicion, distrust, foolish credulity. Against humanity are repugnant sternness, insolence, frowardness, wrathfulness, spite, pride, the feigning of goodwill. Against clemency are repugnant sternness, cruelty, inequality, the strictness of the law, indulgence, the feigning of kindness. Against Zeal are repugnant unjust harshness, and a slackness that contendeth not even about matters greatly necessary.

In **Matthew** the **fifth** Christ distributeth all these sins against the fifth commandment into four degrees. The first degree is violence, or outward murder, of whose punishment it is written, **Deut. 19**: *"Thou shalt put away the guilty blood from the midst of thee; thou shalt not pity."* The second degree is unjust and inordinate anger, or the desire of private revenge, sternness, and hatred, whereof it is written, **1 John 3**: *"Every one that hateth his brother is a murderer."* The third degree is the gesture that showeth ill-will. For the word *Racha* signifieth every imprecation, and all the signs of ill-will, of unjust anger, and of private revenge. The fourth degree is the uttermost derision and contempt of our neighbour, detraction, reproach, revilings, mockeries, sarcasms, disdain.

### *De publica et privata vindicta* · Of public and private revenge

In this question these things are to be considered:

1. Whether all revenge be forbidden.
2. What are the causes of public punishments.
3. Of the ***ἐπιεικείᾳ*** (equity) to be used in punishments.
4. Of intercessions, how far they ought to have place.
5. Of shunning ***προσωποληψίᾳ*** (respect of persons).
6. How the magistrate ought rightly to carry himself, that he exceed not the measure.
