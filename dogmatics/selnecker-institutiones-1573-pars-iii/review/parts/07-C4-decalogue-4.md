<!-- p.117 -->

### *An vindicta sit prohibita* · Whether Vengeance Be Forbidden

All private vengeance is forbidden by God. Now that is called private vengeance (*vindicta privata*), or revenge (*ultio*), which ariseth out of a private and evil affection, and tendeth to the hurt of him upon whom vengeance is sought. This is severely forbidden by God, as **Levit. 19**: *"Thou shalt not avenge, nor bear any grudge against the children of thy people."* **Deut. 32**: *"Vengeance is mine, and I will repay in due season."* **Ecclus. 28**: *"He that revengeth shall find vengeance from the Lord."* And **Prov. 24**: *"Say not, I will do so to him as he hath done to me: I will render to every man according to his work."* **Matth. 5**: *"Resist not evil. Do good to them that hate you."* Likewise **Rom. 12**: *"Recompense to no man evil for evil."*

These sayings are to be weighed more diligently than that either private revenge should be excused, or public vengeance accused. For public vengeance (*vindicta publica*) is not forbidden but commanded by God, which is exercised by a public person, following the prescript of the laws, without evil affection and private hatred. For Paul saith plainly, **Rom. 13**: *"The magistrate beareth not the sword in vain."*

### *De causis publicarum poenarum, sive suppliciorum iustorum* · On the Causes of Public Punishments, or of Just Penalties

There are four chief causes of public punishments.

The first is the justice of God; because God will have it understood in mankind, and from these examples, that there is a God who distinguisheth things just and unjust, and is truly angry with injustice.

The second is the common necessity. Since God is in very deed the guardian of human society, God in this manner taketh away those who disturb lawful society; yea, he will also have it understood that he is the guardian of the political order, because the rule is universal: *"All they that take the sword shall perish with the sword."* And God draweth the guilty back to punishment, even when they have escaped out of the hands of the magistrates.

<!-- p.118 -->

The third is example, that when the multitude seeth the punishments of the guilty, very many may be restrained by fear of like punishments; as it is said, *"The law is laid down for the unjust,"* namely that, being repressed by law and fear, they may rule their outward members, lest they offend.

The fourth cause is the admonition concerning the judgement to come. For from these punishments of a few God will have us reason that, since he is truly angry with sins, there remaineth another judgement, in which God will horribly punish the wickedness of all who have not turned unto him, nor sought remission of sins through the Mediator, as he commandeth. And since sin is an infinite evil, and a proportion between the crime and a temporal punishment cannot be established in this life, it is certain that these brief punishments are only admonitions of the eternal punishments, if there be no conversion to Christ the Mediator.

### *De ἐπιεικείᾳ adhibenda in poenis* · On the Equity to Be Used in Punishments

It is rightly said: *"The utmost right is the utmost wrong."* And there is the celebrated saying of **Menander**:

> ***καλὸν οἱ νόμοι σφόδρ᾽ εἰσίν· ὁ δ᾽ ὁρῶν τοὺς νόμους***  
> ***λίαν ἀκριβῶς, συκοφάντης γίνεται.***  
> *The laws are a thing exceeding excellent: but he that useth the laws*  
> *too exactly seemeth to be a false accuser.*  

There is need therefore of ***ἐπιείκεια*** (equity), neither is ***ἀκριβοδίκαιον***, strict right (*strictum ius*), as **Aristotle** nameth it, to be exercised, which for the most part degenerateth either into cruelty or into chicanery. And unto this equity, which remitteth somewhat of the utmost right, all governors and judges ought to be moved, both by the commandments of God concerning the forgiving of injuries, *"Forgive, and ye shall be forgiven,"* **Luke 6**, and by the example of God, who daily forgiveth us infinite sins, as David saith: *"If thou shalt impute sins unto us, <!-- p.119 -->O Lord, who shall stand?"* And it was not ill said by a heathen man:

> *If, as often as men sin, Jupiter should send his thunderbolts,*  
> *in a little while there will be no man left.*[^C4-1]  

And it is certain that the more excellent natures are, and the more like unto God, the more placable also they are, as it is wont to be said:

> *The greater any man is, the more may his wrath be appeased,*  
> *and a noble mind receiveth easy motions.*  

Contrariwise, the baser and worse natures are more retentive of offences and more desirous of vengeance, as **Juvenal** saith of women:

> *for indeed*  
> *revenge is ever the pleasure of a petty, weak and little mind.*  
> *Gather it straightway from this, that in vengeance*  
> *none rejoiceth more than a woman.*  

**Seneca** said rightly: *"Nothing is great which is not calm."* And **Antoninus** in **Vulcacius** saith: *"There is nothing which better commendeth a Roman Emperor than clemency."*[^C4-2] And **Alfonso**, King of Naples, said that he forgave others many things, because he knew that very many were forgiven him by God. Hence the Germans say, *Gnad gehet für recht*: grace is better than the utmost justice. For thus doth God deal with us. Then, lastly, our own infirmities ought to stir us up unto equity, who daily stand in need of forgiveness, patience and compassion, both divine and human, as **Nazianzen** hath truly and excellently said:

> ***εἴ δ᾽ οἶδας ὄφλων καὶ πρόχρησον τὸ πρᾶον,***  
> ***οἴκτῳ γὰρ οἶκτος καὶ θεῷ σταθμίζεται.***  
> *If thou knowest that thou art a debtor to others, show thyself also gentle:*  
> *for mercy is weighed out by God with mercy.*  

But let the rule be held, lest ***ἐπιείκεια*** degenerate into sloth, too much connivance, and indulgence. The rule <!-- p.120 -->is this: When the intent of the law admitteth ***ἐπιείκειαν***, and the accused can be amended without a harder punishment, and the people is not made worse by the example, the magistrate may use a milder sentence, and spare the suppliant offender in hope of amendment.

Wherefore the judge, according to this rule, ought to have regard unto three things: unto the law, unto the guilty, and unto the people. The authority of the law is to be maintained, without cruelty and without sloth. The former life of the guilty, his disposition and nature, is to be considered. Account is to be taken of the people, lest the example of lenity beget a patronage for sin, lest discipline be loosened, and lest the guilty be spared with damage and peril to the commonwealth.

### *An intercessiones non debeant locum habere* · Whether Intercessions Ought Not to Have Place

Intercessions are by no means forbidden, for accused persons not contumacious, not perverse and stubborn, but whose nature and life is honest. Yet the rule now laid down is not to be set aside. And intercessions are so far to be admitted as they agree with the rule, lest examples of heinous deeds be confirmed. Moreover the entreaties and intercessions which are made for the accused out of compassion are images of the true intercession whereby the Mediator, the Son of God, reconcileth us to his Father.

Here the rules of the jurisconsults are to be observed: *"Where the circumstances are diverse, a dispensation is to be sought."* Likewise: *"It hath been determined that in all things the regard of justice and of written equity should be chief, rather than that of strict right."*[^C4-3]

When therefore there is at hand a clear and probable reason, pardon is to be given to the accused, and intercessions are to be admitted, and ***ἐπιείκεια*** is to be used.

Hence **Aristotle** saith that the laws, like a Lesbian <!-- p.121 -->or leaden rule, are to be bent somewhat to the cases. These words, if they be understood of ***ἐπιεικείᾳ***, are not to be blamed. Now it is called a Lesbian rule when the reason is fitted to the deed, and the rule to manners and examples, in regard of the circumstances, namely in a case worthy of ***ἐπιεικείᾳ***. For otherwise the force of the rule is to be held: *Apply the stone to the line, not the line to the stone*; that is, life is to be corrected according to the laws, and the laws are not to be drawn to our manners. ***πρὸς στάθμῃ πέτρον τίθεσθαι, μήτι πρὸς πέτρῳ στάθμην.*** For so **Plutarch** reporteth it.

Examples of this kind may be set down. The law concerning the hanging of a thief speaketh principally of him who hath committed theft with a heinous purpose. Now if a modest man, driven by hunger, steal, he deserveth pardon, a mitigation of the punishment, and is worthy to be interceded for.

**Gellius**, book 12, chap. 7, relateth the history of a woman of Smyrna, who had slain with poison her husband and his son, who nevertheless had first killed the woman's son, born in a former marriage. The Areopagite judges therefore (***εἰς ἄρειον παγὸν ἀναβαίνοντες***, they that go up to the hill of Ares), who at Athens gave sentence in capital causes with so great gravity that they heard causes by night, not by day, that they might regard not the speakers but only the things that were spoken (whence the proverbs, *graver, sterner, or more silent than an Areopagite*, ***ἀρεοπαγίτου στεγανώτερος***), taking cognizance of the cause, gave sentence that the cause should be heard again in the hundredth year after. Thus they neither approved the heinous deed, and yet they remitted the punishment because of the mother's just grief. Like unto this is that which the Greeks relate of Mars, who slew Halirrhothius,[^C4-4] the son of Neptune, for the violation of Alcippe, the daughter of Mars. Neptune accuseth Mars in the Areopagus. Mars is acquitted by the judgement of the gods. For on account of probable causes of a homicide, punishments are sometimes to be remitted or mitigated.

<!-- p.122 -->

It came to pass somewhere that a brother, going abroad, committed his wife to his brother to be kept. This man, having grown too familiar with his brother's wife, hath incestuous commerce with her. The brother, returning and finding out the matter, slayeth his brother. There was dispute concerning the punishment of the homicide. The magistrate at length judged that the author of the slaying was to be visited with no punishment at all. In the same manner Orestes was acquitted, for having slain his mother Clytemnestra in revenge of Orestes' father, namely Agamemnon, whom she had before slain. Whence **Ovid**:

> *And Orestes, avenger upon his mother, an evil avenger of his father.*  

The Greeks feign that the gods exercised these two capital judgements in the Areopagus, as **Demosthenes** reciteth them in the oration against Aristocrates. For although by the utmost right the authors of the slaying might have been more hardly punished, yet a just grief was to be pardoned. These and the like examples are to be considered, that we may learn to understand and to love ***ἐπιείκειαν***, of which it is said, **Prov. 30**: *"He that wringeth the nose hard bringeth forth blood."* And: *"Be not righteous overmuch."* And the Germans have a witty proverb, *zu scharff macht scharten* (too sharp maketh notches). Whence **Sophocles** saith: *Great trees, because they yield not, are overthrown by the force of overflowing waters; but lowly and pliant shrubs, which yield, are not overthrown.* And the Greeks say that lyres are to be imitated, in which the strings are broken by too much stretching, and give forth dull sounds by too much slackening, as it is wont to be said:

> *The fond indulgence of fathers maketh slothful sons.*  

Let these things suffice concerning ***ἐπιεικείᾳ*** and intercession, which God assuredly commandeth and requireth, other things being equal.

### *An προσωποληψία non sit admittenda* · Whether Respect of Persons Be Not to Be Admitted

***Προσωποληψία*** (respect of persons) is earnestly forbidden by God, because he himself is not ***προσωπολήπτης*** (a respecter of persons), as it is written, **Deut. 10**: *"God, mighty and <!-- p.123 -->terrible, regardeth not persons."* Likewise in **Rom. 2**, **Galat. 2**, **Ephes. 6** and **Acts 10** and elsewhere it is repeated: *"God is no respecter of persons."* Therefore Jehoshaphat, King of Judah, appointing judges, saith, **2 Chron. 19**: *"Take heed what ye do: for ye judge not for man, but for the Lord, and he is with you in your counsels. Wherefore let the fear of the Lord be upon you; be ye wary, and obey.*

*For there is no iniquity with the Lord our God, nor respect of persons, nor approving of gifts."* This speech of the best of kings describeth most briefly the duties of the magistrate, which are: to fear God; to be diligent in exactly examining the cause and in hearing both parties; to be skilled in the law; to be studious of justice according to the rule of the laws; to be equal toward all; to shun ***δωροφαγίαν*** (the devouring of gifts).

Now God requireth of every magistrate equality, which shunneth ***προσωποληψίαν***, that is, the acceptance of persons, which giveth unequal things to equals and equal things to unequals; that is, when for the same cause some are punished and others are not so much as called to account. An example is that of **Horace**:[^C4-5]

> *The censure giveth pardon to the ravens, and vexeth the doves.*  

And **Cato the Elder** is reported to have said that the thieves of private goods pass their life in fetters, but the thieves of public goods walk in gold and purple. **Diogenes Laertius**, in book 6 of the *Lives of the Philosophers*, relateth: When Diogenes on a time had seen the magistrates who were called ***ἱερομνήμονες*** (that is, deacons, overseers of sacred things, whom the Germans name *Kirchvätter* and *Kastenherrn*) leading away a certain man who had stolen a bowl out of the treasury, *"The great thieves,"* said he, *"are leading the little one."* In the same manner **Anacharsis** compared the laws to spiders' webs: for as in these the weaker things are held and the stronger pass through, so by those, he said, the lowly are bound fast and the over-mighty are not tied. Hence arose the Aristotelian proverb: *Love, and hatred, and a man's own profit <!-- p.124 -->do often cause the judge not to know the truth.* And **Cicero** saith: *"Men judge more things by love, or desire, or anger, or grief, or hope, or gladness, or fear, or terror, than by truth, or by prescript, or by any rule of law, or by form of judgement, or by the laws."*[^C4-6]

**Agesilaus** is accused of ***προσωποληψίας***, because he often helped his friends in unjust causes, and on a time commended his friend Nicias to the judge in these words: *"Let Nicias go, if he have not an unjust cause, and acquit him for justice' sake; but if he have an unjust cause, let him go for my sake; but in any wise let him go."*

Darius, to gratify his princes, commandeth the innocent Daniel to be cast into the den of lions. Herod, to gratify the Jews, when James had been slain, apprehended Peter also, **Acts 12**.

Examples meet us everywhere in all histories, and at this day, both in the Church itself among the doctors and others, and in courts, cities and commonwealths, and in common life. And that is most true which is wont to be said:

> *Love or hatred standeth in the way of better judgements.*  

Hence many things are spoken of the favourable judge, and at courts to have had propitious Jupiters is the best thing, whatsoever manner of cause a man may have.

This ***προσωποληψίαν*** the Holy Ghost censureth everywhere in the Scriptures, **Micah 7**, **Isaiah 5** and **10**, **Prov. 17** and elsewhere, and threateneth horrible punishments to the unjust magistrate. Well known also are the verses of **Phocylides**:

> ***ἀδίκως μὴ κρίνε πρόσωπον·***  
> ***ἢν σὺ κακῶς δικάσῃς, σὲ θεὸς μετέπειτα δικάσει.***  
> (*Judge not a person unjustly:*  
> *if thou judge wickedly, God shall afterward judge thee.*)  

**Pericles**, being asked to uphold the unjust cause of a friend, answered: ***δεῖ με συμπράττειν τοῖς φίλοις, ἀλλὰ μέχρι θεῶν*** (I must work together with my friends, but only as far as the gods). *Friendship as far as the altars.* And in this manner is to be taken the <!-- p.125 -->saying, *All things of friends are common*, namely those which by right and by the laws can be common; as **Agis**, the last of the Laconian kings, when his parents pressed him to stand by them in an unjust matter, said: *"I refuse what ye ask, because I am a lover of justice and honesty, which ye yourselves taught me."* And **Phocion** said to Antipater, who asked to be helped by him in an evil matter: *"Thou canst not, O Antipater, have Phocion at once for a friend and for a flatterer."* Likewise King **Philip** answered Harpalus, who asked that the king would acquit Crates, lest Crates and his family should bring ignominy upon themselves: *"It is better,"* said he, *"that Crates be ill spoken of, than that we should be so on his account, who ought to be administrators of justice, not overthrowers of it."*

Let the sayings of the wise be observed by the younger sort: ***τὸ ἶσον ἀντιπεπονθὸς σώζει τὰς πόλεις***: *mutual equality preserveth cities*, saith **Aristotle**. And **Euripides**: ***τὸ ἶσον νόμιμον ἀνθρώποις πέλει*** (equality is that which is lawful among men). **Phocylides**: ***μοῖραν πᾶσι νέμειν, ἰσότης ἐν πάσιν ἀρίστη*** (give every man his portion; equality is in all things best). For God willeth that by the study of equality it be acknowledged that he himself is equal toward all, both in punishments and in mercies for the Son's sake.

But that at this day the study of equality is very slender, and that, as a certain man said in jest, *Ius premit ius, et ut curvum rectum, ita ius ius est*,[^C4-7] this is to be ascribed to the perversity of men, to our sins and to security, until the author, preserver, reformer and restorer of justice, the Son of God, come, and render equal things to equals.

### *Quomodo magistratus in his se recte gerere debeat* · How the Magistrate Ought Rightly to Conduct Himself in These Things

1. Since the fear of the Lord is the beginning of true wisdom, he must live in the fear of God, and the divine help must be implored, that God himself would furnish minds and hearts with true counsels, and <!-- p.126 -->direct them to the glory of his name and to the rule of justice. **Psalm 127**: *"Except the Lord build the house, they labour in vain that build it."* **Prov. 8**: *"By me kings reign, and senators administer justice."* And **chap. 20**: *"The hearing ear, and the seeing eye, the Lord hath made even both of them."* Thus Solomon asketh of God wisdom and counsel for rightly governing the people, **3 Kings 3** [1 Kings 3]. In this way Daniel (*the judge of God*) straightway perceiveth the innocency of Susanna and the wickedness of the judges; and Solomon decideth the cause of the two women.

2. The magistrate ought not to be too headlong and swift to pronounce sentence. **Sirach 5** and **10**: *"Understand first, and then rebuke."* **Genes. 38**: Judah hastily condemneth his daughter-in-law Tamar, who nevertheless was more righteous than he. And Potiphar, the master of Joseph, is too headlong, casting Joseph, upon the accusation of his wife, into the prison, in which he was more than ten years. Hither it pertaineth that the cause be exactly examined, and both parties heard, and that credit be not forthwith given to informations and slanders, to vain rumours and suspicions, as is commonly wont to be done in courts, with great peril and hurt of the innocent. Worthy of observation are the sayings of the wise men of old, as that of **Epicharmus**: *"The sinews and limbs of wisdom are, not to believe rashly."* And **Euripides**: ***σώφρονος ἀπιστίας οὐκ ἐστὶν οὐδὲν χρησιμώτερον βροτοῖς*** (nothing is more profitable to mortals than a prudent distrust). And hither is to be referred the motto of the Emperor **Maximilian**: *"Believe little, keep thine own, and leave what is lost."* **Philostratus**, in book 6 of the *Life of Apollonius*, saith: *"They that give ear to slanderers are found to be lovers of evil-speaking, followers of lightness and cruelty, and shall be held to be envious, bringing a just hearing into ill-will."* In the same manner Solomon also saith, **Ecclus. 19**: *"He that is hasty to give credit is lightminded."* And **Prov. 18**: *"He that answereth a matter before he heareth it is a fool."* And it is a common proverb: *Pronounce not concerning a matter, except it be diligently examined on this side and on that.* <!-- p.127 -->And with these agree the sentences of others, which are everywhere extant, as the Attic law, whereby they swore that they would hear both parties alike, ***ὁμοίως ἀμφοῖν ἀκροᾶσθαι***; which sentence **Phocylides** hath expressed in this verse: ***μήτε δίκην δικάσεις, πρὶν ἀμφοῖν μῦθον ἀκούσῃς***: *Give not judgement, if both have not spoken.* And **Plutarch** relateth that Alexander was wont to stop one ear with his hand in hearing the accuser, and to say that he would keep the other whole for the accused. And the Greeks say: ***ἀνεξέταστον μὴ κόλαζε μηδένα***: *Punish no man who hath not first been convicted.* To this **Seneca** alluded in the *Medea*:

> *He that hath determined aught, the other party being unheard,*  
> *though he have determined what is just, was himself not just.*  

From these things therefore it appeareth that the magistrate, the prince and the judge ought to proceed slowly, until they have diligently inquired into the matter, as it is wont to be said in the verses:

> *Let the prince be slow to punishments, swift to rewards,*  
> *and one that grieveth as often as he is compelled to be severe.*[^C4-8]  

3. The punishment is to be executed in due season, as it is written, **Sirach 22**: *"Discipline and instruction is to be exercised at the right time."* Discipline is not to be so loosened that, when it hath been loosened, the magistrate should then first be minded to punish certain transgressors; but evils are to be met in time, as it is wont to be said: *Withstand the beginnings; the medicine is prepared too late.* Yet godly magistrates ought to correct a discipline that hath been loosened. Sometimes also the punishment is to be deferred, when the peril of some greater harm hangeth over; as David deferreth the punishment of the captain Joab, whom he could not put to death without peril of sedition. For it is rightly said: *He that cannot dissemble cannot rule.* But in these things there is need of the judgement, counsel and help of God.

4. Let not a punishment be imposed greater than the offence. There is need therefore of the knowledge of the laws and of right, as **Plato** elegantly saith: ***ἐπιστήμῃ δεῖ κρίνειν, οὐ πλήθει***, *one must judge by knowledge, not by <!-- p.128 -->multitude.* And **Cicero** saith: *"It is the part of a wise judge to consider, not what he himself would, but what the law and his oath constrain."* The same: *"Heed must be taken that the punishment be not greater than the fault."*

Hither may be referred more sayings, which are everywhere extant: ***μηδέποτε κρίνειν ἀδαήμονας ἄνδρας ἐάσῃς*** (never suffer unskilled men to judge). *An unlearned prince is more truly a statue than a prince.* And **Tacitus**: *"Who ruleth better than the lettered man?"* **Plato**: *"Wisdom is no otherwise necessary to a prince than the soul to the body."* The same: *"Commonwealths would be blessed, if the studious of wisdom governed them."* And Solomon saith, **Prov. 28**: *"A prince without understanding doeth many things unjustly."* Likewise **Cicero**, in the third book *On the Laws*: *"As the laws are set over the magistrates, so are the magistrates over the people; and it may truly be said that the magistrate is a speaking law, and the law a silent magistrate."* Wherefore judgement is to be given according to the laws, lest that come to pass which is said:

> *Where there is no shame,*  
> *nor care of right,*  
> *holiness, piety, faith,*  
> *the kingdom is unstable.*  

For these are not private goods only, neither may kings go whither it pleaseth them, nor is that lawful for them which they list, unless they would keep for themselves the name of tyrants, as is to be said elsewhere.[^C4-9]

5. Heed must be taken that the punishment be not determined out of private affection, hatred, envy and anger. **Sirach 10**: *"Refresh not thy mind when thou punishest."* And **Cicero**: *"Let them that are set over the commonwealth be like unto the laws, which are led to punish not by anger but by equity."* And here the rule of **Christian**, King of Denmark, is to be held: *"Punish those who are to be punished by reason of thine office; but if thou punish those who have in any way hurt thine own person, although thou be a public person, yet thou seemest to exercise private vengeance, unless thou forgive the offence."* Hence also is celebrated the saying of a certain King of Poland, who, being incited to put to death one who had said that the King was ill spoken of among all men, merrily <!-- p.129 -->answered: *"I had rather that one man were ill spoken of among all, than all with one."* And **Alexander**, when he had heard a certain man who assailed him with reproaches, answered: ***βασιλικὸν ἐστι τὸν εὖ ποιοῦντα κακῶς ἀκούειν*** (it is a kingly thing, when a man doeth well, to be ill spoken of). And it is known of **Pyrrhus** that he was quieted and restrained his anger, when he had heard the answer made him by a soldier: *"Assuredly we should have gone on to use our revilings, if the flagon had not failed us."*

6. Above all it is to be considered that an account must one day be rendered to the supreme Judge of the office that hath been borne, as at this day even boys are wont to say, and that truly:

> *The general Judge shall judge the judges,*  
> *neither shall papal dignity avail anything;*  
> *whether he be bishop or cardinal,*  
> *the guilty shall be condemned, nor shall it be said of what quality he is.*  
> *There it shall profit nothing to allege anything,*  
> *nor to take exception, nor to explain,*  
> *nor to appeal to the Apostolic See,*  
> *nor to cite the codicils of Caesar;*  
> *the guilty shall be condemned, and it shall be said wherefore.*  
> *Consider, ye wretched, what or of what sort ye are,*  
> *what ye are able to say in this judgement;*  
> *here shall be no place for the Codex, nor for the Digests;*  
> *the same shall be Lord, judge, plaintiff, witness.*  

### *De Bello* · On War

Although it is certain that wars are punishments wherewith God is wont to punish the sins of men, and that they bring with them a great troop of public and private evils, and have issues altogether uncertain, as sentences confirmed by long experience do testify, such as are these: *Amid arms the laws are silent*; *There is no safety in war; peace we all ask of thee*; *Better is a certain peace than a hoped-for victory*, as **Livy** saith. And that of **Sallust**: *"Every <!-- p.130 -->war is easily taken up, but is with the greatest difficulty ended. The beginning and the end are not in the power of the same man."* Likewise: ***ξυνὸς ἐνυάλιος καὶ τὸν κτανέοντα κατέκτα***, **Homer**, *Iliad* 6:[^C4-10] *Mars is common, and easily slayeth the slayer.* ***νίκη ἐπαμείβεται ἄνδρας***: *Now to these, now to those it falleth to conquer.* *Nowhere do the issues answer less than in war.* And that of **Thucydides**: ***ἥκιστα πόλεμος ἐπὶ ῥητοῖς χωρεῖ*** (war least of all goeth forward upon set terms). And: ***πόλεμον οὐχ ὑπάρχει εἰδέναι καθότι χωρήσει*** (it is not given to know in what way a war will go). Of the most part of soldiers also, how much hurt they do, and what they look to, that saying is known:

> *No faith nor piety is in the men who follow camps:*  
> *their hands are for sale; there is right, where the pay is greatest, etc.*  

Although therefore, I say, wars seldom bring with them any good, yet it is to be known that it is lawful for Christians to wage war in that manner which is shown in the definition of lawful war. For war is either a lawful defence, whereby he that wageth the war protecteth life, body and good name, and goods, and repelleth force offered, upon the instant, without desire of hurting another, and keeping the moderation of a blameless defence (*moderamen inculpatae tutelae*). Or it is a lawful punishment undertaken by the ordinary power, for a just cause, against wicked men, disturbers of the peace, the seditious and the contumacious.

From this definition[^C4-11] it is evident, first, to whom it is granted to undertake war, namely to the lawful magistrate, to whom belongeth the levying of forces in a just cause. For as public vengeance, which is of the office of the magistrate, is the ordinance of God, so war also, in that manner which is expressed in the definition, is a part of the public vengeance granted and ordained by God. And this may be shown, as it were by a certain gathering together and drawing out of the members of the definition, in a syllogism, after this manner:

All defence against unjust and outrageous force is lawful to all men by divine and natural right.

For it is a law born with us, snatched, drawn and pressed out from nature, as **Cicero** speaketh.

<!-- p.131 -->

Lawful war is such a defence, undertaken by the magistrate divinely ordained, as when Constantine defended the godly against the cruelty of Licinius.

Therefore lawful war is granted to Christians.

Likewise:

The execution of lawful punishment is a divine ordinance committed to the magistrate. **Deut. 19**: *"Put the evil away from among you."* **Rom. 13**: *"He beareth not the sword in vain, for vengeance unto thee."*

Certain wars are lawful punishments, as when the superior magistrate punisheth those subject to him for impious contumacy and rebellion.

Therefore certain wars are divinely commanded, and are granted.

**Likewise, from the final cause.**

The defence and protection of commonwealths is commanded to all for four causes. 1. That peace, *the best of the things which it hath been given to man to know*, ***καὶ ἡ κουρότροφος*** (and the nurse of the young), may be preserved. 2. That in peace the true doctrine concerning God, and the other arts and tongues wholesome and profitable to mankind, may be propagated. 3. That our country may be defended, and honest laws, discipline, and the study of piety and honesty may be preserved. 4. That the wicked may be punished and taken out of the midst, as it is rightly said: *The incurable wound must be cut away with the sword, lest the sound part be drawn in.* For evil examples do hurt, and infect others, even the good, as the Germans say of the scabbed sheep that harmeth and infecteth the whole flock.

Now lawful wars are referred to these ends.

Therefore they are granted.

Next it is evident when it is lawful to wage wars, namely, not when private affections, anger, desire of vengeance, avarice, and the longing to seize other men's goods boil up, but when <!-- p.132 -->extreme necessity presseth and constraineth the lawful magistrate to defence, or to the execution of lawful punishment. For it was rightly and wisely said by the ancients: *"It becometh a wise man to try all things before arms."*

From these things it is manifest that, as other political ordinances are granted to Christians, so also it is lawful to bear lawful arms, as the divine testimonies and examples do witness. For it is written: *"The Lord teacheth my hands to war, and my fingers to fight,"* **Psalm 143** [144]. And of Abraham we shall hear a little after.[^C4-12] And the examples are known of Joshua, of Samson, of the Centurion, **Matth. 8**, of Cornelius, **Acts 10**, and of others. And Christ, **Luke 3**, teacheth soldiers what they ought to do and to leave undone. And **Augustine**, writing to Boniface, saith: *"Think not that no man can please God who serveth in arms of war. Among these was holy David, to whom the Lord bore the greatest testimony; among these was that holy Centurion."*[^C4-13]

Rightly therefore is a soldier defined to be a person ordained by the will of God to this work, that in just defence and lawful punishment, with arms and with whatsoever military strength, according to his calling, he fight for the Church of God, and for an honest commonwealth, for altars and hearths, for the law and for the flock.

Celebrated is the saying of **Demosthenes**: ***πόλεμος ἔνδοξος εἰρήνης αἰσχρᾶς αἱρετώτερος***: *An honourable war is to be preferred before a shameful peace.* For it is most shameful to neglect the preservation[^C4-14] of doctrine, the defence of peace, of discipline, of the laws and of our country. And excellently is that of **Homer** celebrated, where Hector saith, in book 5 of the *Iliad*:[^C4-15]

> ***οὐχ οἱ ἀεικὲς ἀμυνομένῳ περὶ πάτρης τεθνάμεν.***  
> *a most beautiful thing it is to meet death*  
> *for one's country, and to make safe the household gods,*  
> *wives, infants, laws, and sweet parents,*  
> *by one's own death.*  

<!-- p.133 -->

**Philip Melanchthon.**

> *For one's country it is a great glory to pour out life:*  
> *for the rest of the citizens are covered by our valour,*  
> *and meanwhile mothers and children shall remain safe,*  
> *who are the seed of the age to come; and the city's*  
> *temples remain, and good laws, and piety and modesty;*  
> *and then shall every man enjoy his own goods and lands,*  
> *when the enemies, driven out, shall flee into their native realms.*  

God grant that we may one day stand with united forces against the Turks, or other foreign enemies, hostile to the Christian name and to pure doctrine, even though life must be poured out.

Worthy of remembrance unto all posterity is the Attic oath, which the soldiers of old took after this manner: *"I will not do things unworthy of the sacred arms, neither will I forsake my **παραστάτην**, that is, my fellow of the maniple and him that standeth by me, to whomsoever I shall be joined in the battle. And I will fight for the temples and for holy things, both alone and with many. I will not make my country worse, but better than I received it. And I will always modestly obey the things adjudged, and the laws laid down, and those hereafter to be laid down by common consent. And if any man shall change the laws, or shall not obey, I will not suffer it. And I will be a defender both alone and with all; and I will honour the sacred rites of my country.* ***ἵστορες θεοὶ τούτων.*** *The gods are witnesses of these sayings."*

Now these things being premised in general, we will here subjoin these two things to be considered. 1. What things before war are chiefly to be revolved in the mind of the lawful magistrate and of the soldiers. 2. In war, and especially when fortune smileth and victory is obtained, how the godly magistrate ought to conduct himself. Of these we shall treat most briefly.

Before war therefore be undertaken, these things are to be premeditated. First, that war be not undertaken out of lightness, malice, desire of vengeance, or <!-- p.134 -->other private affection, but that this be done when a necessity well-nigh extreme presseth and constraineth. For almost all histories do testify that the issues of wars rashly and headlong undertaken have been unhappy. Whence the word of **Scipio** is celebrated, who said that *one ought nowhere to fight, except when there is either necessity or the highest opportunity.* And I remember that **D. Philip** said in a lecture: *"I believe,"* said he, *"that all ordinary magistrates have been slow to move wars, as also our Emperor Charles doth, who hath begun, so far as I know, only two wars, in which he was always unfortunate, namely the war against the King of France, where he lost an army infected with pestilence and wasted with famine; and next, when he made an expedition into Africa, where the army perished by shipwreck. In the other wars, of which he himself was not the author, he was fortunate."*

Let the younger sort learn by heart the most true sentence of **Pindar**: ***ῥᾴδιον μὲν γὰρ πόλιν σεῖσαι καὶ φαυροτέροις***, *it is easy even for the baser sort to shake a commonwealth, but to settle it again in quiet is hard, unless God be the pilot*; in the 4th Ode of the *Pythians*.

Now that cometh to pass which the same saith in the *Nemeans*: *Although men are conscious to themselves of their own weakness, and know not at what moment they must depart out of this life, yet they are proud, and attempt many things haughtily. For our limbs are bound to shameless hope. And the streams of foresight are removed from us.*

Secondly, war is not to be set on foot to this end, that we may thereby possess and acquire other men's wealth, dominions and goods, as for the most part the heathen wage wars to that end, as **Sallust** saith, *for the sake of empire and riches*; as this very Mosaic history testifieth concerning the Chaldeans, who would have subjected to themselves that most flourishing part of the land which lay on this side of Sodom. And **Augustine** saith rightly: *"To serve as a soldier is not an offence, <!-- p.135 -->but to serve as a soldier for the sake of booty is an offence."* And in **Isa. 33** it is written: *"Woe to thee that spoilest, for thou shalt be spoiled."*

**Here by the way it is wont to be asked: Whether things seized in wars are to be kept.**

Now the true answer is this: They that wage lawful and just wars become lords of the bodies and goods which they take; therefore they justly hold and possess both the bodies and the goods taken. And although very many things befall in wars more outrageous than the exact rule of justice requireth, and the mean in these actions cannot be exactly determined, yet if the principal cause be just, the things seized are justly possessed by the warrior; yet so, that needless cruelty be avoided, and that ***ἐπιείκεια*** and a compassion worthy of a godly soldier be used.

Thirdly, heed must be taken that the cause be good and just, not evil, not devised and feigned, not painted over with a false pretext, but true, open, notorious; that is, that it be either a defence against unjust and outrageous force offered, or the execution of a lawful punishment, namely when war is undertaken by the superior and ordinary power to punish the manifest wickedness of some subject people; as the Israelites moved war to punish the tribe of Benjamin for the violation of the Levite's wife, **Judg. 19, 20**. Whence **Cassiodorus** saith: *"Then only is it profitable to run to arms, when justice can find no place with the adversary."* And **Cicero** saith: *"Wars are to be undertaken for this cause, that men may live in peace without injury."* And **Livy**: *"Where the cause is just, there victory hath always stood."* And it is commonly said:

> *The better cause biddeth us hope that the gods will be favourable.*  

Likewise: *At length the good cause triumpheth.*

Fourthly, with all care it is to be avoided that war be undertaken against the lawful superior and ordinary magistrate, even though the cause in all its circumstances seem just. Defence indeed against unjust and <!-- p.136 -->manifest force is granted. But that the inferior should be the author of the war, and make the beginning, is in no wise granted, and, when it happeneth, it hath issues exceeding unhappy and tragical, according to that of **Sirach 27**: *"Whoso casteth a stone on high casteth it on his own head."* **Prov. 26**: *"He that rolleth a stone, it will return upon him."* Of this matter **Luther** hath written splendidly in the book *Whether Soldiers Can Be in a Christian Estate*,[^C4-16] setting down three degrees of persons contending with one another, namely, of equal against equal; next, of the superior against the inferior and subject; afterwards, of the inferior against the superior. The first is granted in the case of defence, and in the execution of a punishment imposed by the lawful magistrate. The second also is granted, in that manner which hath hitherto been set forth. The third is forbidden. For obedience is owed to the magistrate, which no man ought to deny, unless the magistrate himself will not admit it. And although there be diversity in religion, and the magistrate be an idolater, yet obedience is owed him in political things, as Jeremiah biddeth the captive Jews in Babylon to pray for the idolatrous and heathen magistrate and king, under whose power they then were, saying: *"In the peace thereof shall ye have peace,"* **Jerem. 29**. And it is expressly written, **Rom. 13**: *"Whosoever resisteth the power, resisteth the ordinance of God."*

Now we must treat briefly of the other point, namely what the godly magistrate, when necessity presseth and constraineth him to war, ought to do in the war itself, and when it hath been happily ended, and how he ought to conduct himself.

First, let there be piety and the invocation of God, without whose leading and blessing war obtaineth tragical issues. ***ἔα πόλεμον, ἔα μάχαν χωρὶς θεοῦ***, saith **Pindar**: *Let war alone, let battle alone, without God.* Likewise: ***σὺν θεῷ τοὺς σοφοὺς κινεῖν δόρυ στρατηλάτας χρή, τοῦ θεοῦ δὲ μὴ βίᾳ***: *With God wise captains ought to move the spear, but not against his will.* And **Homer** saith:

> *He giveth strength to the oppressed, and taketh it from the proud;*  
> <!-- p.137 -->*these he maketh conquerors, those in turn he will have conquered.*  

Secondly, let trust in human defences, in the army, the forces, the confederates, riches and the like, be taken out of the mind. For that foolish persuasion and arrogance hath cast down and confounded many, as the histories of all times do testify. Therefore it is written, **Psalm 44**: *"I will not trust in my bow, neither shall my sword save me. In thy name we will overthrow our enemies."* And hither pertaineth the most sweet **Psalm 127**:

> *Unless God build the houses and keep the city,*  
> *then shall our labour without God be vain.*  

And Solomon saith, **Prov. 21**: *"The horse is prepared against the day of battle, but safety is of the Lord."* Therefore all rash confidence in our own strength, and in human wisdom, in power, in men, in cities and human defences, and in the aids of others, is to be put away. *"For to the strong their own confidence is destruction,"* saith Solomon. And the wise **Homer** saith rightly:

> *We ought to trust in God, who hath bidden us hope,*  
> *with whom is the everlasting power over men and heaven.*  

> ***τὰ δὲ οὐκ ἀνδράσι κεῖται.***  

*Fortune cometh from God, who exalteth one and casteth down another.* ***ἐκ θεοῦ γὰρ μαχαναὶ πᾶσαι βροτέαις ἀρεταῖς· καὶ σοφοί, καὶ χερσὶ βιαταί***, saith **Pindar**: *From God is all the effecting of human powers, the wise, the strong, and the eloquent.*

Just leagues indeed with good men, joined in one faith, are not blamed or forbidden, nor garrisons, nor bulwarks; but security and confidence is reproved by God.

At this day many leagues are made, few are kept. Most are set up with evil and ungodly men, and are dissolved with the like folly and rashness wherewith they are bound together. *With the good, the sailing is good.* But fellowships and covenants with idolaters lack the blessing of God.

It is reported of certain Imperial Cities, that they were once minded to enter into a league among themselves, and concluded <!-- p.138 -->unanimously that grave and aged men should be employed for the deliberation concerning the making of the league, and that no man who was under sixty years should be admitted. Now it happened that, whether by design or by chance, a certain young envoy was sent, whom, when the rest would not admit him into the sitting, they at the first asked how many years old he was; for without doubt he knew that it had been decreed by the unanimous consent of all that no man should be present in the council unless he had reached at the least the sixtieth year of his age. That envoy answered that he had long since passed a hundred years. Some, thinking themselves mocked by this answer, others judging him to be mad, asked how that could be possible. He answered that a few years before he had been employed for the same deliberation concerning the confirming of a league, and that at that time the league had been entered into and confirmed for fifty years; but that a little after, a tumult having arisen, it had been broken and dissolved. And that he remembered that the confederation was taken up anew and ratified for thirty years; and that he had seen the end of these also. These years therefore, joined together, make eighty. And the envoy showed that, when he was first employed, he had been thirty years old. This answer being heard, one of the elders said that he was deservedly admitted, and had truly and rightly proved his age; but that to themselves was to be imputed the disgrace both of rashness in lightly making leagues, and of inconstancy and lightness in dissolving them again, and upon a light occasion. For that most men play with leagues and oaths, as the old word is, as boys play with knuckle-bones.

To have castles and bulwarks is likewise not forbidden, if security and confidence be absent, as we have said above in the eleventh chapter. But let magistrates see that they build not fortifications and castles against the will of God. For ***θεοῦ ἀέκητι τέτυκτο ἀθανάτου· τῷ καὶ οὔτι πολὺν χρόνον ἔμπεδον ἦεν***, saith **Homer** of the wall of the Greeks: *Fortifications built against the will of God <!-- p.139 -->perish in a short time.* That also often cometh to pass which **Cicero** said: *That all strongholds can be taken, to which only an ass laden with gold can come near.*

**Luther, on Psalm 147.**[^C4-17]

*"Thou oughtest to build, and to make bars, and to fortify the city, and to furnish thyself with good laws and ordinances, as much and as well as thou canst. But herein beware, lest, when thou hast done these things, thou trust in them, and say: Now I sit secure and firm, and all things are well; as the heathen did, as the King of Babylon, **Dan. 4**. But write this beside it as a motto: Do thou, O God, bring help; otherwise we shall profit nothing by these fortifications."*

Well known are the verses of **Sabinus**[^C4-18] concerning the German fortifications, which I will set down:

> *When the towns stood girt about with ruinous walls,*  
> *and there was neither steep ditch nor rampart,*  
> *then, renowned Germany, thou didst flourish in the praise of war,*  
> *no enemy striking any fears into thee.*  
> *But now that thou art fortified with deep ditches,*  
> *and sand walleth in thy cities with ramparts,*  
> *now thou lackest valour, now as a suppliant thou dost of thine own accord stretch forth*  
> *thine unwarlike hands to all thine enemies.*  
> *Surely, as their great horns help the timorous stags,*  
> *so do thy walls, girt about with ditches, help thee.*  

Thirdly, there is need of prudence and circumspection, namely lest either the enemy be despised, or the danger made light of. *Assuredly danger cometh the sooner when it is despised.* And Solomon saith, **Prov. 20**: *"Every purpose is established by counsel, and by counsels war is waged."* And it is commonly said: *By the counsel of the captain is the valour of the soldiers governed.* ***γνῶμαι γὰρ πλέον κρατοῦσιν ἢ σθένος χερῶν***: *Counsels prevail more than the strength of hands.* For which cause **Hannibal** said that he feared Fabius resting and <!-- p.140 -->delaying, that is, doing all things prudently, more than Marcellus fighting. For it often cometh to pass that an army unwary, and buried in sleep and wine, is most easily overthrown. For a great beast without a head, as **Epaminondas** was wont to say, is easily overcome.

The Turkish Emperor is reported once to have said of the Germans that he feared them not, for five causes. First, that God is blasphemed by them, and that very God whom they worship and call upon, and by whom they say that they are defended and preserved. Next, that they are as much at concord among themselves as are the five fingers of the hand, of which there is great unlikeness. Thirdly, that they are impatient of labours, of thirst, hunger, heat and watchings, and are intemperate, drunken, wanton and slothful. Fourthly, that they are not studious and observant of order, but are unwary, headlong, and attempt most things rashly. Fifthly, that they are despisers of military justice, that they do not requite virtues with rewards, nor punish the wicked, and are fierce toward the wretched and merciful toward the heinous.

Fourthly, the goods of private persons and of the poor are to be abstained from, and soldiers ought to be content with their wages, and not to hurt or spoil the innocent.

**Aelius Lampridius, of Alexander Severus.**

*"If any man had turned aside from the road into any one's possession, he was, according to the quality of the place, subjected in his sight either to cudgels, or to rods, or to condemnation; or, if the dignity of the man exceeded all these, to the gravest reproaches, when he would say: Wouldest thou have this done in thy field, which thou doest to another? And he would often cry out that which he had heard from certain men, whether Jews or Christians, and kept in mind, and he commanded it to be spoken by the crier when he corrected any man: What thou wilt not have done to thyself, do not thou to another. Which <!-- p.141 -->sentence he loved so greatly, that he commanded it to be written up in the palace and on the public works."*
