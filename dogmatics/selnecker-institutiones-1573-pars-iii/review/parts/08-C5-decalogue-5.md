<!-- p.141 -->

**Spartianus** also relateth of Pescennius Niger: *"For the plundering of a single cock,"* saith he, *"he commanded ten comrades of one maniple, who had eaten that which one of them had seized, to be smitten with the axe; and he would have done it, had he not been entreated by the whole army, well-nigh to the fear of a mutiny. And when he had spared them, he commanded that they should never take food freshly cooked, but should feed on bread and cold fare, watchers being set over them."*[^C5-1]

But what shall we say of Christian soldiers? who, whether they go any whither or return, steal, plunder, ravish, drive off cattle, stave in vessels, and handle their own people more inhumanly than enemies?

It is read of Croesus, when he had been overcome by Cyrus, and the soldiers of Cyrus were spoiling the city: *"What is this I hear?"* saith Croesus. *"Are not these things now thine, who hast overcome me? Why then do the soldiers lay waste that which is thine?"* Admonished by this word, Cyrus restrained the soldiery from pillage.[^C5-2]

King Lycurgus is read to have charged his soldiers and horsemen that they should spare the captured, and remember that they themselves also were men. This humanity won for him the wonderful goodwill not only of others, but even of the captives.

**Fifthly,** when victory hath been obtained, cruelty is not to be exercised. *"For nothing is more cruel than to raze cities,"* saith **Cicero**. *To spare the subdued, and to war down the proud,*[^C5-3] is the duty and the ornament of godly soldiers. For this cause Philip, King of the Macedonians, after the victory obtained against the Athenians, commanded these words to be daily dinned into him by the voice of a noble boy: *"Philip, remember that thou art a man."* For that may befall any man which befalleth any. And nothing more becometh a victor than clemency, as it is wont to be said: *Nothing is great which is not gentle.* Likewise: *He conquereth twice, who conquereth himself in victory.*[^C5-4]

<!-- p.142 -->

**Sixthly,** thanks are to be rendered to God for success and victory, and peace is thenceforth to be studied, that the true doctrine concerning God, and the other useful and good arts, and honest laws, may be propagated and preserved.

Let these things be said concerning Christian wars, how they ought and may be waged lawfully, rightly, and with a good conscience. What is further required concerning that matter doth not belong to us. Neither is it seemly that Phormio should move Hannibal to laughter.[^C5-5] But this must be added, that the wars which are undertaken and waged at this day, in these dregs of the world, are for the most part pernicious, evil, tyrannical, ambitious, rash and ruinous. And of these the verses of **Homer** may truly be spoken:

> ***ἀφρήτωρ, ἀθέμιστος, ἀνέστιός ἐστιν ἐκεῖνος,***  
> ***ὃς πολέμου ἔραται ἐπιδημίου, ὀκρυόεντος.***[^C5-6]  

> *A shameful man is this, wicked, unprofitable, witless,*  
> *whosoever loveth to stir up among the peoples intestine wars,*  
> *which bring so great evils with them, and bring causes of evils*  
> *innumerable, without end, by civil arms.*  

Likewise **Lucan**:

> *No faith (or at the least rare) and no piety is in the men who follow the camp;*  
> *and their hands are for sale: there is right, where is the greatest wage.*[^C5-7]  

It is manifest also that the most part of the wars of our time seem to be only ***μυομαχίας*** (battles of mice), and to be, as Christ called them, *rumours of wars*, that is, not wars of the Monarchies, the end whereof is peace and the establishment of an honest life and of tranquillity, of laws and of discipline, but to be robberies, tyrannical, ambitious and tumultuous; against which we ought to pray unto God, as David saith: *"Scatter the nations that will have wars."*

But if any godly soldiers and captains remain, whose service we may use against the Turk and other foreign enemies, and haters of the divine word and of honest laws, for these let us give
<!-- p.143 -->
thanks unto God, and let us reverence and highly esteem them, as the *Thundering Legion*, whose prayers God gladly heareth, as **Eusebius** writeth concerning the prayers of the Christian soldiers, who obtained rain for the army of Antoninus, and overthrew the hostile forces of the Germans and Sarmatians with thunderbolts.[^C5-8]

There is extant a little book of **Ferrandus**, Deacon of the Church of Carthage, to Count Reginus, a *Paraeneticus* (an exhortation), what manner of man a Christian captain ought to be in the actions of war.[^C5-9] In it these seven rules are delivered.

1. Let the captain believe that in all his actions he hath need of the help of God, and that without Him he availeth nothing.
2. Let his own life be a mirror unto the soldiers, that they may see what they ought to do.
3. Let him study not to be over others, but to profit them.
4. Let him love the commonwealth as his own life and safety.
5. Let him set divine things before human.
6. Let him not be overmuch righteous, and overmuch wise, after his own judgment and persuasion.
7. Let him remember that he is a Christian.

But of these matters thus far.

### *De Zelo* · On Zeal

The Hebrews name zeal *Keneoh*,[^C5-10] the Germans *Eifer*. The Hebrews say that it is a passion of the mind arising from this, that a man feareth lest another should have that which he would have in common with no other. **Paul** rendereth vicious zeal by *contention* and *emulation*, **Rom. 13**: ***ἔριδα καὶ ζῆλον***. And **Philip. 1**: ***φθόνον καὶ ἔριν***, envy and contention, that is, an envious contention. The Greeks define ***ζῆλον*** as ***διέγερσιν ψυχῆς εἰς καλὰ πρὸς παράδειγμα ὁρώσης***, that is, a rousing[^C5-11] of the mind unto honest things,
<!-- p.144 -->
of a mind that looketh unto a pattern. And **Suidas** nameth zeal ***ἀγαθοῦ τινος ἐπιθυμίαν, φθόνου τινὸς χωρὶς ἐγγινομένην τῇ ψυχῇ***, a desire of some good, existing in the mind without any envy; as if a man should say: That man is endued with fortitude, therefore I emulate his virtue; that is, I do not envy him, but I also study to attain the same good, and would be a valiant man. And an example is cited out of **Appian**: Dentatus was followed, out of emulation of his virtue, by a multitude of chosen young men, to the number of eight hundred, who were ready to do all things; whence also he was grievous to the Senate in the assemblies.

But these definitions are not sufficient enough. The definition will be sufficient, if three things be considered which are wont to concur in the affections of the mind: namely the motion of the mind itself, which in zeal is twofold, either love or indignation; then the object, which also is twofold, either a thing pleasing or unpleasing; afterwards the end, which is either the defence of the pleasing object, or the shutting out of the unpleasing object. Let this therefore be the definition.

**Zeal is an affection mixed of love and of anger or indignation, not suffering that to be given to another which a man judgeth ought to be given to himself, or to some one whom he loveth, and desiring that the thing loved be and remain whole, inviolate and safe, and that he enjoy it who ought, but that the stranger be shut out:** as Elias in zeal slayeth the false prophets; namely, loving God vehemently, he was indignant at the enemies of God, **3 Kings 18**. So a just governor ought to burn with zeal, that is, with the love of justice, and with indignation against the wicked. And in the sixty-ninth Psalm the Son of God saith: *"The zeal of thine house hath eaten me up."* Which words **Nonnus**, on **John 2**, hath rendered in a most sweet little verse:

> ***ζῆλος ἐμὲ ζαθέοιο τεοῦ καταδαίνυται οἴκου.***  

> (*Zeal for thy most holy house devoureth me.*)  

For no man ought to be negligent or cold in his office, seeing it is written: *"Cursed is he that doeth the work of the Lord negligently,"* **Jerem. 48**. And the word *Remiioth*[^C5-12] (which is commonly rendered
<!-- p.145 -->
*fraudulently*) properly signifieth deception and neglect, or fraudulence and hurtful sloth, whereby a man deceiveth himself and others, and hurteth himself and others, as all slothful men do. God therefore requireth zeal, which hath its name either from ***ζηλοῦν***, that is, from imitating another, or rather from ***ζέειν***, that is, from being fervent, namely in the study and labours of vocation, of godliness, and of honesty.

**But it is asked why the thirty-seventh Psalm forbiddeth us zeal.** That we may answer here, the usual divisions are to be held, which are both expounded elsewhere, and may be seen in this Table.

**Zeal is of one kind or another:**

- **Divine,** which is twofold:
    - *beside knowledge:* erroneous zeal, violence and fury, **Rom. 10; Phil. 3; Acts 8; Galat. 1**.
    - *according to knowledge* and the true knowledge of God; and this zeal is either
        - *seasonable,* as in Moses, Elias and Christ; or
        - *unseasonable* and pernicious.
- **Human,** which is either
    - *of duty,* and honest; or
    - *contrary to duty,* base and tragical.
- **Diabolical** zeal, unto death.

**Divine zeal beside knowledge** is an affection born of the love of propagating the truth, and of indignation against those whom it supposeth to be enemies, or at the least depravers of the true doctrine,
<!-- p.146 -->
but falsely. For this zeal hath for its antecedent cause ignorance, or unskilfulness of the truth, and yet an opinion of true understanding. And **Paul** himself also laboured under such zeal, **Acts 8 and 9**. And Joshua against Eldad and Medad, **Num. 11**. And at this day many, even good men, are moved by zeal of this kind, so that they condemn others who have deserved well of the Church, and who teach rightly, whom they themselves hold suspected of errors of doctrine. And this zeal, when it conceiveth flames and, by overmuch indulging itself, looseth the reins, degenerateth into blind violence and fury, envy and hatred, pride and cruelty, as examples everywhere do testify at this day.

**Divine zeal according to knowledge** is an affection sprung from true knowledge and the true fear of God, and inflamed with the love of propagating the divine word, and with indignation or hatred of those things which hinder the course of the word, or which delay, corrupt and deform the true doctrine, whether by idolatry, or by labyrinths of disputations, or by tyranny, or by inconstancy, or by perverseness of life and evil examples. Now this zeal is either seasonable or unseasonable.

**Seasonable divine zeal** is that which burneth at a fit time, and hath sufficient causes of indignation, and overpasseth not the bounds of vocation and of charity, and seeketh nothing else but the glory of God, and directeth all things to edification and to the conversion of many unto God, and to the destruction of ungodliness. Examples in the sacred Scriptures are many. Moses casteth down and breaketh the Tables, and burneth the calf, **Exod. 32**. And against Korah, Dathan and Abiram he ordereth his prayers, in **Numbers, chap. 16**. Phinehas, the grandson of Aaron, burning with zeal for chastity and for propagating the glory of God, slayeth the Israelite who was committing whoredom with the Midianitish woman, **Numer. 25**. Christ overturneth the tables and seats of the merchants in the Temple, and himself saith, **Psalm 69**: *"The zeal of thine house maketh me to pine away, because
<!-- p.147 -->
mine enemies have forgotten thy words,"*[^C5-13] that is, I could not refrain myself from punishing those impure profaners, neither felt I any rest, unless thy Church were reformed, which I perceived to be miserably depraved. Elias, moved by such zeal, slayeth the priests of Baal. **Luther** in like manner, kindled with divine zeal joined with knowledge, seasonably, and upon a most fit occasion, reproved the Papistical errors, and showed forth the true doctrine anew.

**Unseasonable zeal** is that which observeth no fitness of time, or of other necessary circumstances, but by a certain unwary affection of the mind stirreth certain things which might with greater profit to the Church have been deferred, and reserved unto occasions more convenient, and more useful and fruitful both to those very persons against whom the indignation goeth forth, and to others also. I will add one example.

**Theodoret, *Ecclesiastical History*, Book 5, chap. 39.**

Isdigerdes, King of the Persians, moved war against the Church upon this occasion. Audas[^C5-14] was a bishop endued with manifold virtues. He, stirred up by an unseasonable ardour, overthrew a ***πυρεῖον***, a temple of flame or of fire (for they think fire to be a deity). The King, being informed of this matter by the Magi, sendeth for Audas, and at the first, having gently accused him, commandeth him to restore the Pyreum; which when Audas said that he would not do, the King threateneth that he will overthrow all the churches, and as he had threatened, so he did. And when he had commanded that holy man to be slain, then thereafter he commandeth all the churches to be pulled down. And I indeed judge that the overthrow of the Pyreum was not seasonable. For neither did the holy Apostle, when he was come to Athens, and had seen the city to be given over to the worship of images, overthrow any altar which they venerated, but by speech
<!-- p.148 -->
he reproved their ignorance, and taught the truth, etc.

Such was the zeal of the baker who, nigh twenty years ago, at Vienna rushed into a solemn procession, in which the consecrated bread was being carried about, King Ferdinand being present, and snatched from the sacrificing priest the *Monstrance*, as they call it, and trod it to pieces under his feet.

To this unseasonable zeal is referred also overmuch indignation against the felicity and prosperity of the ungodly in this life, which indignation is forbidden as pernicious.

Now for seasonable zeal very many things are required, of which these are the chief. 1. True knowledge of the will of God, and of the true doctrine. 2. True invocation of God, and godliness of life. 3. A certain acknowledgment of the errors which conflict with the true doctrine, or of the crimes which are contrary to godliness. 4. Moderation of private affections, of anger, hatred, envy, revenge. 5. A care to perform the duties of one's vocation, and to stir nothing by any private counsel and will. 6. Consideration of opportunity, lest a spark, being stirred, bring forth a fire which cannot easily be quenched.[^C5-15] 7. An heroical and divine spirit, burning with the love of the glory of God, and yielding to no evils, but rather suffering death and any torments whatsoever, than permitting itself to be plucked away from God and his truth. 8. Modesty joined with gravity, undertaking nothing rashly, headlong and unadvisedly, but going forward step by step to show the truth and to confute falsehood, and always having regard to the weaker, lest they be offended or provoked by overmuch violence.

For the rest, **human zeal of duty** is industry and alacrity in vocation; as the preacher in the church ought to be diligent in the duties of his vocation, the prince in the kingdom, the parent in the household, the preceptor in the school, the scholar in learning useful and necessary precepts. And hither belongeth the exposition of the whole Decalogue,
<!-- p.149 -->
and the table containing the rules of our whole life, political, ecclesiastical and domestic. **Human zeal contrary to duty** is either that of the unchaste and wanton, or is that of men plainly raging, as Medea rageth, tearing her child in pieces, and Nero rageth, crying out: *"When I am dead, let the earth be mingled with fire."*[^C5-16]

**Diabolical zeal** is either a burning hatred of the true worship of God, as Cain, the Scribes and the Pharisees pursued the true worship of God with extreme hatred; or it is persecution itself, or the study and endeavour of oppressing the truth, as the Turks, the Papists, the Jews, and other tyrants and heretics are stirred up by the spirit of Satan to oppress the truth. Let it suffice to have gathered these things concerning zeal, from which it is manifest that vicious zeal is to be discerned from that which is right and laudable, and that in the Psalms and elsewhere private and vicious indignation and impatience is forbidden, but that just zeal is not forbidden, joined with silence and hope, and, when need is, with courage and liberty of the Spirit; concerning which nevertheless certain precepts cannot be delivered, seeing this whole matter belongeth to the peculiar efficacy of the Holy Spirit, and to the examination and proving of every man's conscience.

### *De promissionibus et comminationibus quinti Praecepti* · On the Promises and Threatenings of the Fifth Commandment

In **Luke 6** it is written: *"Forgive, and it shall be forgiven you."* For meekness, gentleness and placableness have always great rewards, as the Son of God saith: *"Blessed are the meek, for they shall possess the earth. Blessed are the merciful, for they shall obtain mercy."* And examples do testify that the labours, the patience and the meekness of the godly are blessed in many ways. And the threatenings also are known: *"He that taketh the sword shall perish by the sword." "Whoso sheddeth man's blood, by man shall his blood be shed." "With what measure ye mete, with the same shall it be rendered
<!-- p.150 -->
unto you." "Judgment without mercy unto him that showeth no mercy."* Examples in Cain, Joab, and in the seditious and the wrathful, are before our eyes in all times. God grant that we may consider the saying of Christ: *"Learn of me, for I am meek and lowly in heart."*

## *De VI. Praecepto* · On the Sixth Commandment

This commandment fenceth the covenants of marriage, and forbiddeth all minglings and defilements outside this order instituted by God. The virtues of this commandment are:

- Chastity.
- Purity of the single life.
- Conjugal chastity.
- Modesty or shamefastness.
- Temperance.

**Chastity** or pudicity (*castitas sive pudicitia*) in general is the virtue which keepeth that cleanness of mind and of body which agreeth with the will of God, and which shunneth all lusts forbidden by God.

**Purity of the single life** (*puritas vitae caelibis*) is the virtue which keepeth cleanness of mind and of body without any abuse of seed, without mingling and defilement of bodies, and without unchaste thoughts, and without any burnings of lusts.

**Marriage** is the lawful and indissoluble conjunction of one male and one female, ordained, established and approved by God, for procreation, and for the avoiding of the confusions of lusts, that we may acknowledge God to be a chaste mind, and may serve him in chastity, and that in this manner the human race may be preserved and propagated, that out of it an eternal Church may be gathered unto God. And **conjugal chastity** (*castitas coniugalis*) is, in marriage, to keep the order instituted by the wonderful counsel of God, without wandering lusts, unto lawful procreation, with mutual love, with sincere faith, with a common fellowship of goods and of hardships, and with defence and ***ἐπιείκεια*** (forbearance) used.

<!-- p.151 -->

**Modesty** or shamefastness (*verecundia seu pudor*) is the virtue which standeth in awe of true infamy, which always accompanieth offences, and which, through fear of the divine judgment, and of its own conscience, and of others who judge rightly, restraineth all desires, and shunneth offences, and so ruleth speech, gestures and the habit of the body, that it may agree with the order of nature, of persons and of places.

**Temperance** or sobriety (*temperantia seu sobrietas*) is the virtue which keepeth a mean in meat, drink and other things, agreeable to nature, and which hindereth not by gluttony prayer, meditation, the labours of vocation, and the exercises of the other virtues, nor hurteth sleep and health by overmuch fasting.

### *Antithesis* · The Antithesis

With chastity conflicteth all uncleanness, and incontinence, impurity, and wandering lusts, and whoredoms outside marriage.

With purity of the single life conflict in like manner the same sins, ravishings, incest, and the like turpitudes.

With conjugal chastity conflict all adulteries, and common and light divorces, perfidy, desertion, rancour, tyranny, frowardness, disdain, suspicion, dissembling.

With modesty conflict impudence, ***δυσωπία*** or stupor, obscenity in speech and gesture, ***εὐτραπελία***, scurrility and filthiness.

With temperance conflicteth drunkenness, voracity, surfeiting, gluttony, riot, profusion, superfluity in meats, apparel, household stuff and other things, superstitious and immoderate fasts, superstitious ***ἰχθυοφαγία*** (fish-eating), overmuch abstinence that hurteth the health.

### *Promissiones et comminationes sexti praecepti notae sunt* · The Promises and Threatenings of the Sixth Commandment Are Known

<!-- p.152 -->

**Matth. 5.** *"Blessed are the pure in heart, for they shall see God."* **1 Cor. 5.**[^C5-17] *"Fornicators and adulterers shall not possess the Kingdom of God."* **Hebr. 13.** *"Whoremongers and adulterers God will punish, or judge."* **Heb. 12.** *"Follow after sanctification, without which no man shall see God."* And examples of horrible punishments do show the wrath of God against lusts, as the burning of Sodom, the destruction of the tribe of Benjamin, the exile of David, the slaughter of the Lacedaemonians in the battle of Leuctra, because of the unpunished ravishers of the daughters of Scedasus, and daily other examples, public and private. Let us therefore be studious and lovers of chastity, and let us use seed reverently, and let us resist the depraved and wandering flames of lusts by prayer, by reading, by the study of temperance, and by the removing of the occasions and enticements whereby the foul burnings of minds are kindled or cherished; such as are wine, wherein is ***ἀσωτία*** (riotousness), immoderately taken, evil companies, ***γυναικὸς ὄμμα τοῖς ἀκμάζουσι βέλος*** (a woman's eye is a dart unto them that are in their prime), and the like enticements.

## *De VII. Praecepto* · On the Seventh Commandment

This commandment is the hedge and the wall wherewith God fenceth our little cottages and little nests, and the things necessary for sustenance, against thieves and robbers, and he approveth the distinction of ownerships both by his commandment and by the fear of punishments. For it is certain that every man's own house is divinely fenced by the Law of God and by the fear of punishments much more than by any walls, ditches, mounds, ramparts and towers, of all which it is rightly said: *"Help us, we perish."*

Now let the order of the commandments be considered: the Fifth Commandment guardeth the life and the bodies of each; the Sixth, marriages; the Seventh, the houses and nests of each, and the things necessary. And the virtues of this commandment are:

- Just possession and the good use of one's own things.
- <!-- p.153 --> Commutative justice.
- Beneficence or liberality.
- Hospitality.
- Parsimony or frugality.
- ***αὐτάρκεια*** (contentment).
- Gratitude.
- Sedulity.

**Just possession** (*iusta possessio*) is the virtue which, in the fear of God, and in faith and a good conscience, holdeth its own goods, and which, acknowledging that they are given and kept by God, diligently studieth to preserve them, and to increase them by honest means, and to bestow them upon the uses ordained by God. Hither belongeth also the definition of ownership (*dominium*). Ownership is a right or faculty ordained by law, whereby the owner, and no other, holdeth a thing by a just title, and disposeth of that thing at his own will, save so far as he is forbidden by law and by covenant. And this is to be held against the Anabaptists and the like. For it is lawful for Christians to have property, riches and goods, so that pride, avarice, security, prodigality and abuse be taken away. And it is written: *"The blessing of the Lord maketh rich."* And the examples of Abraham, Isaac, Jacob, Joseph and others are known. And in general the rule is to be held, that the Gospel doth not abolish political ordinances.

**Commutative justice** (*iustitia commutativa*) is the virtue which in contracts, or in the exchange of things, keepeth an arithmetical equality, lest the one party be drained dry. Hither belongeth the whole doctrine of contracts, ***ἢ συναλλάγμασιν*** (or dealings), and of obligations.

**Beneficence** (*beneficentia*) is the virtue which imparteth goods and services not owed by a civil obligation, but by a natural and divine one, where and when it is seemly, and which maketh a mean between profusions and niggardliness. Hither belong alms, of which three kinds are necessary. The first, to contribute something to the preservation of the ecclesiastical ministry, according to that saying: *"He that shall give unto the least
<!-- p.154 -->
in the name of a disciple, shall not lose his reward."* The second kind is to bestow upon needy kinsfolk. *"For if any man careth not for his own, and specially for those of his own house, he is worse than an infidel."* The third kind is to help strangers, whom the present occasion commendeth unto us, as it is written: *"He that hath goods, and seeth his brother have need, and shutteth up his bowels from him, how abideth the love of God in him?"* **1 John 3**. And **Deut. 15**: *"If one of thy brethren, who dwell within the gates of thy city, in the land which the Lord thy God is about to give thee, shall be brought to poverty, thou shalt not stop up thine heart."* And: *"The poor shall not be wanting in the land of thy habitation: therefore I command thee, that thou open thine hand unto thy needy and poor brother, who dwelleth with thee in the land."* And **Paul** appointeth a measure for alms, **2 Cor. 8**, saying: *"Not that there should be ease unto others, and unto you tribulation; but that out of an equality, for the present occasion, out of that which remaineth over unto us, their want may be relieved."* Now the equality is understood not as arithmetical, but as geometrical, in respect both of the giver and of the receiver. For from greater numbers more units can be taken away, from lesser fewer. And the needy ought not to abuse another's liberality, nor to use that which is another's save in necessity. And beggary which hath not a reasonable cause is a sin, and a kind of theft. And there is no need to bestow upon slothful beggars. It is also to be looked to that alms be not profusions got up for ostentation, but that they provide for the necessity of others, because of the commandment of God. And let these sayings be known: *"Break thy bread unto the hungry; then shalt thou call, and the Lord shall hear thee."* **Isa. 58**.

**Proverb. 21.** *"He that stoppeth his ears at the cry of the poor, he also himself shall cry in his turn, and shall not be heard."* **Proverb. 5.** *"Drink of thine own fountains, and let thy rivulets be led abroad, and be thou lord of them. Blessed shall thy vein be."*

**Matth. 25.** *"I was hungry and thirsty, and ye gave me meat and
<!-- p.155 -->
drink."* And **Matth. 10.** *"He that shall give a drink of water for the doctrine's sake, shall not lack his reward."*

**Hospitality** (*hospitalitas*) is a kind of beneficence, whereby we embrace strangers and guests, and chiefly those who are in exile for the profession of the true doctrine, with true goodwill and with all the offices of hospitality.

**Parsimony** or frugality (*parsimonia seu frugalitas*) is the virtue which keepeth its own things, and layeth them out profitably, shunneth unnecessary charges, the itch of buying, riot, borrowings, buildings, and yet, where reason biddeth, spendeth as much as is meet.

***αὐτάρκεια*** (contentment) is the virtue which is content with the present blessing of God, or with present goods, and beareth it calmly if aught be lacking, neither coveteth the goods of others, nor is broken by want, nor doeth anything against the godliness owed unto God, or against the other virtues.

**Gratitude** (*gratitudo*) is a virtue mixed of truth and of justice, that is, it professeth what and how great benefits it hath received, and studieth to render an equal return, according to its ability, unto its benefactor.

**Sedulity** (*sedulitas*) is the virtue which constantly and firmly, for God's sake and for the common welfare, doeth the labours agreeable to its vocation, God helping, who hath promised help unto them that ask.

### *Antithesis* · The Antithesis

With just possession conflict all thefts, whereby the thing of another is handled, seized and held against the owner's will, without recompense; ***πλεονεξίαι*** (covetous overreachings), frauds and impostures, usuries, robberies of what sort soever, and chiefly of ecclesiastical goods and revenues, of bishoprics and priestly livings, the ministry being neglected, and studies being neglected. It is rightly said that to steal is the same as in any manner whatsoever to lie in wait for the goods of others, and to usurp them unto oneself. This God severely forbiddeth, because, seeing he is most just, he willeth that after the rule of his own justice we should render unto every man his own. He forbiddeth therefore the desire of having, and the effect
<!-- p.156 -->
of the same, that is, theft, which is twofold, human and diabolical. For so we speak for the sake of teaching. The former is committed either in things, or in right. In things, as are:

Privy purloining of another's thing.

Violence, unjust booty, robbery.

Imposture in weight, measure, counting, contract, the exchange of things, counterfeit wares, and the feigned and false commendation of things, of which it is said: *"He praiseth his wares, who would thrust them off for sale."*[^C5-18]

Pretext of right, when we transfer unto ourselves the things of others under some show of right.

Pretext of gift, when superiors ask something of their inferiors, which these dare not deny.

Pretext of prescription, as in ecclesiastical goods. And the interruption of a possession, or quasi-possession, grounded upon lapse of time is brought in by gainsaying, and by proof of possession in bad faith, as the wiser jurists judge and speak. And since the possessor of ecclesiastical goods, the ministry being neglected, and studies being neglected, is a possessor in bad faith, a robber and a spoiler, law and equity pronounce that he is bound, without distinction, to restore all the revenues, whether they be yet extant or be consumed. And altogether the rule hath place: *The sin is not remitted, unless that which was taken away be restored.*[^C5-19] And: *"He that hath stolen, let him henceforth steal no more,"* that is, let him render that which is another's, and cease to be a thief. Yet we do not shut out ***ἐπιείκειαν*** (equity), that offences may be avoided, and infamy be guarded against.

In right theft is committed when we render not unto others those things which we owe them by covenant or by duty; as when we pay not his wage unto the labourer, or show not the offices of charity unto our neighbour. For by a natural obligation we are mutually bound one to another, and each is debtor to the other; by a civil obligation also we are bound to pay wages.

Diabolical theft is that which is committed after the imitation
<!-- p.157 -->
of the devil, who stole the hearts of our first parents, and called them away from God, that they might cleave unto himself. Such are all the seditious, the contumacious, the rebellious, the disobedient, who draw minds away from the obedience owed unto parents, preceptors, the magistrate, the ministers of the word, and masters.

With commutative justice conflict unjust contracts, or frauds in contracts, impostures in wares, usuries, or gains exacted above the principal on account of the service of lending, robberies, unjust pillagings public and private, privy thefts in courts and in public offices, the thieves whereof walk in gold and purple; likewise unjust wages and stipends, which are paid unto the slothful and the idle, beggary that is not necessary, and all things ill gotten.

But it is to be known that if any man have revenues bought upon some fruit-bearing estate, he is not an usurer, because it is a purchase, not a lending. And the substantials of a purchase concur: the ware, the price, and the consent. Likewise, if a man have not the right of demanding the money again, the judgment of the magistrate and of the laws holdeth, which distinguish between usuries and *that which is of interest* (*id quod interest*).

With beneficence and liberality conflict immoderate profusions, and unnecessary charges in banquets, buildings, garments; avarice that doeth good unto none, niggardliness, tenacity, unlawful and forbidden carefulness, and distrust, which is the fountain of thefts and of frauds, and bringeth forth ***πλεονεκτήματα*** (overreachings) of every kind; likewise evil deeds and injuries, whereby we hurt the minds, the bodies, the good name, or the goods of others, burdens overgreat, unnecessary exactions, and rapacity.

With hospitality conflict inhospitality, frowardness, tenacity, inhumanity, niggardliness, prodigality, the ingratitude of guests.

With parsimony: profusions, tenacity, negligence and sloth in guarding one's own things.

<!-- p.158 -->

With ***αὐτάρκεια***, of which **Paul** speaketh, **1 Tim. 6**, conflict the desire of money, insatiableness, the itch of buying, avarice, overmuch carefulness, prodigality, the abuse of one's own things.

With gratitude conflict ingratitude, lying, pride, injustice, the forgetting of benefits, and contempt of the benefactor.

With sedulity conflict sloth, which doeth not the proper labours of its vocation, and ***πολυπραγμοσύνη*** (meddlesomeness), which omitteth necessary works, and thrusteth itself into the vocation of another, stirring and troubling many things without commandment.

### *Promissiones et comminationes septimi praecepti* · The Promises and Threatenings of the Seventh Commandment

**Luke 6.** *"Give, and it shall be given unto you."* **Proverb. 19.** *"He lendeth unto the Lord, who hath pity upon the needy."* **Isa. 33.** *"Woe unto thee that spoilest; shalt not thou thyself be spoiled in turn?"* **Habac. 2.** *"Because thou hast spoiled many nations, all they of the peoples shall spoil thee."* For a curse, an evil conscience, infamy, loss in goods, and other punishments, public and private, bodily and spiritual, temporal and eternal, are laid upon thieves and impostors; and *things ill gotten ill slip away.*[^C5-20]

## *De VIII. Praecepto* · On the Eighth Commandment

This commandment guardeth our good name, and requireth truth, because God is true. And the virtues of this commandment are:

- Truth.
- Simplicity.
- Candour.
- Constancy.
- Teachableness.
- <!-- p.159 --> Taciturnity.
- Eloquence.
- Courtesy or affability; and urbanity.
- Glory.
- Modesty.

**Truth** (*veritas*) is the virtue which inclineth the will, that we love true judgments, that is, such as agree with things as they are, and that we constantly speak, and do, and defend things true, right, plain and wholesome, and in our manners and in all our life flee insidious concealments and simulations.

**Simplicity** (*simplicitas*) is truth perspicuous and open, without wrappings and windings.

**Candour** (*candor*) is a virtue most near unto truth and simplicity, whereby namely we signify our own will truly and openly, and, so far as reason is probable, approve the wills of others, and bear no ill-will, and bend even certain things doubtfully said or done unto the better part, and hope well, neither conceive hatred toward others because of light suspicions.

**Constancy** (*constantia*) is the virtue which speaketh, doeth and defendeth things true, just and necessary constantly, that is, in the same manner and perpetually.

**Teachableness** (*docilitas*) is the virtue which bridleth minds, that they do not, led by arrogance or by self-love (*philautia*), stubbornly embrace false opinions, nor through admiration of their own wisdom despise the judgments of others, but that they hear others, learn, and, being convinced, yield and assent unto true judgments.

**Taciturnity** (*taciturnitas*) is the virtue which moderateth the use of the tongue in those things which it either doth not sufficiently know, or which are not necessary, or which hurt the neighbour and trouble quietness, so far as this may be done without violation of necessary truth.

**Eloquence** (*eloquentia*) is a peculiar gift, and a virtue, whereby the mind ruleth the will and the tongue, that it may speak things true, right, acceptable to God, and
<!-- p.160 -->
wholesome unto men, perspicuously, ornately, gravely and sweetly. **Courtesy** or affability (*comitas seu affabilitas*) is the virtue which inclineth us, that in speaking, in hearing, in answering, and in the performing of offices, in countenance and in gestures, we declare by a certain sweetness our goodwill toward others.

**Urbanity** (*urbanitas*) is the grace of truth, whereby, through things said merrily, or figuratively, or with salt, seasonably, truthfully and honestly, others are admonished, or are stirred up in due place, or are soothed.

**Glory** (*gloria*) is the inward approbation or testimony of a conscience that judgeth rightly, and of others that judge rightly concerning us, or concerning others.

**Modesty** (*modestia*) is the virtue which acknowledgeth its own infirmity, etc., as above in the Fourth Commandment.

### *Antithesis* · The Antithesis

With truth conflict all lies, that is, all opinions, speeches and gestures which agree not with the things themselves, and which hurt either the liar himself or others. But let us know that not every concealment is sin. For concealment is twofold, namely *insidious* and *officious*. The former ariseth from the desire of hurting, or from vanity, and is committed either in judgment, or out of judgment. In judgment, when either the plaintiff layeth a false accusation, or the defendant denieth those things which are truly objected, or the witness beareth testimony of things unknown or false, or the judge, corrupted by some affection, perverteth the true sentence: all which things, namely calumny or false accusation, lying defence, vain testimony, and the depraving of the law, conflict with truth, and are named insidious concealment. But out of judgment insidious concealment and false witness is committed in three manners, in mind, in tongue, and in deed: namely, when the mind cherisheth an evil suspicion of the neighbour, and doth not judge of him candidly, as was meet; and when the tongue teacheth perverse things, slandereth things rightly said and done, aggravateth the
<!-- p.161 -->
weakness of others, wresteth and interpreteth unto the worse part those things which are worthy of mitigation, taketh away honour from others, accuseth, and lieth in any manner whatsoever; likewise, when life, manners and pursuits bear hypocrisy before them, and lack open and natural candour. But officious concealment is a figure of speech, framed to cover certain things which it is not necessary should be laid open.

Antiquity hath thus distinguished: Of lies one is *pernicious*, another *jocose*, another *officious*. The **pernicious** lie is that whereby either the glory of God is obscured, or the neighbour is hurt, either in person, or in goods, or in good name. This lie is a mortal sin. The **jocose** lie is twofold. The one is born of vanity, boasting, flattery, wantonness, the lust of lying, and the desire of gain, without hurt of any man; as Thraso in **Terence** proclaimeth false things of himself out of mere vanity; the flatterers of Dionysius of Syracuse lick the spittle of the tyrant, and say that it is sweeter than honey; mountebanks and buffoons, ***καὶ εὐτράπελοι*** (and jesters), who study to stroke their hearers with vanities and levities. This lie neither becometh Christian godliness and gravity, nor guardeth a good conscience, nor hath the blessing of God, and it may also be a mortal sin. But the other is not against good manners, but for good manners, and serveth for the describing of things, as are dramas, and apologues, and the fables which, as it seemeth, we name *Aesopic* from Asaph. These, properly speaking, are not lies, but are images, figures and admonitions concerning good and true things, shown as it were in a picture, and they have a profitable end, and a moral ***ἐπιμύθιον*** (the lesson added to a fable), agreeing with the Decalogue, and not conflicting with charity. But the **officious** lie is that whereby those things are covered the confession whereof is not necessary, or which it is necessary to cover, lest we hurt others; as Rachel by craft hideth the idols; Michal feigneth that her husband is sick, and layeth an image in the bed; Rahab denieth that she hath Israelitish guests; **Athanasius** maketh the persecutors believe that he is not
<!-- p.162 -->
Athanasius. Hence is the rule: *He committeth no guile, who shutteth out fraud.* And, other things being equal, this also is true: *Guile or valour, who would ask in an enemy?*[^C5-21] And that saying of **Menander**: *It is better to choose a lie,* that is, a probable concealment, whereby namely, according to the commandment of God, a service is rendered unto the innocent, *than a pernicious truth.* For where a voluntary disclosing would be sin, there figures are allowed, or stratagems, or schemes of speech, which cover affairs. And those figures are not properly lies. For a lie properly is the depraving of that which ought to be said. And ironies, which have excuse in certain cases wherein we ought to profit the innocent, it is altogether lawful to use, yet so that an oath and the conscience be not violated. For the rest, unto pernicious lies, which conflict with truth, belong all false opinions, evil causes, evil and painted pretexts, and the whole of sophistry; likewise heresies, corruptions, unnecessary subtleties, calumnies, unjust sentences, and contumelious judgments, such as at this day also are those of the Theologians concerning one another; likewise perfidies, perjuries, frauds, impostures, ironies in the giving of counsels, backbiting, evil-speaking, vanity, boasting, courtly flattery, and hypocrisy of what sort soever; evasions in judgments, contracts, covenants, and in the whole life.

With simplicity conflict doubleness, ***διπλότης***, obscurity, perplexity, sophistry, ***πανουργία*** (craftiness), simulation, ***τὸ ἀκατάληπτον*** (that which cannot be laid hold of), courtly and political schemes lacking candour, and pernicious deceptions and persuasions.

With candour conflict calumnies, suspicions, distrust, and foolish credulity. With constancy conflict: inconstancy, uncertainty in doctrine, levity, stubbornness and perseverance in false opinions or actions.

With teachableness conflict stubbornness, dullness, security, negligence, and the Academic ***ἐποχή*** (suspension of judgment).

<!-- p.163 -->

With taciturnity conflict the dissembling of those things which ought, in the case of confession and in the defence of the truth, to be boldly spoken, or which were to be censured; likewise all the vices of an incontinent tongue, as garrulity, rashness, curiosity, evil-speaking, wantonness, vanity and boasting, vicious irony, ***δυσωπία*** and rusticity, or rustic shame, ***ἀκκισμός***, the feigning of shame and of humility, impudence, scurrility, obscenity, virulence, futility, insipidity, and flattery. With eloquence conflict unskilfulness, to which matter is wanting, words are not wanting; speechlessness (*infantia*), to which matter is at hand, words are wanting; garrulity, volubility, loquacity, plenty of words without good matter, or *loquentia*, as **Sallust** calleth it; impudence; speech obscure, monstrous, sordid and tedious; backbiting, ostentation, the abuse of the tongue.

With courtesy conflict levity, frowardness. With urbanity, scurrility, and rude and light ***εὐτραπελία*** (jesting); obscenity, contumely, a biting tongue, stolidity. With glory conflict levity and ambition. With modesty conflicteth impudence.

**Psalm 15.** *"He that speaketh truth in his heart, and backbiteth not his neighbour with his tongue, he shall dwell in thy tabernacle, O Lord."* **Luke 6.** *"Judge not, and ye shall not be judged."* **Psal. 5.** *"Thou shalt destroy all them that speak a lie."* **Isa. 5.** *"Woe unto you that call evil good, and good evil, putting darkness for light, and light for darkness."* **Proverb. 12.** *"Lying lips are an abomination unto God."*

## *De IX. Praecepto* · On the Ninth Commandment

This commandment, as also the tenth, is the interpretation of those that go before, and testifieth that in the Decalogue not only outward offences
<!-- p.164 -->
are forbidden, but that inward vices also are accused and condemned, as uncleanness of heart, vicious affections, depraved inclinations, concupiscence. And this is the meaning: Not only let thy hands abstain from the violation of thy neighbour, of his body, his things, his means and his goods, whether movable or immovable, but also let there be a perfect agreement in the whole nature, so that the heart or the will do not so much as desire to hurt another, and that all unjust desire and affection of hurting be absent, and that there be present a true, perfect, pure, perpetual love of God and of the neighbour, in no wise hindered and in no wise counterfeit, a true study of helping, defending and preserving the body and the goods of thy neighbour.

Here let there be considered the difference between political laws and the divine law; then, what is called *concupiscence* in the doctrine of the Church; afterwards, who is named *neighbour*.

Political laws command only concerning outward actions, and are a bridle, of what sort soever, whereby outward discipline is ruled. But the divine laws command concerning entire obedience, inward and outward, and accuse depraved affections, doubtings concerning God, the burnings of minds in unjust hatreds or in lusts, as hath been said more largely above, in the *locus* on the Law, out of the fifth chapter of **Matthew**.

Concupiscence in general signifieth the appetite of good or of evil things. The appetite of good things is not forbidden, as to desire meat, drink, marriage, and other necessary and profitable things in their order; as when Isaac desireth Rebecca, Samson Delilah. For appetites were created in nature, and would have been also in nature unfallen, as the love of God, ***στοργαί*** (natural affections), just anger, zeal, etc. But evil concupiscence is accused, which is an ***ἀταξία*** (disorder) of all the powers of man, which ought to have been conformable unto the law of God. The virtues of this commandment are:

- Original righteousness.
- The lawful desire of good.
- <!-- p.165 --> Perfect cleanness of the human heart.
- Perfect love of God and of the neighbour, or obedience toward the whole Law.
- Perpetual mortification, and repentance.

### *Antithesis* · The Antithesis

Original sin. Evil concupiscence, which is an ***ἀταξία*** of all the powers in this depraved nature. The appetite of the things of others. Assent unto desires, and the tasting of the same. Security and impenitence.

Now although concupiscence is forbidden in the former commandments also, yet a distinction is to be made between the fountain, which is here stopped, and the rivulets, which in the remaining commandments are severally pointed out, that they may be dammed up. Therefore also it is thus said, that here *passive* concupiscence, in the other commandments *active* concupiscence is forbidden. But concerning concupiscence it hath been spoken in the *locus* on sin.

Further, every man is named *neighbour* who needeth our work and help, and who is to be helped by us with heart, with mouth, and in very deed. And he is named neighbour by reason of creation; then, of likeness; afterwards, of common life and of human society. But Christian neighbours are bound unto us by a closer kindred: by reason of the mystical body; then, of regeneration; afterwards, of the common vocation of the godly unto the unity of the Spirit in the bond of peace; and lastly, by reason of the glory to come.

All these things we ought therefore to consider, that, the evil of our origin being perceived, we may acknowledge and bewail it, and may flee for refuge unto the Son of God, our Lord Jesus Christ, the common and only Physician, praying that he would purify us with his precious blood, and endue us with his Holy Spirit, that we may be ready and cheerful to perform our duties unto our neighbour.

## *De X. Praecepto* · On the Tenth Commandment

The meaning of this commandment is the same as that of the preceding one, save
<!-- p.166 -->
that the ninth commandment is rightly referred to the genus, but this to the species, and to the things contained. For the ninth forbiddeth all desire which conflicteth with charity, and by name, in general, mentioneth the house of the neighbour, or his possession and quietness, or the place wherein our neighbour is, and liveth with his household. But the tenth expresseth the things contained in the house of the neighbour, and commandeth that no man, upon any occasion, under any show whatsoever, draw the things of another unto himself.

For the rest, the promises and threatenings added to the Decalogue are often repeated. **Levit. 18.** *"He that doeth these things shall live in them."* **Deut. 27.** *"Cursed is he that continueth not in all things which are written in the law."* But let all temporal promises be understood with the exception of the cross and of chastisement; the threatenings also with the exception of repentance and of conversion unto God, according to the doctrine of the Gospel.

Let it suffice to have briefly pointed out these things concerning the Decalogue. Now something is to be added concerning the Apostles' Creed, which among the parts of catechetical doctrine holdeth the second place. And that we may the more conveniently, in the exposition of the Creed also, speak ***χρήσιμα παισί*** (things profitable for children), let these questions be always before the eyes of the boys, which we shall subjoin as an *Anacephalaeosis* (a summing up) and repetition of all the former things of which it hath been spoken hitherto. And this very *Anacephalaeosis* may serve in the place of a Catechesis.
