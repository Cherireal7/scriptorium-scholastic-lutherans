Next, Euphrosyne is gladness: for in giving and in requiting there ought to be gladness, without angling for a return (*sine captatione*).

**Hesiod**:

> ***Εὖ μὲν μετρεῖσθαι παρὰ γείτοτος, εὖ δ' ἀποδοῦναι***  
> ***Αὐτῷ τῷ μέτρῳ, καὶ λώϊον αἴκε δύνηαι.***  

Let a neighbour measure well, and let that which is owed be rendered to him again in equal measure, or in fuller. Hence a knuckle-bone, or die (*Astragalus seu talus*), is borne by this Grace: for benefits are to be given without angling for a return. And it often cometh to pass that he who giveth receiveth nothing thence again. For not all are thankful.

After that is Thalia, pleasure or budding (*germinatio*). For benefits ought to flourish among men and to be ever joined together, even as for that cause the Graces are painted linked hand in hand, and naked, because good offices ought to be free. And the myrtle, the more it is cut back, the more it flourisheth, and getteth larger leaves. By this picture is signified that benefits return in heaped measure to those who bestow the most. The more a man giveth, the more he findeth that more is rendered him from heaven, as the Germans godly say:

> *Allmosen geben, armet nicht,*  
> *Kirchen gehen, saumt nicht,*  
> *Unrecht gut, faselt nicht.*  

> Giving of alms maketh not poor,  
> Going to church maketh not late,  
> Ill-gotten goods do not thrive.  

Likewise: *"He lendeth unto the Lord that giveth to the needy."* And **Tiberius**, when he was rebuked for draining the treasury with alms, answered: That money would never be wanting to the King's exchequer, so long as there should be poor men to receive alms. But O our times! To-day the poor are compelled to give to the rich, and the rich in their turn give to the rich; unto the poor nothing returneth. But of these things thus far. Now something must be said of Covetousness.

<!-- p.262 -->

## *Avaritia* · Covetousness

Covetousness doth good to no man, and hath only ***κτήματα*** (possessions), but never ***χρήματα*** (things for use); or rather it hath neither ***κτήματα*** nor ***χρήματα***, because the covetous man is possessed, he possesseth not, neither knoweth he how to use his means; he is not a master, but a bondslave, and a body without a breast; he hath money without a man, a mistress of the man, and the very soul of his life, which being taken away he perisheth and dieth, and then at last doeth rightly, according to the little verse:

> The covetous man doeth nothing rightly, save when he dieth.  

This man, while he liveth, is sordid, as one who defraudeth both himself and others, and delivereth both soul and body to mammon, being never, whether waking or sleeping, without cares and without rapacity. From this most foul vice let these things dissuade us: first, the Commandment of God, **Matt. 6**: *"Lay not up for yourselves treasures upon earth."* **Luke 21**: *"Let not your hearts be overcharged with care for your living."* Secondly: Our coming forth from our mother's womb, whence, as **Job** saith, we came out naked. Likewise: The end of our life, out of which also we depart naked, carrying nothing away thence, save that we be filled with earth and dust, and be turned into earth, as it is wont most rightly to be said:

> Born of earth, he is nourished by earth, and moveth over the earth,  
> And, returned into earth, shall in the flesh rise again from earth.  

And that saying:

> Naked, thou fool, shalt thou be carried to the boats of hell.  

Well known also is the history which **Baptista Campofulgosus**, Duke of Genoa, relateth in book 7, in these words: *"Saladin, King of Asia, Syria and Egypt, declared that he was no less wise in his death than he had before been while living, in the conduct of affairs. For he commanded that the inner linen garment which he was wont to put on should be set upon a very long spear and carried through the camp, and that he who bore it should say with a loud voice unto all: That Saladin, the subduer of Asia, out of so great riches as he had gotten, carried away with him in death this one linen garment. For being wisely, though late, put in mind of human misery, he was minded in that manner to admonish others also."* Thirdly: The consideration of our nourishing in infancy, wherein God feedeth us with milk made ready in the breasts, without our own <!-- p.263 --> industry. Fourthly: Because every[^P13-1] covetous man is an idolater, and falleth into the snares of the Devil, and into destruction, **Ephes. 5**, **Coloss. 3**. Fifthly: Covetousness is the root of all evils, saith Paul, **1 Tim. 6**: *"We brought nothing into this world, neither shall we carry any thing out thence: and having food and raiment, let us be therewith content. For they that will be rich fall into temptation and into the snare of the Devil, and into many unprofitable and hurtful lusts, which drown men in destruction and perdition. For the root of all evils is covetousness."* And **Bernard**, in an elegant personation (*prosopopoeia*), painteth covetousness in this manner: The burning desire of having is the charioteer; the beasts over which the charioteer presideth are rapacity and niggardliness (*tenacitas*), which draw the chariot, that is, the mind of the man. The wheels of the chariot are four: faint-heartedness, inhumanity, contempt of God, and the forgetting of death (even as **Sallust** also saith: *"The lust of money and of dominion are, as it were, the matter of all evils: for covetousness overthrew faith, honesty, and the other good arts, and in their stead taught pride, cruelty, to neglect God, to hold all things for sale."*). And the whips which the charioteer useth to urge on the beasts that draw the chariot are the lust of getting and the fear of losing. Sixthly, let the punishments both in this life and in the life to come affright us, and let the examples be considered of Tantalus, Midas, Judas, Ananias, and others, and the squanderings of goods unjustly gotten, which seldom come down to the second degree, but vanish and are scattered in a wonderful manner.

**Lambert of Aschaffenburg** relateth that in the year 1066 the Provost of Goslar, having received the Bishopric of Meissen, died in this manner: When he had come to Goslar, after his repast he withdrew himself into the chamber where, no man being privy to it, he had buried his treasures, as though minded to rest a little while. But when, the day declining toward evening, he seemed to indulge in sleep beyond measure and custom, the chamberlains, wondering at the unwonted thing, began to knock at the door. But neither to them that knocked nor to them that cried aloud was any answer given. The doors therefore being broken open, <!-- p.264 --> they found the Bishop lifeless, lying upon his very treasures, of a foul colour, and with his neck broken.

Well known is the history of the maid of Frankfurt on the Oder, which befell in the year 1537. For she was possessed by the Devil, and for a whole month whatsoever she touched or took was turned into money, which she both shewed, and held in her hands, and devoured.

> Am I deceived? or, because all things are full of unwonted riot,  
> Doth this prodigious hunger of gold give warning?  
> And because, the word of God being despised, mortal breasts  
> Are wearied by the sole care of getting wealth?  

The Poets also set forth a little fable of a covetous man, who in the country had buried his money in the earth under a tree, with a stone set thereby as a marker of the place. Now when a certain other man, brought to extreme poverty, had betaken himself to that tree, taking a halter with him, that, being overcome with despair, he might hang himself, he perceived by chance — the stone being rolled nearer to the bough of the tree on which he would cast the halter — a hollow; and while he searcheth it, he findeth the treasure, and for joy casteth the halter into the place of the money, and, the stone being rolled back, goeth his way thence. Shortly after, that covetous Euclio, when he had come to look again upon his treasure and had seen it taken away, ended his life, for consternation of mind, with the halter which he found.

Here we shall put an end to the seventh Commandment, and shall pray God to grant us that we may have our measured portion of bread; that He give us neither beggary nor riches, but bestow only the things needful for our sustenance; lest haply, being full, we be enticed to deny, and say, Who is the Lord? or, being driven by want, we steal, and forswear the name of our God, **Prov. 30**.

AMEN.

# *Octavum Praeceptum* · The Eighth Commandment

### *Non dices falsum testimonium contra proximum tuum* · Thou Shalt Not Bear False Witness against Thy Neighbour

There is commanded the love of truth in the whole life, and the hatred of lying, of slanders, of flattery and of falsehood.

Now first it is to be considered in general what truth is, and what it is called. The Greeks name it ***ἀλήθειαν***, from ***α*** and ***λανθάνω*** (not, and I lie hid), because it hideth nothing, but both in the breast and in the mouth of a truthful man there is candour and simplicity, without paint and without doubleness. For such as the speech is, such is the mind and the heart.

Now the use of the word Truth is manifold. In the Sacred writings it sometimes signifieth the true knowledge of God, but that general and universal knowledge which ariseth from the beholding and consideration of the creatures, as **Rom. 1**: *"They hold the truth captive in unrighteousness,"* that is, the true knowledge of God, so far as it can be gathered from the contemplation of the creatures.

Sometimes it signifieth the true acknowledgment of God, and of the divine essence and will, as **John 1**: *"Grace and truth came by Jesus Christ,"* that is, the true acknowledgment of God, true righteousness and life. Sometimes it signifieth a kind of virtue, which is named Faithfulness in the offices owed to friendship, or in true good will, as it is said: *"He hath shewed mercy and truth unto me,"* that is, he was bountiful, and faithful or constant in true good will toward me.

Lastly, truth signifieth also the virtue of which it is chiefly spoken in this commandment, which is, namely, a firm purpose in the will, to will constantly to embrace true opinions, to keep covenants, and in speech and gesture to agree with the things themselves.

<!-- p.266 -->

To the loving and defending of this truth let God Himself move us, who is true, and a hater of lies, whose word is true and certain, and whose threatenings and promises are sure and firm. **Psalm 25**: *"All the paths of the Lord are mercy and truth."* Mercy is a benefit freely bestowed, with no regard of merits. Truth is the performing of the promises, which is done of debt, because God hath bound Himself to us. Mercy promiseth freely. Truth fulfilleth the promise. This mercy and truth lieth open unto all that seek out the covenant and the testimonies of God, that is, unto them that fear God, and rest in the truth. And it irketh me not to write down the most sweet words of **Augustine**, expounding the hundred and tenth Psalm and discoursing of this truth of God in this manner: *"The faithful God hath made Himself our debtor, not by receiving any thing from us, but by promising us so great things. The promise was a small matter: He willed also to be held by writing, making with us as it were a bond (chirographum) of His promises; and not only did He make a writing with men, that they might believe, but He also set a Mediator of His faithfulness, His only Son."* Next, let there move us to the defending of truth the immense difference whereby we ought to distinguish God and all the Angels and Saints from the Devil, who from the beginning is a liar and a murderer, the father of lying, that is, of false opinions concerning God, and of all sophistry. Thirdly, let us be lovers of truth, that the arts may be established and retained in the Churches, the Schools and the Commonwealths, lest we be Academics,[^P13-2] or authors and defenders of false opinions; but let us embrace the rules of certainty, the theoretical and practical principles, universal experience, and the syllogistic connexion, out of which a true consequence is built up. For these arts are the fountains of all good things in the common life, and ***ἀρχαὶ μεγάλων ἀρετῶν*** (the beginnings of great virtues),[^P13-3] as **Democritus** said; so that **Dürer** the painter most truly said to **Luther** and **Philip** that a man without the knowledge of the honest arts is as an unpolished mirror. Fourthly, that courts of judgement may be had, <!-- p.267 --> and that friendships, covenants and contracts, that is, the whole civil society, may be preserved; concerning which that saying is rightly spoken in this place: *"Among good men there ought to be good dealing."* But truth being removed, there cannot be good dealing. Fifthly, that we may retain faith, and call upon and fear God, and consider what, and how great a thing, hath been said: *"I am the way, the truth, and the life."* For without the love of truth we cannot call upon the true God. For we cannot say: *"Rule me by thy Holy Spirit, living, chaste and true, that I may truly acknowledge thee, the eternal Father of our Lord Jesus Christ, and Jesus Christ thy Son, and thy Holy Spirit; that I may truly believe thee, truly obey thee, give thanks unto thee, truly fear thee, rightly call upon thee, truly serve thee, and behold thee gracious in all eternity, etc."* Sixthly, that we may have regard to our good name, which, if it be good, is more precious than much riches, **Prov. 22**. A truthful man is deservedly preferred and honoured. A lying man is not to be trusted, in what authority soever he be. There are many who are strong in dignity, and have accustomed themselves beyond measure to lying speeches, either out of a certain boasting and ambition, or out of dulness. Such there are many everywhere, who take away all credit from themselves, so that it is very often said of them: *"All that this fellow uttereth is foul with lying."* Likewise: *"He never lieth, save when he speaketh."* **Aristotle** saith thus: *"Two are the works of a wise man: not to lie concerning the things which he knoweth, and to be able to lay open him that lieth."* Seventhly, that we may avoid the wrath of God and punishments. God is vehemently angry with liars, and punisheth them hardly, **Prov. 12**: *"Lying lips are abomination to the Lord."* And **19**: *"He that speaketh lies shall perish."* **Wisd. 1**: *"The mouth that belieth slayeth the soul."* And many such sayings there are everywhere in the Sacred writings. **Ambrose** saith thus: *"All that love a lie are sons of the Devil."* And **Augustine**: *"As God the Father begat the Son, the truth, so the Devil, being fallen, begat as it were a son, the lie."* Saul perisheth for lying. Ananias, **Acts 5**. Antiochus, **1 Macc. 6**. And most true is **Ps. 15**: *"Lord, who shall abide in thy tabernacle? He that speaketh the truth in his heart, etc."*

<!-- p.268 -->

**Objection.** But it is not good always to speak the truth, and oftentimes a man cometh into peril of life and goods. And the saying is well known: *"Truth begetteth hatred."* And **Menander**: *"It is better to choose a lie than a hurtful truth."*

**I answer.** Such things say they who are either flatterers, or covetous, and in sum ***ἄθεοι*** (godless men). Of these **Jerome** saith: *"Lying followeth the heaping up of riches. For the hand that is accustomed to lay up treasures possesseth a deceitful tongue: truth begetteth poverty, lying begetteth riches."* But let every man be mindful of his office, and know that a lie cannot stand, neither before God nor before men, and that a lying lip is a vessel of the Devil, who speaketh a lie of his own. Nay, the greater the peril is, the more let us speak forth the truth, according to the reason of our office and the rule of the divine word, in the case of confession, and in our calling. Let us have before our eyes the saying of **Plato**: ***Ἀλήθεια πάντων μὲν ἀγαθῶν θεῷ ἡγεῖται, πάντων δὲ ἀνθρώποις*** (Truth leadeth the way of all good things for God, and of all for men).

### *Quaestio: Estne omne mendacium prohibitum?* · Question: Is Every Lie Forbidden?

Three kinds of lies are commonly reckoned: the hurtful lie (*perniciosum*), the officious (*officiosum*) and the jesting (*iocosum*). Now the hurtful lie is that which is made with an unjust will to hurt, and it is mortal sin; or which, although not with a will to hurt, yet is made out of vanity of disposition, without a reasonable and honest cause, as are the lies of flatterers and boasters, or out of evil custom. Officious lies are when something is concealed for an honest and just cause, as when Abraham dissembleth that Sarah is his wife, and calleth her his sister. The Egyptian midwives excuse themselves with a lie, why they slay not the infants of the Hebrews, **Exod. 1**. So Rahab with a lie defendeth the spies, and Rahab would have sinned if she had of her own accord betrayed the spies, **Josh. 2**. And a voluntary <!-- p.269 --> disclosing would have been sin. But, to speak properly, this was not a lie. For a lie is properly a corrupting of that which ought to be said. In this case Rahab was asked concerning a matter of such sort as she ought not to tell. So it is lawful to hide an innocent man whom some tyrant is seeking. When Saul would tyrannically have slain David, Michal, the daughter of Saul and wife of David, when she saw that the house was already beset, delivereth David from the peril, letting him down through a window toward night, and laying an image in the bed, and answering the soldiers and assassins that David was sick, **1 Kings [1 Sam.] 15**. The woman in Bahurim, when David's messengers were in peril, let them down into a cistern, and covered it over with a cloth, and laid wheat upon it, as though she were drying wheat. When the enemies came, she saith that they are gone away. And there are many other like examples. David feigneth that he hath a disease, when he was come to a King that was his enemy. Judith deceiveth Holofernes. Physicians often deceive the sick, that they may help them. **Athanasius**, when Julian was persecuting him, and had sent a band and commanded him to be taken, went away by sea. His companions see that the pursuers are making haste; straightway Athanasius saith: *"Let us go to meet them."* This boldness deceiveth the pursuers. The pursuers ask whether they had seen Athanasius. They answer that he was left not far behind. These therefore hasten with all speed, that they might take elsewhere him whom, when set before their eyes, they had not seen. In such cases the Rule holdeth: *"He committeth no guile who shutteth out a fraud."* And the verse of **Menander** may be thus excused:

> ***Κρεῖττον δ' ἑλέσθαι ψεῦδος, ἢ ἀληθὲς κακόν.*** (It is better to choose a falsehood than an evil truth.)  

***Ψεῦδος*** (a falsehood), that is to say, ***καταχρηστικῶς*** (so called improperly): a concealment upon good reason, used in those things which it is not necessary to tell, nay, used for the sake of some profit; as Christ, talking with the two disciples, sheweth not Himself so as to be known forthwith, and doth this that He may shew His own power and their blindness, and stir up in them a greater longing and a greater faith, etc. And hither belong almost all sayings and doings which contain a figure in them.

<!-- p.270 -->

Jesting lies they call apologues, fables, poems and witty sayings. But these are not lies, but pictures of that which we mean. The wolf is the image of a tyrant; the little fox, of a crafty man. **Judg. 14**: *"If ye had not plowed with my heifer, ye had not found it out."* *"Out of the eater came forth meat, and out of the strong came forth sweetness."* Rightly saith **Augustine**: *"Whatsoever is done or said figuratively is not a lie."* And hither belong Proverbs also, as: Truth begetteth hatred, compliance begetteth friends. Time bringeth truth to light. The speech of truth is simple. A liar hath need to be mindful. A profitable lie. For a good cause three words — that is, as the Logicians speak, the major, the minor and the middle term. And Apophthegms, riddles, poems and witty sayings, which are not always to be taken as they sound; and that the Poets were, in their kind, the Prophets of the Gentiles, there is no doubt. And thus far of truth and lying in general. Now something must be said of truth in particular also.

Truth is to be considered in the order and estate of all men, that is, in the whole life:

I. In the Church.

II. In civil governments and courts of judgement.

III. In households.

IIII. In private life, or in the conversation of each man with others.

In the Church let there be the truth of the Law, let there be the truth of the Gospel, that is, of the whole Christian doctrine; let there be also the truth of life, as **Ambrose** saith: *"Not in false words only, but in feigned works also, is there a lie: for it is a lie to call oneself a Christian, and not to do the works of Christ; it is a lie to profess oneself a Bishop, a Priest or a Clerk, and to work things contrary to this order."*

The truth of the Law is, that it be told who and what manner of God He is, what original righteousness is, what is the cause of sin, what are the causes of calamities; or, that it may be more clearly expressed, Who and what manner of men we have <!-- p.271 --> been, Who and what manner of men we now are, Who and what manner of men we shall be. For these things are learned out of the Law.

Next, the truth of the Law consisteth in the reproving of sins without difference, and in the threatenings of the wrath of God, and of temporal and eternal punishments. For the minister of the divine word ought to rebuke sins in every order and estate, without respect of persons; which if he do not, he is compelled to render an account of his office evilly and perversely neglected. And let godly and learned Teachers (for of rude and rash bawlers we speak not) be called fathers, whom all the hearers should obey, as they are called in **Prov. 13**: *"A wise son suffereth himself to be corrected of his father: but a scorner obeyeth not."*

But the men of the world say: **I.** Our Preacher is choleric, and by nature wrathful. Therefore he cannot but rebuke those things which please him not. **II.** This man rebuketh the Magistrate, and giveth a handle to sedition. **III.** To what purpose is it? Even though the Magistrate be rebuked, yet the lords do what they will, as they are wont. **IIII.** By reproofs of this kind the minds of the Subjects are estranged from the Magistrate, which is an evil thing. **V.** The Preachers set one foot in the administration of the Commonwealth. **VI.** The text is to be handled, not superiors rebuked. **VII.** If these men knew any thing else to handle, they would not so inveigh against us and others. **VIII.** Men are not so to be terrified with threatenings, but are to be comforted. **IX.** In general, indeed, sins are to be reproved; but by name the Magistrate, or others, even though they persevere in manifest sins against conscience, and admit no admonition, whether gentle or even earnest, are not to be rebuked. And these and the like things say politic and secure men, who take it ill that the Holy Spirit judgeth of their vices and sins. **X.** Some are not so greatly angered, but say out of contempt: Let us e'en suffer the mass-priests to talk of what they can, and let us do what it shall please us to do. What are their revilings to us? And the mighty say: What of the priests?[^P13-4] They themselves <!-- p.272 --> also are yet our Subjects, not shut out from our dominion. But they who are more bitterly incensed against the preachers of truth say: If these men go on to be troublesome to us, we will drive them out, we will put a bridle upon them, we will take them away and kill them.

To the first objection we answer: although there ought to be a measure in rebukes, lest they proceed from private hatred and wrathfulness (as is wont to happen in many), yet we cannot be without rebukes, even as we cannot be without the other virtues; and these rebukes ought to flow from Christian zeal and fatherly good will, that the teachable may be stirred up to amendment.

To the second objection we answer, that the firebrand of sedition is not godly reproof proceeding from the office and the word of God, but rather silence toward vices and sins. For by their silence the Preachers heap up sins as well in the Magistrate as in the subjects; thence, by the permission of God, arise seditions, which are nothing else than punishments and the divine vengeance. And the authors of sedition are they who never repent, and hear not them that admonish them well.

To the third we answer: Let godly ministers be content with the office and commandment of God, and commend the success unto God. If the hearers will not obey, yet we have been salt, and have executed our office, and stand before God as faithful stewards; but the hearers can have no excuse.

To the fourth we answer: Why do these wise men speak with us of this matter? The matter, surely, is not ours, nor doth it hang upon us. Let them speak rather with Him who said: *"The Holy Spirit will reprove the world of sin."* Therefore He will not hold His peace, much less will He flatter, and say such things as please. If they will not endure to be rebuked, the Spirit will not cease, but will reprove them, until they perish. Moreover, those very men estrange minds from themselves by their own sins, by negligence, security, drunkenness, pillagings, and in other ways.

<!-- p.273 -->

To the remaining objections there is no need to answer, they being unworthy of rehearsal, much less of confutation. But let these four causes be before our eyes, why that truth of reproving sins is neither to be omitted nor to be held in hatred. The first cause is the Commandment of God, **2 Tim. 4**: *"Preach the word; whether in season, or at a time less convenient, reprove, threaten, admonish."* And let the words of **Ezek. 3** be read in this place: *"The word of the Lord came unto me, and said: Son of man, I have made thee a watchman over the house of Israel. Thou shalt hear the word at my mouth, and shalt give them warning in my stead, when I say unto the wicked, Thou shalt surely die. If thou do not this, the wicked man shall indeed die, but his blood will I require at thine hand, etc."* But altogether it must be seen to, that nothing be undertaken out of any private affection — a thing which at this day is wont to be done by many with great peril to the Church, who even with the thunderbolt of excommunication fill up and feed their own private affections.

The second cause is that by which the hearers ought to be moved to obey: that they know that godly ministers have a care of their salvation, and rebuke them for this end, that they may be converted and saved. David doth not acknowledge his fall, nor ask remission, before Nathan rebuketh him, and declareth to him his sin and the wrath of God.

The third cause is, that ministers are in the place of God, **Luke 10**: *"He that despiseth you despiseth me."* Solomon, **Prov. 29**: *"He that is stubborn shall suddenly perish."*

Let the fourth cause be the examples of those who have miserably perished, as the history of the Flood witnesseth. Likewise: Sodom, Jerusalem, Ahab, **1 Kings 18**, Herod, and others without number.

Now there is no doubt that godly and wise Magistrates and Princes are not offended by the truth, but love both it and the Teachers of it, even though they know that many things are said against themselves. **Theodoric of Verona** beheaded a certain Deacon who was a flatterer, and who changed his sermons to win the favour of Theodoric, whom he knew to be an Arian; and he said: <!-- p.274 --> *"If thou canst not keep faith with God, much less wilt thou be able to keep faith with me, who am a man."*

Liars, therefore, are all they who do not earnestly reprove sins, but either spare them and wink at them, or are altogether silent, or excuse things unjustly done; as at this day there are many such, especially in courts and cities; and many also are buried in the very same sins which they ought to rebuke: in drunkenness, in arrogance, in usuries, in covetousness, etc.

But many say: Truth, surely, hath place nowhere. And we are driven hither and thither, if we will speak nothing else but the truth. And the matter is now come to this, that he who would gladly be freed from any office and place should begin to speak the truth; for then he shall not easily find any man who will bear it with an even mind. Princes, Senators, Counsellors, Nobles, Citizens, Peasants are angry, and all would have painted ministers and preachers, who should say this and that, and keep silence of that and the other.

Let such thoughts depart from our minds, who seek the truth. And let us often think upon the fervent prayer of the Son of God: *"Father, sanctify them through thy truth: thy word is truth."*

But this also is to be added, that it must diligently be seen what is done with true judgement and sincere zeal, and with the study of godliness and love of the glory of God, truly, gravely, circumspectly and lawfully, and with some fruit, and what is done otherwise. For at this day the Sacramentarians and their followers, out of private affection, rashly babble out this and that, rebuke and condemn any thing whatsoever, and inveigh often against the absent, often against the good and innocent; they wrest and slander things rightly said and done, or at least not undertaken or begun with an evil mind and purpose; they cry out and bawl even to hoarseness, with strange gestures, as though mad, and rave and rage beyond measure, and are all but carried out of themselves, destitute of almost all sense; they suppose that they are all things, can do all things, ought to reform all things, that they are set in the highest place, and that all things are subject to them; that whatsoever smileth not upon them, or assenteth and applaudeth, is straightway anathema; <!-- p.275 --> but that whatsoever adoreth them is divine, and proceeded from a godly and holy zeal, although otherwise it be defiled with the foulest vileness. By this means and by this pretence of zeal, an exceeding wicked one, we have seen in these years, with great damage of the Churches, the Schools and the Commonwealths, the minds of many seduced, of Princes, of Nobles, and of others, especially of those whose judgements were not from tender years accustomed to the true knowledge of things; nor indeed hath that perverseness of restless men yet ceased, of whom truth itself assuredly denieth that they are studious of the glory of God and of wholesome concord. Many labour under a fault of nature, and swell with envy and spite, which flasheth openly out of their eyes; many are big with ambition and haughtiness; many by hypocrisy set off themselves and their most foul poison to others, especially to the simple and the ***μετεωρίζοντας*** (those of a wavering and unsettled mind) and the inconstant, and feign a zeal of such a sort as outwardly appeareth divine, but inwardly is in truth a fury, like the zeal of Medea tearing her infant in pieces, and of Nero crying out, ***ἐμοῦ θανόντος γαῖα μιγείη πυρί*** (when I am dead, let the earth be mingled with fire); many, out of desire of revenge, stir up this and that, and utterly destroy things most excellently built, pretending godliness. And seeing that this our age everywhere bringeth forth such men, or rather monsters, in great plenty, let us be the more diligent in our offices, and acknowledge our sins, and the punishments of sins, and let us bid farewell to the Erinyes and devilish furies, and pray God to have mercy upon us, and to rule us by His Holy Spirit, that in quietness and in hope we may think, speak, read, write and do things pleasing to Him and wholesome to the Church: let us not uncover the nakedness of Noah; let us not disturb wholesome concord and peace; let us not ascribe to ourselves that which belongeth to the Son of God, namely, the searching of hearts; let us not condemn men who have deserved well, and who godly and rightly call upon the Son of God; let us not make an Elephant out of every Fly; let us not arrogate to ourselves a title and name, as though we alone were they who cannot err, and with whom is the power of reforming all things that seem to offend our nostrils; let us not <!-- p.276 --> imitate swine, which with their snouts taste and devour dung only, even in the fairest gardens, passing by the violets and flowers; let us rather forgive one another our errors, if any have befallen; let one admonish another, as Christ commandeth; let us hear others who think rightly, and rest in the true declaration of words and phrases; and with mutual love — for the glory of God, and the safety of our faith and conscience, and for example's sake and the public tranquillity, and also for the sake of the judgement seat of the Son of God, before which we shall all be set — let us embrace one another, and resist our turbulent and violent affections: envy, ambition, madness, desire of revenge, and the like. Surely, if mutual love be wanting in us, as, alas, it is wanting, the mark of the whole divine image, and the certainty and rule of our faith, is in jeopardy, neither shall we ever be able to be called disciples of Christ, how much soever we arrogate to ourselves godliness, zeal, and the highest holiness.

Furthermore, the truth of the Gospel consisteth in the doctrine of the redemption of mankind, of Justification, of the use of the Sacraments, etc. And hence are shut out, as liars, all Workmongers or justiciaries (*Operarii seu iustitiarii*), and others, of whom John preacheth, **1 John 2**: *"Who is a liar but he that denieth that Jesus is the Christ?"*

The truth of life is comprehended in the words of **1 Tim. 3**, concerning the office of a Bishop, what manner of man he ought to be. And the verse of **D. Mathesius** is most worthy to be remembered:

> The fairest rule of virtues is the life of him that teacheth.  

Liars, therefore, are they who live otherwise than they teach, as covetous pastors, usurers, drunkards, slanderers, the proud, the arrogant, unjust censurers of others, the light, the rash, the mad, who verily with the one hand hold out bread to their hearers, and with the other a stone.

**Chrysostom** saith excellently: *"There are many Priests, and there are few. Many in name, few in deed and work. See ye how ye sit upon the seat of Moses: for the seat <!-- p.277 --> maketh not the Priest, but the Priest the seat; the place sanctifieth not the man, but the man the place; neither is every Priest holy, but every holy man is a Priest. He that sitteth well upon the seat receiveth honour from it; he that sitteth ill doth wrong to the seat. And therefore an evil Priest getteth from his priesthood a charge of guilt, not a dignity. For thou sittest in judgement: if thou live well and teach well, thou art a Judge; but if thou teach well and live ill, thou art the condemner of thyself alone. For by living well and teaching well thou instructest the people how it ought to live; but by teaching well and living ill thou instructest God how He ought to condemn thee."* But of these things thus far.

Truth, moreover, in civil governments pertaineth first to the lords themselves; next to the Counsellors; thirdly to the Judges and Governors. It belongeth to lords that they themselves be lovers of truth, truthful, and constant in leagues, in speeches, in honest promises, and in the whole life. Truth establisheth the throne; craftiness and fraud destroy the same.

In time past the Princes of Germany were eminent for this praise, that they were truthful, and had in heart, mouth and deed, *Yea, yea; Nay, nay.* At this day among Princes the styling one another brethren is usual, and great promises, many leagues, safe-conducts; but good faith is most rare, so that it may be said:

> No faith nor godliness have the men who follow camps,  
> And their hands are for sale; there is right, where the greatest wage is.  

Likewise:

> It is a kingly thing, believe me, to promise much;  
> But to keep faith is the work of clownishness.  

And because this is usual, hence have arisen proverbs very ***δύσφημα*** (ill-sounding): That the images of all good Princes may be graven upon one ring. Likewise: That a Prince is as rare a creature in heaven as is a stag in a poor man's kitchen. Likewise: That a Prince is seldom saved, unless he die in the cradle.

Many examples of such perfidy and lies have been found in the Roman Pontiffs deceiving the highest Rulers and Emperors. <!-- p.278 --> Likewise: In the Turks cruelly slaying captives and others. And above, in the second Commandment, we rehearsed an example of the perfidy of Hatto, Archbishop of Mainz. And the history of John Hus and Jerome of Prague — who, a safe-conduct being given, were cited to the Council of Constance in the year of Christ 1414, and not long after were condemned and burned with fire — is known from the acts of the Council, and from the Paralipomena added to the Chronicle of the Abbot of Ursperg. And experience also at this day supplieth too many examples. But at length that will come to pass which **Seneca** said:

> Where there is no shame,  
> Nor care of right,  
> No holiness, godliness, faith,  
> The kingdom is unstable.  

In the year of Christ 1056, Gunther, Chancellor of the Emperor Henry IIII, saw this vision: The Lord sitting upon the throne of His majesty, and, His arm being lifted up on high, brandishing a drawn sword with great force, and saying to them that stood by: *"I will render vengeance to mine enemies, and will reward them that hate me."* A little after, many Princes of the realm died; which destruction being fulfilled, he saw again the Lord sitting, and — the sword being now put back into the sheath and laid upon His knees — saying to them that stood by: *"A fire is kindled in mine anger, and shall burn unto the lowest hell."* Now there died at that time Hermann, Bishop of Cologne; the Emperor Henry III; Otto, Duke of the Swabians; Arnold, Bishop of Speyer; Conrad, the Emperor's son, Duke of Bavaria; the Count Palatine, slain by a certain Clerk of Bremen. And many others in one year.

And now within the space of two years there have died Charles V, Emperor; Christian, King of Denmark; Christiern, the exiled King of Denmark; Gustavus, King of Sweden; two Kings of France; three Queens, of England, of Hungary and of Poland; three Electors, of Trier, of Cologne and the Palatine; and many other illustrious Princes. And this is that which God saith: *"I will pour contempt <!-- p.279 --> upon Princes."* Likewise, **Ps. 82**: *"They suffer not themselves to be admonished; therefore all the foundations of the earth shall fall."* Now the foundations of the earth are threefold: First, cities, castles, strongholds, as **Isa. 24**: *"The windows of heaven are opened, and the foundations of the earth shall shake; the earth shall perish, and shall be overthrown."* Next there are the Laws and statutes. For by righteousness the throne is made firm, is founded and established, **Prov. 16**. After that there are the Princes and Magistrates themselves.

These things godly Princes ought to consider, and so much the more to be bent toward the truth. For when they are admonished of these things, they are admonished out of a faithful heart; which thing they ought to think upon, and therefore to love, not to hate, those who admonish them according to the voice of God. But if they hear not, let us reckon that all is verily lost, as at this day it cometh to pass everywhere.

Tyrants and ungodly men suffer not the truth to be told them, neither by faithful Counsellors, nor by godly ministers of the divine word, nor by others; as was shewn above, in the exposition of the fourth Commandment, concerning Cambyses slaying Prexaspes, and his wife, his own sister. And the same is shewn in a Fable of **Aesop**. A lion, a fox, a boar, a horse and an ass had taken three bulls. The lion biddeth the ass divide the prey. He cutteth the bulls into five equal parts, and giveth one to each of those who had hunted together. For truth also is simple, and the simplest men are lovers of truth, as is shewn in the Proverb: *"Children and fools for the most part speak out the truth."* The lion, being angered, teareth the ass in pieces, saying that more was due to himself than to the rest, because he excelled the others in strength, and had laboured more than the rest. This is the reward of simple truth. He therefore biddeth the horse make the division; who, being warned by the end of the ass, and thinking that somewhat more was due to the lion, assigneth to him a whole bull, and to each of the rest a half. These are they who, being warned by the fall of those who speak true things simply, are minded to spare the more worthy, and yet at times profess true things, as it were making a dispensation with the truth. But neither do these abide unharmed. For the lion seizeth the horse and rendeth him in pieces, no longer alleging his labours, but he saith that more <!-- p.280 --> honour is to be given to him, as to a King. At last he biddeth the fox divide the prey. Here also are the wresters of truth, who are wont to consider not the truth, but the examples and the ends of those who have spoken true things, and who learn to apply themselves to the manners of their superiors. The little fox heapeth together all the parts, and biddeth the lion carry away the whole prey. *"To thee,"* saith he, *"all is due, to us nothing. We are under thee, and we serve thee."* The fox, being asked whence he had learned this manner of dividing, answered: *"From these Doctors clothed in purple,"* that is, from the mangled dividers; signifying that tyrants rage against Judges, and Counsellors, and Preachers who serve not their lusts. Now the ass had kept the Arithmetical proportion, the horse the Geometrical, the fox none.

But let there be before the eyes of all godly Princes the causes by which they ought to be moved to truth, and which have been rehearsed above. And first of all let them consider the commandment and the glory of God. Truth is of God; lying is of the Devils. Next, let there be before their eyes the dignity of the office which they administer. They are Gods, that is, they bear a divine office, and are to be acknowledged as the vicars of God in this life, even as Nathan the Prophet also acknowledgeth David the King, **3 Kings [1 Kings] 1**. And therefore the people, **2 Kings [2 Sam.] 18**, saith unto David, when he would go forth together with them into the battle: *"Abide thou. For though we flee away, or die, yet will they not so greatly care for us. But thou art as ten thousand of us."*

### *Sed quae sunt praecipua obiecta veritatis politicae?* · But What Are the Chief Objects of Civil Truth?

The first is religion itself, or the Christian doctrine of the Law and of the Gospel: that, idolatrous worships being abolished, the word of God be purely taught, and true worships be instituted, **Isa. 1**: *"Hear the word of the Lord, ye Princes."* And so did Darius the Mede, the Emperor Constantine, Valentinian,[^P13-5] Theodosius, and others. And hither belong also the honest arts, which are the baskets <!-- p.281 --> (*cophini*) of the saving doctrine in the Schools and Churches. For it is needful that they also be rightly delivered; and those wits are not to be endured who judge it to be the highest praise of sharpness to be able to overthrow all things rightly said by shifts and slanders, to sow new and monstrous opinions, and to disparage and disallow the studies of Philosophy laid at the knees of Christ, as **Nazianzen** speaketh, and to feign an enthusiastic *theodidascalia* (a being taught immediately of God), rude and rash, barbarous, and entangled with monsters of opinions and labyrinths of disputations. And because at this day it is usual to curse the studies of Philosophy, and to traduce all who rightly, soberly, godly and in due place use Philosophical terms and similitudes for the explication and illustration of certain places in the heavenly doctrine, and so to call back anew into the Churches and Schools that old ***κυβείαν*** (sleight, dice-play) and barbarism, therefore, only for the sake of the studious youth, I will rehearse one or two sayings out of the Writings of godly antiquity, that it may be shewn by the way how far Philosophy is allowed to Christians. Now **Tertullian** saith thus in the book *Of the Resurrection of the Flesh*: *"It is indeed possible to be wise in the things of God out of the common notions also; but for a testimony of the true, not for a help of the false; that which is according to the divine ordering, not against it. For some things are known even by nature, as the immortality of the soul among many, as our God among all. I will use therefore also the sentence of Plato, pronouncing: 'Every soul is immortal.' I will use also the conscience of the people, calling to witness the God of Gods in these words: 'God seeth,' and 'I commend it to God.' But when they say, 'What is dead is dead,' 'Live while thou livest,' and 'After death all things are ended,' then will I remember both that the heart of the common people is by God accounted ashes, and that the very wisdom of the world is pronounced foolishness. Then, if a heretic also shall flee for refuge to the vices of the common people, or to the wits of the world, I will say to him: Why leanest thou upon a blind guide, if thou seest? Why art thou clothed by one that is naked, if thou hast put on Christ? Why usest thou another's shield, if thou hast been armed by the Apostle?"* Thus Tertullian. Now let us hear **Basil**, who, in the Oration on the profit of the Reading of the Heathen Writers, setteth against the despisers of Philosophy the examples of Moses and of Daniel, and among other things saith that Philosophy is like unto the leaf, wherewith, namely, the fruits are covered and adorned which are plucked from the doctrine of the Gospel. **Nazianzen** saith the same, and saith that he layeth the discourses of Philosophy prostrate upon the pavement of Christ, that they may give place to the word of the great God. I will add the verses, that they may be considered:

> ***Μοῦνον ἐμοὶ φίλον ἔσκε λόγων κλέος, οὓς συνάγειραν***  
> ***Ἀντολίη δύσις τε καὶ ἑλλάδος εὖχος ἀθῆναι,***  
> ***Τοῖς ἐπὶ πόλλ' ἐμόγησα πολὺν χρόνον, ἀλλὰ καὶ αὐτοὺς***  
> ***Πρηνέας ἐν δαπέδῳ χριστοῦ προπάροιθεν ἔθηκα,***  
> ***Εἴξαντας μεγάλοιο θεοῦ λόγῳ, ὅς ῥα καλύπτει***  
> ***Πάντα φρενὸς βροτέης στρεπτὸν πολυειδέα μῦθον.***  

That is, as **Budaeus** hath rendered it:

> Eloquence alone was dear to our heart, as much of it  
> As the Eastern band of the world, and the Western region  
> Together have gathered, and renowned Athens, the glory of the Greeks.  
> But so soon as at length it befell me to have gotten it with much labour,  
> I myself left it laid before the feet of Christ.  
> For to the word of the highest Godhead there yieldeth every  
> Changeful and shifting word of the human mind.
