# *Prima Pars Capitum Doctrinae Christianae, complectens explicationem Decalogi* · The First Part of the Heads of Christian Doctrine, containing the Exposition of the Decalogue

<!-- p.21 -->

## *Concio prima de Verbo Dei, et eius studio* · The First Sermon, of the Word of God, and of the Study thereof

### *Esaiae VIII.* · Isaiah VIII.

> *"If they shall say unto you, Enquire of them that have familiar spirits, and of the soothsayers, that chatter and dispute: (say ye) Doth not a people seek counsel of their God? are the dead to be consulted on behalf of the living? Nay rather, according to the law and the testimony. If they speak not this, they shall not see the morning dawn."*

Before we begin the heads of Doctrine, we will set before them a very brief admonition concerning these three things, which are always to be diligently considered by us, namely: I. Why the word of God is diligently to be heard and learned. II. Unto what the reading of the word of God ought to serve us. III. Where the word of God is rightly taught, and may rightly be learned.

### *De primo* · Of the First

Unto the hearing of the word of God these things ought to drive us: I. The most earnest and withal most gentle commandment of God. II. Next, because for this cause we were created and made men, and for this cause we live, that <!-- p.22 --> we may know God, as he hath revealed himself in his word. III. Afterward, our infirmity and misery without divine consolation and promise. IIII. Lastly, the punishments of them that neglect and despise the doctrine concerning God, and the rewards of them that are studious of the word.

I. God oftentimes commandeth us to hear, read, and learn his word, and to turn it to use, as **Deut. 13**: *"Ye shall walk after the Lord your God, and keep his commandments, and obey his voice."* **Deut. 30**: *"Let the Law of the Lord be always in thy mouth."* And Christ, **John 5**: *"Search the Scriptures."* **John 14**: *"He that loveth me keepeth my word."* **2 Tim. 3**: *"From a child thou hast known the holy Scriptures, which are able to make thee wise unto salvation, etc."* Above all, the voice of God the Father from heaven, bidding the Son to be heard, is to be thought upon, **Matt. 3**:

> This is my Son, the beloved and mine only delight,  
> In whom are the living joys of my soul.  
> Hear ye him; out of my bosom he bringeth forth whatsoever  
> Pertaineth to the sweet way of your salvation.  

The same speech **Moses** also expresseth, **Deut. 18**, saying: *"The Lord thy God will raise up unto thee a Prophet from the midst of thee, of thy brethren, like unto me; unto him ye shall hearken."*

II. Next, we were for this cause created, for this cause also redeemed, that we may learn to know God, and may worship and celebrate him; even as certain of the Heathen also, moved by the light of nature, have said: That all things were made for man's sake, but man was made for God's sake, that is, to know and to glorify God; in such wise that man now standeth as it were in the midst of all creatures, a Prince and Lord, that he may enjoy their service, and himself render his own service unto God; and both are done for the profit of man, whether he be served, or whether himself serve, as **Paul** saith, **1 Cor. 3**: *"All things are yours,"* whether the things that are above us, or the things that are equal to us, or the things that are beneath us. The things above us are ours to enjoy, as God himself, whom we know and worship. The things equal to us are ours to live together with, as the Angels, <!-- p.23 --> who are to live with us for ever, and meanwhile in this life are our companions and keepers. Lastly, the things beneath us are ours to serve us, as are all the creatures made subject unto us and unto our use. The Redemption also of mankind was wrought for this cause, that we might be brought back to the true knowledge of God. We live likewise for this cause, that first we may learn the true doctrine concerning God; next that we may exercise it, and confirm it by use; afterward that we may study to propagate it unto posterity, according to the saying: *"I shall not die, but live, and declare the works of the Lord."* For this cause also a long life is to be asked of God, that we may perform these three things, without which life is not to be wished for.

III.[^P01-1] Afterward, let our infirmity move us to the study of the true doctrine concerning God; we who verily without the word of God are nothing, know nothing, avail nothing; nay, we ought not so much as to wish that we were men, unless the divine promise did comfort us, and affirm that we are the sons of God. He that considereth this cause can, casting away security, live in the fear of God. And of this preacheth **Ps. 90**, which ought to be most familiar unto all: *"Lord, thou hast been made our refuge, from generation to generation, etc."*

IIII. Lastly, let both the punishments and the rewards be considered. *"He that believeth not is condemned already."* *"He that abideth not in the doctrine of Christ, hath not God."* *"To the law and to the testimony: if they speak not according to this word, they shall not have the dawn."* And **Daniel 12**: *"The teachers shall shine as the brightness of the firmament; and they that instruct many unto righteousness shall glitter as the stars unto all eternity."* And hither pertain the most sweet promises of Christ: *"He that keepeth my word, or he that believeth in me, shall live, and shall never see death, etc."*

### *De secundo* · Of the Second

The reading of the word of God serveth us for the knowing both of the essence and of the will of God, that is, for the glory of God, and for our salvation. We all by nature desire both to know and to see <!-- p.24 --> God, and we all carry about some natural and Philosophical and universal knowledge, that God is an eternal mind, the cause of good in nature, as **Plato** hath said: the whole workmanship of the world also testifieth that there is a God, whether we behold the heaven or the elements;

> And slight is the sod that proveth there is a God.  

We all desire also stability of life, and immortality, and we judge that the mind of man, enlightened with so many notions and gifts, was not born unto destruction. But nothing certain, nothing firm, are we able to say and determine concerning either of these our desires, unless we take up this sentence: *"Thy word is a lamp unto my feet, and thy word a light unto my paths."* Out of the word of God we learn both the essence and the will of God, which otherwise the whole world knoweth not. The essence is one, in three distinct persons, the Father, the Son, and the holy Spirit. The will of God is expressed in the Law and the Gospel, **John 6**: *"This is the will of the Father, etc."* Worthy therefore of remembrance is the saying of **Irenaeus**: *"Forasmuch as it was impossible without God to learn God, by his Word he teacheth men to know God."* And I add unto this also the notable sentence of **Hilary**: *"That alone do we understand to be well believed concerning God, unto the believing whereof concerning himself he himself hath stood forth unto himself both witness and author."*

### *De tertio* · Of the Third

The word of God is rightly taught and learned where the Law and the testimony of God is heard, that is, where the Prophetical and Apostolical books are aptly and godly expounded, or, to speak more plainly, where the true Church is, in which is set forth the true doctrine concerning God and the Redemption of men, with the lawful use of the Sacraments adjoined. Of this third member speaketh **Isaiah**: *"To the law, and to the testimony."* For he comprehendeth the two chief parts of Christian doctrine, the Law of God, and the doctrine of the Gospel. Where these two are rightly delivered, there is the true Church, there shineth the dawn, the dayspring from on high, Christ, the brightness of the Father, light of light. Where these two are not, there the dawn is absent, and there lie beneath darkness, hell, death, the devil and <!-- p.25 --> lying. Therefore let us love the Law and the testimony, and let us often think upon that fervent prayer of Christ, **John 17**: *"Sanctify them in thy truth: thy word is truth."* This hath the truth itself spoken, to wit, Christ, who saith of himself: *"I am the way, the truth, and the life"*; who is the Word of his Father, to whom be praise and honour unto all eternity, Amen.

## *Unde sciri possit, Doctrinam Christianam esse certam* · Whence it may be known that the Christian Doctrine is certain

In all men since the fall there is inborn a doubting concerning the essence and will of God, and especially concerning the word of God, whether this be certain. Therefore minds are to be fortified against doubtings, and the causes of certainty in Christian doctrine are to be held fast.

The first and chief cause of certainty is the divine authority and revelation, that is, God himself, who knoweth not how to lie and to deceive. For the author concerning God is God himself, that is, God hath revealed and doth reveal himself by the Word of his Son, who is ***λόγος*** (the Word), and this same word by the power of his Spirit he breatheth into godly minds, as **Lucian**, presbyter of Antioch, said unto the Governor of Antioch. For being asked why he, a man that had obtained praise and admiration for wisdom, would make himself partaker of that sect which was repugnant to the reason of all men, that is, the Christian, he answered, That the author of the doctrine concerning God is God himself, whom it is not lawful to withstand. Next there are the miracles proper to the divine nature, as the Raising of the dead, the going back and the staying of the course of the Sun, the eclipse of the Sun in the Passion of Christ, etc.

These things also are to be considered: the universal experience of the godly in invocation and in the exercises of repentance. The antiquity of the doctrine. The event of the prophecies. The preservation of the Church, and the continued succession of the teachers of the doctrine, even from the beginning of mankind. The hatred of the devil against this doctrine. Lastly, the very kind of the doctrine, laying open things secret and unknown to human <!-- p.26 --> reason. But of these things we have treated more largely in the *Institutions*.

## *Quid sit Catechesis* · What Catechesis is

The word *Catechesis* or *Catechism* signifieth properly a sound sent forth and a sound given back, *red und antwort* (speech and answer); for ***κατηχέω*** is "I instruct," in such wise that the things which have been said are to be required again of the hearer. For Antiquity kept this custom, that by set examinations and dialogues it required again of the hearers the things dictated; and especially in the primitive Church examinations were appointed, in which the catechumens, before they were received into the Church by Baptism, were wont to testify or confess that they embraced the true doctrine concerning Christ. Hence also the sureties, or godfathers, or witnesses of Baptism were named ***κατηχησταὶ*** (catechists),[^P01-2] as they who answered unto the questions in the place of them that were to be baptized, or rather, who instructed the catechumens: ***ἠχώ*** is "a sound," and ***ἠχεῖν*** "to sound." And the Hebrews also observed this custom, that they delivered the sacred instructions from hand to hand, and did not commit them to writing. Whence also *Cabala*, that is, the doctrine concerning God, was that more secret instruction called, which **Ezra** is said first to have reduced to writing. For the Cabala among the Fathers was not that foolish transposing of letters, but it contained sentences which the godly delivered from hand to hand unto their posterity, which were not open in the Law, as, that the Law doth not justify, etc. And rightly do they say that the Cabala was received on the mount, because it was the doctrine of the holy Spirit; from *Kibel*, "he received." Thence they said *Cabala*, as it were a doctrine delivered from hand to hand, and received from the Fathers. Hence also they will have the name of the *Sibyl* to be derived.

But the word *Catechism* is altogether Ecclesiastical, and not well known to the Heathen Writers. **Paul** useth it several times, and chiefly is the place **Gal. 6** to be observed: *"Let him <!-- p.27 --> that is catechized communicate unto him that catechizeth him in all good things."* Now we call Catechism in the Church nothing else than the childlike, simple, categorical and methodical, perspicuous and briefly comprehended doctrine concerning God, which is delivered by the living voice unto the hearers, plainly and distributed in order, in such wise that the hearers in their turn may be able to give back and recite the things which they have learned. And let us give thanks unto God, that the doctrine which once was secret, and therefore was called Cabala among the Fathers, is now made a children's doctrine, known unto all that are in the assembly of Christians.

## *Partes Catechismi* · The Parts of the Catechism

Most simply the doctrine of the Catechism is distributed into these five heads: into the Decalogue, into the Apostles' Creed, into the Lord's Prayer, into the Sacrament of Baptism, and into the Sacrament of the Lord's Supper. The reason of the order is the whole sonship (*filiatio*), whereby we are made the sons of God. God declareth himself to be our Father in his Law, wherein are prescribed unto us the duties which it becometh us as Sons to render unto God. We in our turn acknowledge and confess in the Creed, that God is our Father, Redeemer, and Sanctifier. And as Sons in their necessities implore the help of their Father, so we also do the same in the Lord's Prayer. And that we may be certain that we are not forsaken of God, we have the Sacraments, the seals of our faith. These things are worthy of meditation. For these things pertain unto our sonship, namely:

That he is a Father, the Decalogue showeth.

That God is acknowledged as Father, the Creed.

To implore the help of the Father, the Lord's Prayer.

To be certain of help and deliverance, the Sacraments.

In earthly parents also this image is expressed, that Fathers declare themselves in very deed to be Fathers, and the children <!-- p.28 --> acknowledge and reverence them, and are certain of their ***στοργῇ*** (natural affection), help and service.

Now God declareth himself to be our Father in these three things: that he created us, when we were nothing, that is, gave us life; that he redeemed us, when we had fallen out of life; that he sustaineth and strengtheneth us, whereas we are faint, and cannot stand firmly. This is the work of the holy Spirit in us. We in our turn acknowledge and confess this God as Father in our Creed. And when anything is wanting unto us, we ask of him counsel and help, and are certain of true deliverance, that is, of eternal salvation.

Of these things it is elsewhere said more briefly in the Schools: namely, that the whole Christian doctrine is distributed into the Law and into the Gospel, or, as **Isaiah** speaketh, into the Law and into the Testimony.

The whole doctrine of the Gospel is a testimony, whereby God testifieth that he is gracious unto us who are converted unto the Son. *"As I live,"* saith he, *"I will not the death of a sinner, but that he be converted, and live."* He testifieth of the Son: *"I will raise up unto you a Prophet."* *"This is my beloved Son."* The Son of God testifieth of the Father: *"The Father hath sent me, he hath anointed me with the Spirit to preach the Gospel."* *"God so loved the world, etc."* The holy Spirit testifieth in the Church, and in the hearts of them that believe, that there is none other name whereby we may be saved, but the name of Christ Jesus. Let us therefore be bound unto the Law and the testimony, and let us not regard other disputers, sophists, contentious men and soothsayers, such as this our age hath brought forth very many in the Church.

Moreover, the order of the parts of our Catechism the Apostle **John** also hath set down, **1 John 5**, when he saith: *"There are three that bear witness in earth, the spirit, the water, and the blood: and these three are one."*

The Spirit comprehendeth the Law, the Gospel and Prayer. For he is the holy Spirit of grace and of supplications, according to the promise: *"I will pour upon you the Spirit of grace and of supplications."* Now the grace of the Gospel is that which nevertheless without the Law <!-- p.29 --> cannot be known. For grace is opposed to wrath, as health to sickness. Wrath and sickness are discovered out of the Law, grace and healing out of the Gospel. The Spirit of supplications is that whereby we come unto the throne of the divine majesty, saying: *"Abba, Father."* Likewise: *"Our Father which art in heaven."* The water and the blood are the two Sacraments, of Baptism and of the Lord's Supper.

## *De discrimine Legis et Evangelii* · Of the Distinction of the Law and the Gospel

Here we must speak of the distinction of the Law and the Gospel. Now the first distinction is taken from the form of the knowledge. For the Law which is commonly named Moral, as shall afterward be said, is known by nature, as **Paul** showeth, **Rom. 1** and **2**, where this place is especially to be observed: *"Men hold the truth captive in unrighteousness,"* that is, they corrupt the true notions concerning God and concerning the Law by disobedience: the appetitive part doth not obey the knowing part: against the judgement in the knowing faculty the will and the heart rush into foul wickednesses, as **Medea** also saith:

> I see the better things and approve them, I follow the worse.  

There shine in the knowing part the notions of the law, divinely implanted, and affirming that there is a God, wise, good, an avenger of wickednesses, and they are also the rule of an honest life: yet in the hearts there is a turning away from God, and concupiscence, which when it conceiveth bringeth forth sin, as **James** speaketh.

Here fall in these Questions: Whether the Law entire be known unto nature, without the word of God. Whether the Law be possible, seeing it is most of all according to nature. Why so many furies and wickednesses of men are done against the Law, whereas yet the judgement in the knowing part counselleth otherwise. Concerning these Questions it is necessary that the youth be rightly instructed.

Concerning the first Question we answer thus: That the notions of the entire Law were known unto nature entire (*natura integra*), according to obedience <!-- p.30 --> inward and outward. But depraved nature,[^P01-3] without the true Church, that is, not instructed by the word of God, knoweth only a small portion of the Law, concerning certain outward and civil duties; it knoweth not the entire Law concerning inward obedience, and concerning the wrath of the true God against sin, even as **Paul** also saith: *"I had not known that concupiscence is sin, except the Law had said, Thou shalt not covet."* For even in the knowing part there is a darkness that knoweth not God, and distrust, and doubting concerning providence.

Concerning the second Question we answer most briefly, that natural things are possible unto nature entire, not depraved, that is to say, unto that nature whereof they are the proper things. But seeing nature is depraved, although the Law be according to nature, yet it is not possible, by reason of the stubbornness of the will resisting the judgement of right reason. For in the will and the heart there is stubbornness, although in the knowing faculty the knowledge of the Law be born with us, even as the understanding of numbers. And in this question there falleth in also this special Question, Why God commandeth things impossible; whether God be a tyrant, commanding us those things which cannot be performed? To which question we answer simply, That God commandeth those things which are conformable to his own will, just, wise, chaste, and entire. And seeing he created human nature conformable to his will, or, as the Scripture speaketh, after his own image, he would not command things alien from this conformity, but he put his Law into human nature, as the rule of a life agreeing with the will of God. But when nature was depraved, the Law became impossible, not in respect of God, who planted the Law in human nature, but in respect of human nature, rushing against God who planted his Law in nature. Wherefore the Law is impossible, not unto nature entire, which God created, but unto nature depraved, that is, turned away from God by the craft of the devil, and by the will of man. **Plato** also in the *Protagoras* moveth almost a like question:

> He is a tyrant that compelleth against nature.  
> The Law compelleth against nature.  
> Therefore the Law is a tyrant.

But to the minor it is answered: I deny it of the natural judgement, or of the knowing <!-- p.31 --> part. Likewise:

> That is a tyrant which destroyeth nature.  
> The Law destroyeth nature.  
> Therefore the Law is a tyrant.

That which destroyeth nature, to wit, just nature, is a tyrant. But the Law destroyeth unjust nature, as when it punisheth the manslayer by the law of retaliation. But here let us bewail the greatness of human infirmity, seeing there is so great a discord of two chief and kindred powers in man. Let us consider also the counsel of God, why he would have the knowledge of the Law to remain in us, namely, that the knowledge may be a testimony concerning God, and that it may reprove sin, and that it may be the governess of outward discipline. We come to the third question, which is moved by them that deny the divine Laws to be natural, seeing they have so often been changed, with this argument:

> Natural things are unchangeable.  
> The divine Laws have been changed, as against the first table many deities have in all times been feigned, and against the second table many things have been permitted concerning compacts of adulteries, manslaughters, and other things unspeakable.  
> Therefore those Laws are not natural.

First we answer to the argument, denying the minor. For those Laws have not been changed, as touching the knowing part. And as the knowledge of numbers always shineth in the minds of men, so there remaineth the true knowledge, that one God is to be called upon, that there are not many Gods, that a man is not to be slain for sport, that wandering lusts are not to be approved. Hence so many excellent writings of the Heathen concerning the virtues are extant. Hence **Xenophon**, **Aristotle**, **Cicero**, and other wise men disallow a plurality of Gods. ***οὐκ ἀγαθὸν πολυκοιρανίη, εἷς κοίρανος ἔστω*** (the rule of many is not good; let there be one ruler), saith **Aristotle** against the crowd of Gods. And although in this human darkness this light shineth less clearly in the minds of men, yet it cannot utterly be put out. And the wickednesses that are done against the Law do not prove that the Law is not natural, but they show our infirmity and malice, which is to be acknowledged and bewailed; that even against the judgement of reason, which in outward discipline exhorteth unto the best things, seeing it is a ray of the divine wisdom implanted in the mind of man, the will and the heart rush into foul wickednesses, as when the will among the Romans received the slaughters of gladiators, against the judgement of the mind. And the <!-- p.32 --> Lacedaemonians permitted compacts concerning adulteries, and the Germans and others at this day permit usuries and riotous gluttonies. And hereunto is added the cruelty[^P01-5] of the Devils, driving hearts unto horrible furies, in such wise that the darkness also in the knowing part overwhelmeth the judgement. Hence madmen rage against themselves, and against their own. **Hercules** slayeth his wife and his sons.

In the history of the Xanthians a mother hangeth herself from the roof, bearing her son hanged from her neck, and holding a torch wherewith to set the house on fire. And many horrible things doth our own age show. So do the Devils drive nature forsaken of God. Wherefore, being admonished by these tragical examples, whereof scarce any house is void, let us ask governance of the Son of God, who came that he might destroy the works of the Devil, that the light of judgement be not darkened in us, but that it may be polished by his blood, and cleansed. Let us also often think upon and repeat this prayer: *"Lead us not into temptation."* Let it suffice to have admonished these things concerning the divine Law, that it is, and how far it is, naturally known unto us. But the Gospel is not known by nature unto any creature, but was brought forth by the Son of God out of the bosom of the eternal Father, **John 1**, and, as **Isaiah** speaketh, it is a testimony bound up and sealed, that is, not known by nature. Of this property of the Gospel speaketh **Paul**, **1 Cor. 2**, when he saith: *"We speak the wisdom of God in a mystery."* But of these things enough.

The second distinction of the Law and the Gospel is taken from the matter. For the Law is a doctrine enjoining, commanding, accusing, condemning. But the Gospel principally promiseth Remission of sins and life eternal, through and for the sake of Christ, and pronounceth us to be perfect in Christ by the imputation of the perfection of Christ, and by the beginning of new obedience, or the firstfruits of the holy Spirit, but not yet by entire consummation.

The third distinction of the Law and the Gospel is taken from the form of the promises. For the Law hath conditional promises: *"If thou do these things, thou shalt live in them; if thou do them not, thou shalt surely die."* This is the condition of perfect obedience performed, without <!-- p.33 --> pardon, in such wise that even though the sinner repent, yet by the Law he cannot any more be received into favour, but is utterly accursed according to the sentence of the Law, albeit a bare contrition ariseth out of the Law showing sin. But the Gospel hath free promises, that is, it promiseth remission of sins and life eternal freely, without our works and merits, for the sole obedience of Christ, unto all that turn unto Christ, as God saith: *"As I live, I will not the death of a sinner, but that he be converted, and live."* Likewise: *"He that believeth on the Son hath everlasting life."* Than this doctrine there is none more ample, none more pleasant, none more excellent. Unto God be praise and honour. Now there ariseth out of this distinction that kindred one also, That the Law only accuseth and condemneth sin, and all men by reason of sin, and doth not so much as admit any conversion, but simply condemneth the disobedient with eternal death. But that the Gospel freely for Christ's sake giveth unto them that believe remission, and life eternal. These things both ought and may be set forth in a copious discourse. And that this fourth distinction, taken from the form of the threatenings, might be made very plain, God in the promulgation of the Law willed that there should be thunders, lightnings and clouds, a trumpet also exceeding loud and framed to the terror of the whole people, and a thick smoke, signifying that by the voice of the Law man is terrified: but in the promulgation of the Gospel he used a fatherly and sweet voice: *"This is my beloved Son, in whom I wholly rest; hear ye him."* Hence in the Scripture the Law is a flaming sword, forbidding that any of sinners should come unto life, **Genesis 3**. Likewise: hard tables and of stone, to signify the outward and the inward, both obedience and condemnation, **Exod. 34**, **Deut. 6**; a very great fire, **Deut. 18**; the yoke of burden, the staff of the shoulder, the rod of the oppressor, **Isaiah 9**; the band of the neck, **Isaiah 52**; a hammer that breaketh in pieces, **Jerem. 23**; a yoke of iron, **Jeremiah 28**, **Deut. 28**. But the Gospel is the rod of the Kingdom of God, **Ps. 110**; a great Light, **Isaiah 9**; the horn of salvation, the virtue and power of God, <!-- p.34 --> the bread of life, living water, the ministration of the Spirit, not of death, **2 Corinth. 3**, and it offereth pardon unto them that by faith flee unto Christ.

The fifth difference of the Law and the Gospel is taken from the effects. The Law worketh among us wrath, terrors and torments, and is the knowledge of sin, and oftentimes casteth a man into doubtings and despairs. But the Gospel raiseth up minds and consciences, comforteth, healeth, and is the power of God unto salvation to every one that believeth.

The sixth difference is taken from the objects. The Law is to be set forth principally unto the unrighteous, for whom it is made, **1 Timoth. 1**, unto Epicures and hypocrites, the secure and the proud. But the Gospel is set forth unto them that repent, and that tremble with the feeling of the wrath of God against sins, and with the fear of eternal damnation, according to that saying: *"Come unto me, all ye that labour and are heavy laden."*

## *Divisio Legis Divinae* · The Division of the Divine Law

In the holy Scriptures diligent heed is to be given unto the word *Law*, where the Decalogue, where the ceremonies, where the judicial ordinances are understood. Therefore it is usual to say that one Law is Moral, which is the Decalogue; that other Laws are ceremonial; that others, lastly, are judicial (*forenses*). The Moral Law is the Decalogue itself, which God on mount Sinai by his own voice, amid thunders and lightnings, promulgated, in the year of the world 2454. In this year of Christ 1559 there are passed since the giving of the Law 3607 years.[^P01-4]

Here by the way let the words be considered. *Sinai* signifieth a thorn-brake. And the mount is situated in Arabia Petraea, which hath its name from the smelling out or the expounding of dreams and riddles. For truly the Law, the tables whereof are of stone, setteth riddles before us, and containeth the figures and types of the Gospel, and cannot bring us unto true waking, or life, but overwhelmeth us with heavy sleep and terrible dreams. All these things pertain to the vehemency of the Law.

<!-- p.35 -->

Many godly men also reason that the thunders, which are wont still to be, are admonitions both of the past and of the future: of the past, namely, the Law that was given; of the future, the last judgement, wherein the Son of God shall come to judge the quick and the dead. But *Sion* signifieth a watch-tower or a sign, *ein Schawbergk* (a look-out hill): the mount of the city of Jerusalem, upon which the stronghold of David was placed, of which it is said: *"Out of Sion shall the Law go forth."* Hither are the promises of the Gospel referred. And the explication of these words illustrateth the distinction of the Law and the Gospel.

The Moral Law therefore, or the Decalogue, is the eternal and unmoved wisdom and rule of righteousness in God, discerning things right and not right, and horribly angry with the stubborn, revealed unto men in the Creation, and afterward often repeated, and declared by the divine voice, that we may know that God is, and of what manner he is; binding all reasonable creatures, either unto obedience, or unto punishment or damnation, unless there be made remission and reconciliation for the sake of Christ the Mediator.

The ceremonial Laws ordered the outward gestures in the sacrifices, and distinguished places, times and persons, and described certain other outward exercises, that they might be signs and distinctions of the Mosaical polity from other nations, *Kirchenordnung* (a church order).

The judicial Laws are the Laws concerning judgements and magistrates, concerning outward discipline, concerning punishments, and the whole political order.

## *De abrogatione Legis Mosaicae* · Of the Abrogation of the Mosaical Law

I. As touching justification, all the parts of the Law avail nothing, **Rom. 3**: *"By the works of the Law shall no man be justified."*

II. As touching obedience, only the ceremonial and judicial Laws are abolished: but the Moral Law remaineth unmoved, **Rom. 3**: *"Through faith we establish the Law."* And the Son of God himself saith: *"I am not come to destroy the Law."*

<!-- p.36 -->

III. As touching condemnation, the curse of the Decalogue is abolished in them that believe, according to the saying: *"There is now no condemnation to them which walk in Christ Jesus."* Therefore it is wont commonly to be said: We are no more under the Law, although we be in the Law; that is, the Law doth not condemn us who believe on the Son of God, although we owe obedience unto it.

**But it is asked, why the ceremonial and judicial Laws, as touching the species (for the Genus always remaineth), may be utterly omitted?** We answer, Because they were promulgated only for a certain time, so long as the Mosaical polity should endure. The polity therefore being fallen, it was necessary that the Laws also should cease. For the Mosaical polity was ordained for this cause, that there might be a certain place and a certain people, wherein the Messiah should be born. When therefore the Messiah was born, the polity perished, according to the prophecy: *"The sceptre shall not be taken away from Judah, until Shiloh come,"* the promised Messiah.

## *De usu Legis Moralis* · Of the Use of the Moral Law

**Seeing the Moral Law cannot be blotted out, and yet cannot be fulfilled by us, it is asked, what is the use thereof?**

**I answer.** The use is threefold. The first is universal, which for teaching's sake is named the Pedagogical or Political use (*usus Paedagogicus seu Politicus*): namely, to restrain the outward members in all men, or the locomotive power, that the outward deeds may agree with the Moral Law, and discipline may be preserved, according to the rules: *"He that taketh the sword shall perish with the sword."* *"Woe to thee that spoilest, for thou shalt be spoiled."* *"Whoremongers and adulterers God will judge."* **1 Timoth. 1**: *"The Law is made for the unrighteous."* Likewise: Heinous offences are punished with heinous punishments, as experience in the whole race of mankind, and all histories, do testify. Hence among the Heathen there are very notable Legal sentences:

> Learn righteousness, being warned, and not to despise  
> the Gods, etc.  

<!-- p.37 -->

And this use of the Law is named by a general word, Discipline, the profits whereof it very greatly concerneth us to remember. First, it is necessary to render discipline, because of the most earnest commandment of God, unto which obedience is due. Next, to avoid the punishments wherewith either the magistrate or God punisheth heinous offences. Afterward, for the sake of public peace and tranquillity, that men may be governed and taught. Fourthly, because discipline is a pedagogy unto Christ, that is, in them Christ is not effectual, who cease not to defile themselves with wickednesses against conscience. This praise of discipline is great, and unknown to the Heathen. And these things may be unfolded in a notable discourse.

The second use of the Law is the Theological (*usus Theologicus*): to show and reprove sin, to strike in terror, to make manifest infirmity and the wrath of God. Hence **Paul** saith: *"The Law worketh wrath."* *"The sting of death is sin, etc."* *"By the Law is the knowledge of sin."* *"By the Law sin becometh exceeding guilty."* This use of the Law is felt in true repentance, not in secure men, as **Paul** saith: *"I was alive without the Law once,"* that is, I was secure, I felt not the judgement of God; afterward, being thoroughly terrified, I acknowledged my sins. But always unto this use of the Law there is to be added the voice of the Gospel, showing the Lamb of God, which taketh away sins. For we are terrified by the Law, not that we should perish, but that we should flee unto the Mediator, as **Paul** saith: *"He hath concluded all under sin, that he might have mercy upon all."* And this use of the Law chiefly showeth that men are not justified by the Law, but accused.

The third use of the Law is in the regenerate only: namely, to teach the regenerate saints what worships please God, and what manner of obedience God requireth, as it is said, **Ezek. 20**: *"Walk ye in my statutes, not in the statutes of your Fathers."* Likewise: *"In vain do they worship me with the commandments of men."* Likewise: *"Thy word is a lamp unto my feet, and thy word a light unto my paths."* For to this end were we created, that we should obey God; to this end are we renewed, that there may be in us a conformity with God according to the Law, as it is said, **Rom. 8**: *"That the righteousness of the Law might be <!-- p.38 --> fulfilled in us."* And Christ himself saith: *"I am not come to destroy the Law, but to fulfil."* And verily it is to be lamented that this use of the Law is taken away and made light of by certain fanatics, whether Antinomians or enthusiasts, whom nevertheless let us now dismiss. For although the justified are delivered from the Law, that it condemn them not, yet they also have need of the doctrine of the Law in three ways. First, as touching doctrine; next, as touching the oldness of the flesh; afterward, as touching obedience. For concerning doctrine it is manifest that even the righteous have need of the word, that they may know what worships and what manner of works please God, lest they devise new worships without the word, and monkeries and other superstitions and idolatries; whence **Paul** saith: *"All Scripture is profitable."* It is necessary also that the oldness of the flesh, or the old man, be restrained and reproved, because the Law always reproveth present sin; but we are free in this wise, not that the Law doth not reprove, but if by faith we overcome the Law reproving, as the Lord's prayer testifieth. There would be no debts, unless the Law did bind and reprove. Lastly, although we be free from the Law, that it condemn not, yet there remaineth the ordinance and will of God, that the creature obey. Wherefore obedience is necessary; and of what manner it ought to be, the Law showeth: but how it pleaseth, especially seeing it is very faint and imperfect, the Gospel teacheth. And the ordinance of God remaineth unmoved, that the creature obey, even when it obeyeth willingly, as Christ saith: *"This is my commandment, That ye love one another."* Obedience ought to be voluntary and of free accord, and yet it is a commandment, and is necessary for him that believeth. For to be necessary, and to be of free accord or free, are in no wise opposed, seeing Christian liberty also is necessary, and yet is liberty. And the Gospel meanwhile teacheth how far we are free, to wit, that the Law condemn not, not that it be not done. Therefore Christ saith: *"I am not come to destroy the Law, but to fulfil."* Now Christ fulfilleth the Law in four ways. I. By his own obedience. II. By turning the punishment upon himself. III. By restoring in us righteousness and life eternal. IIII. By ratifying and confirming the precepts of the Law. And let it suffice to have briefly admonished these things concerning the <!-- p.39 --> threefold use of the Law. Now, before we take in hand the exposition of the Decalogue, let us set before us these four things to be considered:

I. Unto what man was created.

II. Of the misery of man fallen.

III. Of the restoration, that the Law may be done.

IIII. Of the appeasing, and the fulfilling of the Law, for the sake of and through the Son of God.

### *De primo* · Of the First

Unto what man was created, the Law showeth. For human nature was to have been such as the Law describeth, with full knowledge of God, without sin, without calamities and death, in such wise that man should be like unto God, and that in him there should shine a mind clearly knowing the essence and the will of God without any doubtings and errors, and that in the mind there should give light, without any darkness, a wisdom discerning things honest and things base, as the rule of all actions: next, that he should have a will free, not hindered, having uprightness and a righteousness agreeing with God, full of trust and gladness in God, and ruling the lower powers, without their stubbornness, without sin, and without death. This purity and dignity of man the Law describeth unto us, and what the entire image of God in man was, it showeth according to the inward and the outward obedience and purity.

### *De secundo* · Of the Second

The Law admonisheth us of the present misery, of the defacing of the image wrought by the fall of our first parents, and derived by carnal propagation into us all. We see that our nature fighteth with the Law, and that there is in the mind a darkness that knoweth not God, and doubtings concerning the providence and the <!-- p.40 --> will of God; and in the will a turning away from God, an emptiness of the fear of God, of faith, of love, carnal security, distrust, admiration of self, concupiscence, corrupt inclinations; in the heart a stubbornness of all the affections against the will of God. And there is no moment of time wherein the godly, as well in the inward motions as in the outward deeds, gestures and speeches, do not feel and bewail this depravation in themselves: albeit this also is true, that we do not understand our misery, nor truly hear the Law, save when in true terrors and punishments we do in some sort acknowledge the wrath of God against sin. *"If thou, Lord, shouldest mark iniquities, O Lord, who shall stand?"* **Ps. 129 [130]**. Let us therefore pray God that he would mitigate the wrath, which no man is able to sustain and bear, because God is a consuming fire.

### *De tertio* · Of the Third

The Law admonisheth us also of the restoration and renewing of the former excellency, and of life eternal. For seeing God repeateth the voice of the Law after nature was fallen, he willeth verily that the Law be at some time done. But seeing in this life the Law is not satisfied by us, there shall be a restoration of mankind, and a life eternal, wherein the Law shall be satisfied. This doctrine of the Restoration is afterward illustrated in the promises.

### *De quarto* · Of the Fourth

When we acknowledge the misery of mankind out of the Law, let us always over against it behold the Son of God, who for us bare the curse of the Law, or the wrath of his Father against sin, appeasing the Father by his obedience, in such wise that grace doth now abound above sin. This benefit of the Son of God is in this place to be considered, with notable testimonies added: *"Behold the Lamb of God, which taketh away,* ***αἴρει****, the sins of the world,"* that is, transferreth them upon himself and abolisheth them. For ***τὸ αἴρειν*** (to take away) comprehendeth both, <!-- p.41 --> to take away from another, and to turn upon oneself. **1 Pet. 3**: *"Christ hath suffered for our sins, the just for the unjust, that he might offer us unto God, etc."*
