**XII.** Now it remaineth that something be said of the regenerate man, who, having heard the word, receiveth the Holy Spirit. For he is so enlightened of God in his understanding, by the new power of the Holy Spirit, that he is able rightly to acknowledge the things which are of God and which pertain to eternal salvation; and next he is so led of the merciful God in his will, by the working of the same Spirit, that he rightly and spiritually willeth the things which are of God, and is borne cheerfully unto good actions. And hence are sprung the phrases, taken out of Paul and from elsewhere, concerning the regenerate man:

<!-- p.342 -->

**Of the regenerate man it is wont to be said:**

- The will is not idle, nor doth it behave itself as a statue, but it doth somewhat, and is the companion of the Holy Spirit.
- There is a certain synergy (*synergia*), and a new liberty, **1 Tim. 4**: *"Exercise thyself unto godliness."* **Phil. 2**: *"Work out your own salvation."*
- He doth not behave himself purely passively (*pure passive*) in spiritual newness.
- Unto the inward renewing of the heart three causes concur: the Word, the Spirit, and the will.
- Man hath free will, which is the faculty of applying himself unto grace, especially in the spiritual wrestling, when faith is assaulted.
- The obedience of man is spontaneous and free.
- He who, being unregenerate, did by nature resist the Holy Spirit, now, being regenerate, resisteth his own nature and flesh, the Holy Spirit being his leader, and represseth the rebellion of nature.
- The regenerate man retaineth the Holy Spirit by the exercises of faith, of invocation, and of good works, according to that saying:[^P17-1] *"War a good warfare; holding faith, and a good conscience,"* etc.

In these and the like manners of speaking (which of themselves are most false concerning the unregenerate man, or concerning free will not set free) it is significantly said that the godly themselves do will and work, both in conversion — because it is necessary that wrestling and assent be present, in respect not of merit or of efficacy, but of the order prescribed by God — and afterward also in the whole life. Yet meanwhile all the power and efficacy is ascribed to God Himself as received from Him, in such wise that, if the understanding and will of the regenerate man be considered by themselves, to wit, abstractively (if indeed that can in any way be done), they truly avail nothing of themselves unto their own regeneration, illumination, <!-- p.343 --> and perseverance; but if God be considered, of free mercy giving unto him the Holy Spirit, who enlighteneth the understanding, bendeth the will, reneweth hearts, and confirmeth the godly in true faith, confession and constancy, then let man be understood to be able both to will and to accomplish that which is good, and that indeed by his own will, of his own accord, or *"of the good purpose of the mind"*, **Phil. 2**, that is, that some things may be done which are acceptable unto God. For thus saith Paul: *"With fear and trembling"* (as it were with the highest degree of fear, as **Chrysostom** saith) *"work out your own salvation"*, ***κατεργάζεσθε***, that is, with the whole mind and with all diligence apply yourselves, that ye may retain purity of doctrine, the benefits of Christ, Faith, and the Holy Spirit, and may avoid falls against conscience. For God is ***ὁ ἐνεργῶν ἐν ὑμῖν***, that is, He who implanteth efficacy in you, and supplieth the faculty of doing — not by compulsion, or by a snatching and violent motion upon men unwilling and unknowing, but He goeth before them as men understanding and following, as **Prosper** saith, and maketh them desirous of subjection, and perfecteth in you the willing, that it may be full, and that all things which ye will and accomplish may be directed to the glory of God, or, ***ὑπὲρ τῆς εὐδοκίας***, that is, that some things may be done which are acceptable unto God. For the preposition[^P17-2] ***ὑπὲρ*** signifieth the final cause, not the efficient; ***εὐδοκία*** signifieth delight, approbation, and a gladness that resteth in some thing, even as God Himself speaketh of the Son, ***ἐν ᾧ εὐδόκησα*** (in whom I am well pleased). And the Angels sing: Unto men ***εὐδοκία***, that is, a gladness resting in the glory of God, and in the peace established through the Son of God between God Himself and mankind.

**XIII.** Now that abstraction of which we gave warning a little before is diligently to be held fast, whereby the understanding and will of the regenerate man is separated from the Holy Spirit; even as for the same cause Paul also opposeth the old and the new man, or flesh and Spirit, not so much in diverse men (in whom the new man is so named on account of faith) as in the same men, that is, in the regenerate, in whom, namely, the new man is so named on account of the new fruits of the new tree, that is, on account of the new obedience and life which followeth the justification of faith. But the powers of the soul are never in this <!-- p.344 --> life so confirmed in good, that the regenerate are able either to avoid all occasions of sins, or all sins, or to accomplish all the good things which they would. Therefore in the regenerate that which is opposed to the Spirit is called the old man. For because the regenerate remain endued with natural understanding and will, therefore there ever cleaveth in them something born of the flesh and subject to sin. Again, because they have received the Holy Spirit, they themselves approve not their own sin, nor suffer it to have dominion, but resist sin by invocation, by the help and excellent power of the Holy Spirit, and ever pray God that He would remit their sins for the Son's sake, and rule and lead them in their calling and in their whole life, lest, either through their own weakness or circumvented by the snares of the Devil, they rush into errors and into wicked deeds, but that they may be and remain temples of the Holy Spirit. They acknowledge also withal, that God doth very many things in all the Saints in such wise, that the will is only a patient subject (*subiectum patiens*). Yet meanwhile they hold fast the Rule: *"Faith cometh by hearing"*; and they sustain themselves by the thought of the promise, they resist distrust, and they know that they have a faithful and strong advocate and ***παραστάτην*** (one who standeth by them), namely, the Holy Spirit, whom they grieve not, but stir up by meditation on the word of God and by ardent prayer, even as Paul biddeth Timothy ***ἀναζωπυρεῖν***, that is, to kindle again, as it were a fire almost quenched, the gift of God; and also in their own infirmity and languor of faith they know that they ought and are able, the Holy Spirit helping them, to resist doubting, and amid true groanings they say:

> ***Πιστεύω μικρῇ πίστει ὦ χριστὲ βοήθει***,

*"I believe, Lord, but help Thou my weakness."* For the gifts of God are stirred up first of all by meditation on the doctrine; next by invocation, by the exercises of repentance, by the consideration of our own infirmity, by resisting the sudden burnings of the affections, by sustaining conflicts and perils in confession.

**XIIII.** It is wont sometimes to be objected by the godly who are engaged in the exercises of repentance, that a great infirmity <!-- p.345 --> of faith is felt, which showeth that our powers are plainly none. For this objection there is need not so much of the theoretical doctrine which hath hitherto been delivered, as of consolation, which is the virtue, energy and strength of doctrine. It is true that faith is ***ὑπόστασιν*** (a firm standing), as it is named in the book of **Wisd. 16** and in **Heb. 11**, or *Iezer Somuch*,[^P17-3] **Isa. 26**, that is, a thought propped up and sustained from above, which the doctrine of the Gospel nameth ***πεποίθησιν***, ***παῤῥησίαν*** and ***πληροφορίαν*** (confidence, boldness and full assurance). Whence **Basil** saith: ***τὶ ἴδιον τῆς πίστεως; ἀδιάκριτος πληροφορία τῆς ἀληθείας τῶν θεοπνεύστων ῥημάτων, οὐδενὶ λογισμῷ οὔτε φυσικῆς ἀνάγκης εἰσαγομένῳ, οὔτε πρὸς εὐσέβειαν ἐσχηματισμένῳ διασαλευομένη*** (What is the property of faith? An unhesitating full assurance of the truth of the God-breathed words, shaken by no reasoning, whether one brought in of natural necessity or one dressed up in the form of godliness). And all those are to be abhorred who confirm doubting, whether by speculations concerning the number of the predestinate outside the word, or by security and the Academic ***ἐποχῇ*** (suspension of judgement), or by the Pontifical depravation of the doctrine of faith; as we have also spoken in its own place of these three kinds of men, who bring in doubting concerning the grace of God. Here only tender minds and consciences, which acknowledge, confess and bewail the weakness of their faith, are to be admonished, that we must not always judge of the truth of faith by feeling, but by the word of God. For feeling goeth not before faith, but followeth it; and faith is oftentimes so overwhelmed and hidden by the cross and other calamities, that it is not felt. Now out of the word of God we learn these four things. First, that the strength of the Lord is made perfect in weakness, **2 Cor. 12**. And, as **Luther** was wont to say, all the works of God are done in the midst of contraries. He is mighty in the greatest weakness, He enlighteneth minds in the thickest darkness, He quickeneth in the midst of death, and, as the Church singeth of the Holy Spirit, in labour He is rest, in heat a tempering, in weeping solace: He washeth that which is foul, watereth that which is dry, healeth that which is wounded, bendeth that which is stiff, cherisheth that which is cold, ruleth that which is astray, etc. Secondly, faith is not always a burning light, or a trust sensibly perceptible, and ever abiding in one and the same manner, or a flame ever spirited, and openly and brightly <!-- p.346 --> shining, but it is only smoking flax, **Isa. 42**, **Matt. 12**: sometimes in knowledge, **Rom. 14**; sometimes in trust, **Mark 9**. And often faith is great, as in the Centurion and in the woman of Canaan, **Matt. 8** and **15**. Sometimes it is weak and languid, and yet is true Faith. And the changes and alterations of the quantity, so to speak, or of the decrease and increase of faith, according to the nature of the objects, circumstances and temptations, are felt by all the holiest men, Jacob, Moses, David, Peter, and others. Thirdly: the worth of faith is not esteemed by its own quantity or quality, but by the object which it apprehendeth; and when it erreth not in that, although it be exceeding languid, small and infirm, yet it is true faith; as **Doctor Cruciger**, of godly memory, had said before his last agony: *"I believe with a languid faith, but yet with faith."* For faith is only as it were a hand or a finger apprehending a ring; which whether a weak child lay hold of, were it but with the little finger, or a strong man with his whole hand, certainly each hath the ring. Fourthly: Scripture biddeth us pray that our faith may be confirmed and may increase, **Mark 9**, **Luke 17**. This Prayer is an evident testimony of the presence of faith, although the feeling of it be either very small or plainly none. For the feeling of grief, groaning, prayer, wrestling, and the desire of the increase of faith, do abundantly testify that faith is present.

**XV.** But as touching perseverance, concerning which also question is wont to be made, it is to be known that, although many by their own fault, by security, by distrust and by the works of the flesh, fall from grace, yet this cometh not to pass thence or for this cause, as though God were unwilling to preserve unto the end the believers whom He hath once received into grace; but men themselves of their own will fall from grace, which, the Holy Spirit being their Leader and helper, they ought and were able to retain by the assiduous and frequent use of the Word and Sacraments, and by prayer unto God for the gift of perseverance, and by wrestling against the wantonness of the flesh. We have assuredly God ***ἀρχηγὸν*** (the author), the Son ***τελεσίουργον*** (the finisher), the Holy Spirit ***βεβαιώτην μόνιμον*** (the abiding confirmer). He therefore who hath begun in <!-- p.347 --> us will perform it until the day of Jesus Christ, **Phil. 1**. And: *"Who shall separate us?"* *"I am persuaded, that neither things present, nor things to come, shall separate us"* — provided only that our trust and persuasion of certainty degenerate not into carnal security, whereby true faith is choked and quenched, nor defile itself with the actions of the flesh, whereby faith is shaken out. For it is written: *"Let him that standeth take heed lest he fall"*, namely, by carnal security or insolence, and by the actions of the flesh.

Let these things, spoken of the free will of man and set forth in few words, now suffice. For to discourse more largely of all those things which we have touched will perhaps belong to another place. Now, as we proposed, we shall subjoin a brief admonition concerning the sixth point, namely, concerning the works of unbelievers.

## *De Sexto, videlicet de operibus infidelium* · On the Sixth Point, namely, the Works of Unbelievers

It is certain that the things which man, even unregenerate, hath remaining from the first creation, are of themselves good, but yet are so depraved by sin, that the actions thereof do themselves also degenerate into sin before the judgement of God, to wit, in two ways: in respect of the end, and in respect of the old man not yet born again. For unbelievers, or Heathen, refer not their virtues to this end, that they may glorify God, but seek thence their own fame and glory, or else pleasure, and other bodily advantages. Next, the Heathen lack true faith, and are as yet an evil tree, or wild Olive; and although they put forth works most fair in show, yet they do them out of an ungodly heart, seeing they are not yet reconciled to God. Now there abideth the most true Rule: *"Whatsoever is not of faith is sin."* Likewise: *"Without faith it is impossible to please God."* And it is rightly said: The person displeasing, the work displeaseth. For God judgeth not according to the outward work, but looketh upon the heart. And duties are weighed not by their acts, but by their ends. And worthy of remembrance is the saying of **Ambrose**: *"Without the worship of the true God, even that which seemeth to be virtue is sin, neither can <!-- p.348 --> any man please God without God."* And of this matter **Augustine** discourseth at large in many places, and in book 4 *Against Julian*, chap. 3, saith: *"Far be it that any should be truly just, unless he live by faith: for the just shall live by faith. Who of them that would be accounted Christians will call just an unbeliever, an ungodly man, one made over in bondage to the devil? though he be that Fabricius, Fabius, Scipio, Regulus? How are they truly just, to whom the humility of the truly Just One is a thing of no price?"* etc. We do not indeed shut out the Gentiles, but we say that they are saved in no other way than by the word of Christ. False therefore, and blasphemous against the word and the merit of the Son of God, is that which some have affirmed concerning Numa, Hector, Scipio, and other Heathen who knew not the Son of God: that every man is saved by his own faith, whether natural or legal; because it is certain that all have ever been saved, and are saved, in one and the same way, namely, by the Lamb that was slain from the beginning, **Rev. 13**. For there is none other name given unto men, nor other foundation laid, whereby men must be saved, but the name of JESUS Christ. Whosoever believeth in Him is saved; whosoever believeth not in Him perisheth by the just thunderbolt of the Law.

The places which are cited out of **Justin** concerning Socrates and Heraclitus, as though they, seeing they lived according to right reason, were to be numbered among Christians; and out of **Clement** concerning Philosophy, as though by the study thereof the Greeks were justified; and out of **Epiphanius**, who affirmeth that many were saved by the Law of nature: these we now set aside, not indeed with any dissembling of the true judgement, which is fetched, namely, from the analogy of faith, but because we are minded to commend them to no man by any excuse. For it is certain that there is no salvation, whether natural, or Philosophical, or any other, by whatsoever name it be called, save that which is given from above, of the mere mercy of God, for the Son's sake, to all that believe in the Son. This is the divine, true, fixed, unmoved and only, and the one, way of salvation and of eternal life.

This indeed is true, that the virtues even of the Heathen have their own sure rewards in this life, and merit the mitigation of many <!-- p.349 --> punishments. And there shall also be certain degrees of the eternal punishments. For not all sins, even in the unregenerate, are equal, as is said in **Matt. 11**: *"It shall be more tolerable for Sodom in the day of judgment, than for Chorazin."* And they who are not good may be, some less, some more evil; and they who are less evil shall be more tolerably punished, because they have, in a sort, done by nature the things contained in the Law. Fabricius shall be less punished than Catiline, not because the one is good, but because the other is more evil, as **Augustine** speaketh. Moreover this also is true, and indeed at this day too true, that those very men are put to confusion who would be reported Christians, and indeed the foremost and most godly of all — the Zealots, I say, the Sacramentarian hypocrites, the ***ἁρμοστὰς, ἐπαρχοὺς ἐπιστατὰς*** (governors, prefects, overseers) and others of the same meal — and that they ought deservedly to be made ashamed, if they would institute a comparison between the honesty of the Heathen, of very many of them truly, and that loosening of discipline and ***ἀναρχίας*** (anarchy) which hath now been brought in by their wantonness, ambition, discord, and furious and devilish stirring up of all things. So much, forsooth, hath an erroneous and unseasonable zeal effected, swollen with envy, with ambition, ***καὶ μνησικακίᾳ, καὶ δοξομανίᾳ*** (and with remembrance of injuries, and with a madness for glory) in the extreme; so much doth the contempt and condemnation of others who think rightly and godly effect; so much our own arrogance, and the persuasion of I know not what holiness inborn in us. But let us now break off these things also, and commit them to the Son of God to be reformed, seeing that now, as **Basil** saith, ***μήτε ναυάρχου, μήτε κυβερνήτου φωνὴ*** (the voice neither of admiral nor of pilot) is heard, but there is of all things ***δεινή τις ἀταξία καὶ σύγχυσις*** (a certain dreadful disorder and confusion). Yet it pleaseth me, as it were to satisfy the grief of my mind, to add the words of **Eusebius**, describing the state which followed the times of the Apostles, that it may be compared with our own condition in this age, which, chiefly through the Sacramentarian Apostates, hath followed the death of **Luther** and of **Philip**: *"After that our affairs, through too much liberty, degenerated into softness and sloth, and some pursued others with hatred and reproaches, and we all but assailed our own selves by our own selves with the arms and darts of words, wheresoever it befell, and Bishops rushed upon <!-- p.350 --> Bishops, and peoples stirred up seditions against peoples; then, when unspeakable hypocrisy and dissimulation had advanced even to the height of wickedness, the Divine judgement, after its wont, while as yet the Ecclesiastical assemblies were gathered together, began gently and by slow degrees to visit us, so that the persecution took its beginning from the brethren who were in the army. But when we were touched with no feeling thereof, neither endeavoured to render God appeased and propitious unto us, but, as ungodly men, supposed that God neither cared for our offences nor visited them, we heaped our wickednesses one upon another. And they who seemed to be our Pastors, casting off the rule of godliness, were inflamed with mutual contentions among themselves; and while they heap up these things only, namely, contentions, threatenings, emulation, mutual hatred and enmity, and each man eagerly pursueth his own ambition after the manner of a tyranny, then truly the Lord, then, I say, according to the voice of Jeremiah, darkened the daughter of Zion, and cast down from heaven the glory of Israel, and remembered not His footstool in the day of His anger. And the Lord hath swallowed up all the beautiful things of Israel, and hath thrown down all his hedges. And according to the prophecies of the Psalms, He hath overturned and made void the Covenant of His servant, and hath profaned His sanctuary to the ground, through the destruction of the Churches, and hath broken down all his hedges. He hath made his strong holds a terror, and all that passed that way have spoiled the multitude of the people. And moreover he is become a reproach to his neighbours. For He hath set up the right hand of his enemies, and hath turned away the help of his sword, and hath not succoured him in the battle; but He hath also ceased from his cleansing, and hath dashed his throne to the ground, and hath shortened the days of his time, and over and above all these things hath poured out shame upon him."*[^P17-4]

These things we were minded to discourse of briefly concerning these matters, reserving a larger ***ἐξήγησιν*** (exposition) to a more convenient occasion. Now, putting an end to the former part of the Christian rudiments, we shall weave on one Question only.

<!-- p.351 -->

## *Possumusne Legi Dei satisfacere?* · Can We Satisfy the Law of God?

From those things which we have already said it is manifest that no man can satisfy the Law of God. For the whole lump of our nature is corrupted. And the same is witnessed by the universal experience in all the Saints, who groan and grieve that they are hindered by this evil from doing the good things which they would, **Rom. 7**: *"O wretched man that I am! who shall deliver me from the body of this death?"* And a certain Hermit: *"O Lord, grant that I may die unto myself."* And this was **Luther's** exposition of the saying, *"If thou wilt enter into life, keep the commandments"*: that is, die. And it is reported of Charles IIII, King of France, that on a time, being deep in thought, he bewailed the mass and heap of his sins, and with a great sigh and groan said unto his wife: *"Now, God helping me, I will so bear myself in my whole life henceforth, that I may no more offend God my Creator and Redeemer"*; and that, having said this, he straightway fell down and gave up the ghost.

The First Commandment cannot be satisfied by us in this corrupt nature, which is exceeding unlike unto this Law, **Deut. 6**: *"Thou shalt love the Lord thy God with all thine heart."* Our heart is cold and ice; it burneth not with true love toward God.

The Second Commandment is not satisfied, because no man calleth upon God and giveth thanks ardently enough, neither have we a zeal ardent enough in the confession and propagation of the true doctrine.

The Third Commandment is not satisfied in this life, because no man reverently enough honoureth the most holy ministry, in teaching, hearing, learning, and the other duties.

The Fourth Commandment is not satisfied, because parents are too negligent and too indulgent, and sometimes also too savage; children and subjects are unthankful and disobedient. No man is diligent enough in his calling, neither parents nor children, neither Magistrates nor subjects. We are all guilty.

The Fifth Commandment is not satisfied, because in the heart even of the Saints there remaineth uncleanness; there is not true and perfect meekness, <!-- p.352 --> ***συμπάθεια*** and ***φιλοστοργία*** (fellow-feeling and tender natural affection); there is present impatience, and anger, etc.

The Sixth Commandment is not satisfied, because the heart is defiled and impure, and feeleth inordinate flames, wandering outside of order.

The Seventh Commandment is not satisfied, because distrust concerning sustenance ever cleaveth in this corrupt nature, which distrust bringeth forth many other sins.

The Eighth Commandment is not satisfied, because uncleanness in the heart, and an inclination unto lying, slanders and backbitings, remain in the minds of men. There is not a hatred of lying ardent enough, nor a sincere love and ardent zeal for truth. Therefore we are all guilty, as it is written, **Ps. 116**: *"All men are liars."*

The Ninth and Tenth Commandment cannot be satisfied, because Concupiscence hath taken possession of the whole nature of man; which Concupiscence is that dreadful depravation of the appetites in nature, and ***ἀταξία*** (disorder) of the affections and of all the powers. As, in the mind, darkness and a mist concerning God, doubtings concerning the providence and will of God. In the will, a turning away from God, the being without love and fear of God, without ardent faith. In the heart, contumacy, rebellion, uncleanness, depraved inclinations, out of which arise depraved affections, and impulses wandering and warring with the Law of God, namely, envy, rages, lusts, covetousness and lies.

Now since we cannot satisfy the Law of God, the threatening abideth, **Deut. 27**: *"Cursed is every one that continueth not in all things which are written in the Law."* We are therefore all children of wrath, condemned and cast off of God, as touching ourselves and our own merits.

Since also we know that the Son of God, our Lord Jesus Christ, ***λόγον καὶ εἰκόνα ἀϊδίου πατρὸς*** (the Word and image of the eternal Father), hath fulfilled the Law by His obedience, by His passion and death, that He might redeem us from eternal punishments, it is necessary that the Law of God be begun by us in this life, seeing we cannot perfectly perform it, <!-- p.353 --> as Christ saith, **Matt. 19**: *"If thou wilt enter into life, keep the commandments."* Now we know that an obedience pleasing to God is begun when, having heard the Gospel, we acknowledge the mercy freely promised, and believe that for the Son's sake we are received, and that our sins are remitted unto us. By this faith the person is reconciled, and pleaseth God, and the Holy Spirit reneweth the heart, and bendeth and inclineth it, that it may now begin to obey God. This beginning, although it is imperfect, yet now pleaseth, because the person is received, and pleaseth for the Son's sake. For it is necessary that Christ the Son of God be apprehended, without whom the Law is not done, **Rom. 10**: *"Christ is the end"*, that is, the fulfilling. And for Christ's sake the new and begun obedience pleaseth, when, namely, these three things concur: **I.** When thou believest that the person is received for the Mediator's sake. **II.** When thou acknowledgest and grievest that thou hast sin, and askest pardon, **1 John 1**: *"If any man confess,"* etc. **III.** When thou holdest that the begun obedience pleaseth God for Christ's sake, and that the uncleanness of the heart is forgiven, and askest to be governed and helped by the Holy Spirit. But if we shall not study to begin the Law in this life, by calling upon God, and by the help of the Holy Spirit, and by our own diligence, but live secure, as is done at this day everywhere in Germany and elsewhere, with a foul licence unto all vices, behold, let us hear the threatenings of God against the stubborn and disobedient, **Lev. 26**, **Deut. 28**.

**I.** *"I will visit you with terrors, with fevers, with consumption, with diseases and with many wars."* This is the first degree of punishments, the first admonition to repent; which admonition if it have no weight, there is now at hand the second degree of punishments.

**II.** *"I will visit you with barrenness, with dearth of corn, with famine. I will make the heavens as iron, and the earth as metal. All your labour shall be in vain. But if ye will not regard this either:*

**III.** *I will send wild beasts, monsters and brutes, which shall lay waste your fields, and tear you in pieces. But if ye do not yet come to a better mind:*

**IIII.** *I will draw out the sword of vengeance against you, namely, pestilence, epidemical diseases, plague, wars and famine. If neither by these ye be moved to repentance:*

<!-- p.354 -->

**V.** *I will withstand you, and will wholly take away my hand from you. Famine shall consume you. Idolatry shall rage among you. Your families shall be blotted out. Ye shall be made subject to your enemies. Ye shall have hearts tender and fearful. Ye shall despair in your sins, and shall say: Rightly hath God sent these punishments upon us, who would not obey Him."* And this is that which **Clement of Alexandria** saith of the procedure of God: *"The Word, threatenings, the Rod, Fire."* And in **Ps. 7** David saith: *"Except ye be converted, He hath brandished His sword,"* etc.

We in Germany have hitherto had: **I.** Diseases and wars. **II.** Famine and barrenness. **III.** Wild beasts and brutes, bodily and spiritual, in the Churches and Universities, laying waste the land. **IIII.** Pestilence. **V.** The Turk shall fill up, or the Muscovite, or some other enemy of the Christian name. The Word of God hath been miserably blasphemed, and is blasphemed daily: therefore we shall suffer somewhat. God have mercy upon us graciously, even as He hath promised in the same place that He will do: *"I will be mindful,"* saith He, *"of my covenant, and will not utterly cast them away, although they be in the land of their enemies. For I am their God, and I loathe them not, if only they turn unto me, and say: Rightly and justly hath God dealt with us. Righteous art Thou, O Lord, and righteous is Thy judgement."* Let us know also, that rewards are promised of God, out of grace and mere goodness, unto our godliness and conversion, both in this life and in that which is to come. Life indeed is not given on account of works; but in life there is made a recompense, as it is written: *"Godliness is profitable unto all things, having promise of the life that now is, and of that which is to come,"* **1 Tim. 4**; and **Matt. 5**: *"Great is your reward in heaven."* **Luke 14**: *"Thou shalt be recompensed at the resurrection of the just."* Let us therefore study godliness, let us love and reverence the doctrine, let us be modest, temperate, of one mind, chaste, truthful, knowing that whatsoever good thing any man doeth, the same shall he receive of God, **Eph. 6**. Unto the Eternal Father of our Lord Jesus Christ, and unto Jesus Christ our Redeemer and Saviour, and unto the Holy Spirit, be praise, honour, thanks and glory from everlasting to everlasting, AMEN.

# *Appendix* · Appendix

Forasmuch as we have hitherto expounded the words of the Decalogue in order and briefly, and Ethical doctrine is a part of the Decalogue, concerning which it is necessary that youth be rightly instructed, now, in the end of the first part of our *Paedia*, something is also to be added for the sake of the Younger sort: **I.** Of the comparison of Heathen and Christian discipline; which being considered, not only is the deciding of very weighty disputations made easier, but it may also thence be seen in what things Ethical doctrine doth not satisfy the argument which it nevertheless handleth, and whence and how it may be filled up, confirmed and perfected. **II.** Of the uses of Ethical doctrine. **III.** Of the Question, whether, how, and how far the use of Philosophical doctrine is granted unto Christians. **IIII.** Of the end of man.

## *De Primo, id est, de collatione disciplinae Ethnicae et Christianae pietatis* · On the First Point, that is, the Comparison of Heathen Discipline and Christian Godliness

Although one who beholdeth only in passing and from without the exercises of the virtues in an unregenerate man, especially a heroic one, judgeth that they may deservedly be compared with the virtues of regenerate men, even the greatest, yet that there is an immense difference between the virtues of the Heathen and those which are proper to the Church of God and to the members thereof, is shown by the consideration of the impelling causes (*causae impulsivae*), of the efficient cause, of the form, and of the end. **Livy** writeth a celebrated history of Scipio <!-- p.356 --> restraining himself from the captive bride of Allucius, and, in words most worthy of a great Hero, thus addressing the bridegroom, whom he had sent for unto him: *"Young man,"* saith he, *"I address thee as a young man, that there may be the less bashfulness between us in this discourse. When thy bride, taken by our soldiers, had been brought unto me, and I heard that she was dear to thy heart, and her beauty made it credible"* (a beauty so excellent, that wheresoever she went she drew the eyes of all upon her), *"because I myself, were it permitted me to enjoy the sport of youth, especially in a right and lawful love, and had not the Commonwealth taken possession of our mind, should wish that pardon were granted me for loving a bride too ardently: I favour thy love, which I am able to favour. Thy bride hath been with me in the same modesty as with thy parents-in-law, her own parents: she hath been kept for thee, that she might be given thee a gift inviolate, and worthy of me and of thee. This one recompense I bargain for in return for that gift, that thou be a friend to the Roman people; and if thou believest me to be a good man, such as these nations knew my father and my uncle to be heretofore, know thou that there are many like unto us in the Roman state, and that no people on earth can be named which thou wouldest less wish to be an enemy to thee and thine, or more wish to be a friend,"* etc. **Pindar** recounteth, in the fifth Ode of the *Nemeans*, the history of Peleus abstaining from Hippolyta the daughter of Cretheus,[^P17-5] wife of Acastus King of the Magnetes, by whom he was solicited, and who, being wroth for the repulse, accused Peleus before her husband; who, suspecting evil of him, commanded him to be cast before the Centaurs, against whom Peleus is written to have defended himself with a sword sent him from heaven. For God giveth rewards unto chastity, and, as **Pindar** most sweetly saith, accompanying every man from his birth, He is arbiter concerning deeds, ***κρῖνει συγγενὴς ἔργων περὶ***. **Herodotus** relateth in *Calliope*, the ninth book, a history of Pausanias worthy of remembrance, namely, that there came unto Pausanias, after the slaughter of the Barbarians wrought at Plataea,[^P17-6] a woman of Cos, most beautiful, adorned with much gold and borne in a Chariot, and that, embracing the knees of Pausanias, she besought as a suppliant to be delivered from the bondage of captivity; to whom Pausanias answered: *"Be of good cheer, woman, both because thou art a suppliant, and because thou askest just things, and speakest true."* And that she was so <!-- p.357 --> dismissed, and commended to the trust of the Ephors, **Herodotus** affirmeth. Known also are other examples: of Bellerophon restraining himself from Antia, or Stheneboea, the wife of Proetus, by whom he had been solicited, and who, bearing the repulse with a vexed mind, afterward accused him before her husband; by whose commandment he was cast before the Chimaera, against which, flying up into the air upon Pegasus sent him from heaven, he overcame the Chimaera — that is, God gave him a testimony of his innocence. Likewise: of Cyrus, who would not look upon Panthea; of Alexander the Great, who would not behold the captive wife of King Darius; of Hippolytus the son of Theseus, invited by his stepmother to incestuous converse. One who considereth these and the like examples of chastity, to say nothing now of the other virtues, will say: What difference, then, can there be between the continence of Scipio or of Peleus, and that of Joseph abstaining from the wife of his master, when he had been oftentimes solicited by her to break his faith? Or what difference can be shown between the patience of Socrates and of David? **I answer:** The difference consisteth first in the impelling causes. For the impelling causes which bend the unregenerate unto moderation, and unto the study of chastity, of patience and of the other virtues, are rightly numbered four:

The First: Punishments, of which it is said:

> — ***Ἔχει θεὸς ἔκδικον ὄμμα*** (God hath an avenging eye).

And

> — ***Οὐκ αἰεὶ κακὰ ἔργα*** (Not for ever do evil deeds [prosper]).

> No man shall long be happy in his crimes:  
> Yet punishment, though late, cometh on silent feet.

Likewise:

> ***Οἷα τ' ἀνὴρ ῥέζοι, τοῖον τέλος αὐτὸν ἱκάνει*** (Such things as a man doeth, such an end overtaketh him).

And the verse of Rhadamanthus, which **Aristotle** citeth:

> ***Εἴκε πάθοι τάδ' ἔρεξε, δίκη τ' ἰθεῖα γένοιτο*** (If a man should suffer the things which he hath done, straight justice would be done).

And:

> ***Ὡς ἀπόλοιτο καὶ ἄλλος, ὅστις τοιαῦταγε ῥέζοι*** (So perish also any other, whosoever shall do such things).

Likewise:

> ***Στεῖχε δίκης ἄσσον, μάλα τοι κακὸν ἄνδρασιν ὕβρις*** (Walk nearer unto justice; a very evil thing unto men is insolence), etc.

<!-- p.358 -->

The Second cause: Rewards, which are set before honesty and innocency of life in outward discipline, such as are tranquillity of mind, gladness of conscience, and glory among them that judge rightly, of which it is said: *"An honest report is a second patrimony."* And concerning the mind conscious of right there are everywhere many excellent sentences, as that of Cephalus in **Plato**: ***τῷ μηδὲν ἑαυτῷ ἄδικον ξυνειδότι, ἡδεῖα ἐλπὶς ἀεὶ πάρεστι, καὶ ἀγαθὴ γηροτρόφος*** (To him that is conscious to himself of no unjust deed sweet hope is ever present, and a good nurse of old age). And notable is that saying of **Cicero**: *"Virtue hath no theatre greater than conscience."*

The Third cause is reverence and gratitude toward other men: the Aged, Parents, Teachers, the Magistrate, Priests, Children, and the female sex. **Juvenal**, *Sat.* 13:

> They believed this a great impiety, and one to be atoned for by death,  
> If a young man had not risen up before an old one, and if  
> A boy before any bearded man, though he himself beheld  
> More corn at home, and heaps of greater acorns.  
> So venerable was it to go before by four years,  
> And the first down so equal unto sacred old age.

And **Cornelius Gallus**:

> Let gravity, let old age itself be venerable unto thee;  
> Let that be so which thou knowest thou dost desire, to live long.

Concerning reverence toward Parents, the verses of **Orpheus** ought to be known:

> It is Jupiter who guardeth the sacred rights of parents,  
> And marketh the wicked, who desire not from the heart  
> To hearken to their parents, and punisheth them with bitter furies:  
> But to the gentle he giveth both wealth and a mild lot.  
> For as the curse pronounced by parents is vehement,  
> So unto godly offspring the blessing of the Fathers is welcome.

Concerning the reverence due to children **Juvenal** saith:

> The greatest reverence is owed unto a boy: if aught  
> Shameful thou preparest, despise not thou the years of the boy,  
> But let thine infant son withstand thee when thou art about to sin.

<!-- p.359 -->

The same:

> The tender must be spared; not yet have there filled their marrow  
> The evils of inborn wickedness.

Concerning the reverence due to Teachers, known is the sentence of **Aristotle**: Unto the Gods, Parents and Teachers there is never rendered ***τιμὴ ἰσόῤῥοπος***, an equivalent honour or price. And Alexander so greatly revered **Aristotle**, that he said he owed no less to him than to his parents, because from his father he had received only the beginning of living, but from his Teacher the beginning of living well and rightly. And **Juvenal** saith: *"Our forefathers would have the Teacher to be in the place of a sacred parent."* For Teachers are the parents not of bodies, but of souls, saith **Quintilian**.

Now let the Studious gather by private diligence places, sentences and examples of the reverence owed to other men, and transfer them unto use, and let them be modest, humble, patient, meek. And that they may be able to render this reverence, let these things be considered: **I.** That we are by nature bound unto human society, and that therefore, publicly and privately, regard is to be had of the neighbour with whom we live. **II.** That each one of us, if we were in the place of those whom we ought to reverence — Rulers, Parents, Teachers — would desire that the due obedience, reverence and gratitude should be rendered unto us, and should not without grief and indignation suffer the violation of reverence and discipline. **III.** That we could not be safe even for one moment, if all men should put on fierceness. **IIII.** That no man rightly enjoyeth the virtue and modesty of others, if he himself also be not studious of virtue and modesty: for what doth a swine among sheep? **V.** That every man ought to set before himself the examples of honest men, after whose leading his life, and his reverence toward others, is to be framed, even as Evander saith unto Aeneas concerning his son:

> — Under thee as master to endure  
> Warfare and the heavy work of Mars, to behold thy deeds  
> Let him grow wont, and let him marvel at thee from his first years.

The Fourth cause is the very majesty and dignity of virtue, which shineth forth in the agreement of actions with the judgement of right reason, <!-- p.360 --> as **Cicero** saith: *"That which is honourable, although it be not made renowned, or though it be praised of no man, is by nature praiseworthy. And if it were beheld with the eyes, it would stir up marvellous loves of wisdom."* For by the excellency and goodness of virtue men of sound mind rise up in honour unto virtue, although the notions (*notitiae*) are often overthrown by the turbid motions of the affections, and men are made drunk with the taste of vices. And celebrated is the saying of **Isocrates**: ***Ἀρετῆς οὐδὲν κτῆμα σεμνότερον οὐδὲ βεβαιότερον ἐστὶ*** (No possession is more august or more sure than virtue). And of **Herodian**: ***Τὰ μὲν τῆς τύχης δῶρα καὶ ἀναξίοις περιπίπτει, ἡ δὲ τῆς ψυχῆς ἀρετὴ ἰδίαν ἑκάστῳ δόξαν περιτίθησιν*** (The gifts of fortune fall even unto the unworthy, but the virtue of the soul putteth upon each man a glory that is his own).

These causes the Sacred Scriptures do not disapprove, but adorn, unfold and illustrate them; as Job saith concerning punishments, chap. **9**: *"I feared all my works, knowing that Thou wouldest not spare the offender."* And Paul, **Eph. 5**: *"No whoremonger, nor unclean person, shall have any inheritance in the Kingdom of Christ."* Concerning rewards it is written in the former Epistle to **Timothy**, the fourth chapter: *"Godliness is profitable unto all things, having promise of the life that now is, and of that which is to come."* Concerning Reverence and Gratitude, the doctrine of the Church delivereth precepts and admonitions. The beauty of virtue it illustrateth much more truly than any Philosophical disputation. Moreover it addeth many other impelling reasons, more weighty, of which six are the chief:

The First is: the Commandment of God, which is no constraint of small account, but God Himself with His own mouth speaketh unto thee — not an Emperor or some Prince, but God, of whom Isaiah saith: *"Hear, O heavens, and give ear, O earth: for the Lord hath spoken."*

The Second is the end of creation, of Redemption and of Sanctification. Wherefore wast thou made a man? was it that thou mightest enjoy free will in all thine actions without Law? Wherefore wast thou redeemed with the blood of Christ? Wherefore art thou endowed with the Holy Spirit? that thou mightest be an *alastor*?[^P17-7] By no means; but that thou mightest obey God and His word, as **Ps. 119** teacheth: *"Blessed are the undefiled in the way, who walk in the Law of the Lord."*

The Third is the remembrance of the Divine mercy, which shineth both in the other benefits wherewith God crowneth and loadeth us, <!-- p.361 --> and most of all in the Remission of sins. He giveth not only sound members, food, raiment and peace, but also daily and every moment remitteth sins. Therefore Paul saith, **Rom. 12**: *"I beseech you by the mercies of God, that ye present your bodies a living sacrifice, holy, acceptable unto God."* And if in any man there abideth a spark of the remembrance of the benefits of God, he assuredly studieth godliness. It is reported of the Landgrave Lewis the Sixth, that when he had turned in to lodge with another Prince, an ungodly and unclean man, he found in the chamber a wench suborned with harlot's arts, and, having seen her, said unto his servant: *"Lead her out; for although it were no sin, yet I would not do this, for the love of my wife."* And Joseph useth the same arguments against his Mistress.
