Let these things be briefly said of anger, to which oftentimes even good men are subject, as Theodosius, whom **Ambrose** counselled that he should utter no word in anger before he had recited the Greek Alphabet. The same did **Athenodorus** advise Augustus. The history of Maurice is known, which is surely worthy to be often considered, by reason of the memorable saying which Maurice uttered at the slaughter of his two sons, his three daughters, and his wife Constantina, the daughter of the good Prince Tiberius: *"Righteous art thou, O Lord, and righteous is thy judgment"*: which is the same with this: *"O Lord, righteousness belongeth unto thee, but unto us confusion of faces."* Now let us return to the Commandment.

### *Non occides* · Thou Shalt Not Kill

Private revenge (*vindicta privata*) is forbidden, according to the sayings: *"He that taketh the sword shall perish with the sword." "Whoso sheddeth man's blood, by man shall his blood be shed." "Vengeance is mine; I will repay." "If ye forgive not, neither will my heavenly Father forgive you." "He shall have judgment without mercy, <!-- p.183 --> that hath shewed no mercy." "With what measure ye mete, it shall be measured to you again,"* etc.

The Jurists make homicide twofold, namely, parricide, and homicide in the special sense (*homicidium in specie*). Parricide is, when a man hath slain any one of his ascendants or descendants, or of his nearest collaterals, as father, mother, son, daughter, etc. And the punishment of the slayer is appointed according to the Pompeian Law, that, being sewn up in a sack with a dog and a dunghill cock, a viper and an ape, he be cast into the neighbouring sea or river, that so he may be deprived of the use of all the elements, while yet living and when dead. And the viper is added, because its young, when it is come to ripeness, having gnawed and eaten through the sides of the mother's womb, breaketh forth, and is brought to birth by the killing of its mother. But homicide in the special sense is, when a man slayeth him who is not joined to him by blood: and it is done in three ways: either by chance, or of set purpose, or by accident. The punishment of homicide by chance is penance. Now homicide is said to be done of set purpose, when a man of principal intent setteth upon another to kill him, or killeth one whom he could have spared. So the homicide is delivered to the Magistrate. By accident homicide is done, as in Hippodromes, in tournaments, in a duel, or in shooting with arrows, etc.

But this is now to be considered by us, that by the fifth Commandment not only is the slaying of others forbidden, but it is also commanded that we be not a hindrance and a hurt unto ourselves, nor injure our own bodies with cares, with night-studies, with Venus, and with drunkenness, whereby it cometh to pass that we must die before our day, who are the executioners of our own bodies. And here we must speak chiefly of drunkenness, which hath so far prevailed, that today it lacketh even the name of sin and vice, and is found everywhere among those who ought to excel in dignity, virtue and authority, who are called valiant, magnificent, mighty, illustrious, add also, holy and learned. Now the causes which ought to draw us away from this poison we will recite, and we pray God that this our admonition be not altogether unprofitable, and vanish not vainly into the air.

<!-- p.184 -->

The first cause is the most severe commandment of God, **Isa. 5**: *"Woe unto you that rise up early in the morning to drink, and sit until deep night,"* etc. **Luke 21**: *"Take heed, lest your hearts be overcharged with surfeiting."* **Eph. 5**: *"Be not drunk with wine,"* for thence ariseth ***ἀσωτία***, that is, a Cyclopic and beastly life. **Rom. 13**: *"Have care of the body, not unto concupiscences,"* that is, not to the fulfilling of inordinate desires and lusts.

**II.** The threatening of punishments in this life and in the life to come. **Isa. 5**: *"My people shall be led away unawares, and their honourable men shall be famished, and the multitude shall thirst."* This is the temporal punishment, which the Turks shall one day execute upon us. Now the eternal punishment also is expressed: *"Therefore hell hath enlarged her soul wide, and opened her mouth without measure, that there may be thrust down both the honourable and the multitude, the rich and the merry. Woe unto them that are mighty to drink wine, and wage wars in drunkenness."* This voice of God, *"Woe unto them,"* is not to be despised. **1 Cor. 6**: *"Such shall not possess the kingdom of God."*

**III.** The invocation of God is hindered, than which we have nothing more excellent, nor of more present help, in every calamity and in our whole life. The drunkard withdraweth himself from God, and delivereth himself to the Devil, driveth away the good angels, who ought to be the keepers of his life and actions, and despiseth the invocation of God, that is, he is plainly ungodly, even as **Ps. 53**, when it would mark out the most ungodly of men, saith: *"But they call not upon the Lord."*

**IIII.** A man loseth all his reason. **Prov. 20**: *"Wine maketh men foolish."* **Sirach 32**: *"It maketh fools more foolish,"* and fasteneth to the ground a particle of the divine breath. Examples everywhere, in courts and elsewhere, are too well known.

**V.** Thence arise manifold diseases, and untimely death. **Prov. 23**: *"Where is woe? where is sorrow? where are contentions? where are wounds, trembling, weakness of eyes? In revellings,"* etc. Hence is *crapula* (surfeit) so called, from ***κάρα πάλλειν***, from the shaking of the head. And the Greek verses are well known:

<!-- p.185 -->

> ***Λυσιμελοῦς βάκχου καὶ λυσιμελοῦς ἀφροδίτης***  
> ***Γεννᾶται θυγάτηρ λυσιμελὴς ποδάγρα.***  
> (Of limb-loosening Bacchus and limb-loosening Aphrodite  
> Is born a daughter, limb-loosening Gout.)  

Likewise:

> ***Οἶνος καὶ τὰ λοετρὰ καὶ ἡ περὶ κύπριν ἐρωὴ,***  
> ***Ὀξυτέρην πέμπει τὴν ὁδὸν εἰς ἀΐδην.***  
> (Wine, and the baths, and the eager pursuit of Cypris,  
> Send a man by the swifter road to Hades.)  

Often are drunkards found in their beds, dead. Promachus dieth after three days, when by drinking he had won a golden crown, as victor, from Alexander the Great.

**VI.** Every moment we look for death. Let us therefore seek the Lord while He may be found, lest death slay us in our sins, and cast us into eternal damnation.

**VII.** The offence which is given unto others, or evil example, **Matt. 18**: *"Woe to that man who giveth offence."* And this let Princes consider, Magistrates, Burgomasters, Householders, and many today of the pastors of the Church.

**VIII.** Drunkards abuse the gifts and the blessing of God, so that they are even brought to extreme poverty, as **D. Philip** most truly said of the Germans, which saying we have thus expressed:

> Her riches, her soul, her strength doth Germany lose,  
> Slackening the reins too far, unhappy, to her own riotings.  

**Ps. 104** showeth to what end wine was created, namely, that it may refresh and make glad the weary and afflicted. **Prov. 10**: *"The wicked useth his revenues to sin."* Nor is it doubtful that the creatures, wine, the fruits of the earth, and other things, cry out against us, who so abuse them to the dishonour of God, **Rom. 10**:[^P09-1] *"They groan and travail, as a woman in childbirth."*

**IX.** Drunkenness is also the root of all other sins and wickednesses, unto which the Devil driveth the secure and unwary. Hence the ancients rightly said: ***ὁ ἄσωτος ἄσωστος*** (the prodigal is past saving). And there is extant in **Augustine**, in the Sermons, an example very memorable, comprised in these verses:

> In the city wherein the holy father Augustine lived,  
> There was a citizen rich, and of excellent name.  
> <!-- p.186 --> One male offspring only had he,  
> Whom, when he went astray, the father could not correct.  
> Liberty being granted him, this son  
> Committeth what wickedness he will, his father looking on.  
> Drunken, alas, he runneth his own father through, before  
> His mother's sight, and savagely slayeth him with the sword.  
> "Spare thy father, I pray thee," the mother, great with child,  
> Saith, and at the word she also straightway falleth.  
> He goeth on, and woundeth both his sisters unto death,  
> And another (a thing unspeakable) he seeketh to overcome by force.  
> O drunkenness, fountain of crimes, and sea of evils,  
> O field and ample harvest of sins, etc.  

### *Quaestio* · Question

**Whether the Senate of Geneva did rightly, in putting to death Servetus the heretic.**

**Answer.**

**I.** The Magistrate ought to be the keeper both of the first and of the second Table, and the defender not of bodies only, but of the soul also, so far as pertaineth to outward hurts which are manifest.

**II.** And unto the Magistrate pertaineth judgement not only of bodily things, but of spiritual also, as the examples in the Sacred Scriptures, of David, of Solomon, and thereafter of Constantine, Theodosius, etc., do show.

**III.** Let the Magistrate therefore take care that the true doctrine be delivered in his dominions and cities, and let him abolish idolatries, errors and heresies, and take away the authors and spreaders of them.

**IIII.** But if heretics manifestly convicted, when they are admonished and thoroughly instructed, will not obey, the Magistrate doth rightly, if he take them out of the way. For if they are to be taken away <!-- p.187 --> who hurt bodies, much more are they to be taken away who corrupt the salvation of souls.

**V.** The Magistrate therefore hath power to take away false teachers, or to restrain them, if they be teachable, and if they can be bent. And so the Senate of Geneva did rightly, in that it took away Servetus, who could not be instructed and bent.

And so Valentinus Gentilis was rightly put to death, and at Heidelberg Sylvanus, and elsewhere others like them have been restrained by godly magistrates even with bodily chastisement, that they might not scatter their poison, nor be able to infect Churches and Schools with their venom.

**VI.** But let the Magistrate be not unskilled in doctrine, nor given over to pleasures, but let him diligently learn the doctrine himself, and understand the Articles, read, hear, prove, etc.

### *Quaestio* · Question

**Whether the Magistrate hath power to put to death those who kill wild beasts.**

Seeing that wild beasts are no man's own, neither the subjects' nor the Magistrate's, by natural Right and Law, and yet the Magistrate hath today claimed them for himself, perchance that he may have a recreation from his labours, and hath been able to do this of his own right, being moved especially by examples, as is evident from Daniel, who ascribeth to King Nebuchadnezzar a right over the fowls and the wild beasts: subjects ought not on that account to be angry with their Magistrate, or to envy him this prerogative, such as it is. And so we grant at length that the wild beasts are by custom left to the Magistrate.

And seeing that Magistrates defend their Subjects from the wild beasts, lest the goods of the Subjects, their fields and their grounds, be spoiled, the less complaint is to be made of this prerogative of the Magistrate. But since the Magistrate willeth that wild beasts be not killed by the subjects save by his own grant, it is asked, if any man transgress this edict, whether he be guilty of a capital crime, whether he have committed theft, whether he is to be hanged or beheaded?

<!-- p.188 -->

To this Question answer is made either out of ancient Right and Law, or out of custom, or out of the new edict of Princes: and these two latter do today concur, and have the appearance of a true constitution. For of ancient Law, especially among the Greeks, concerning wild beasts there was none. The Greeks even granted leave to kill them, as enemies, as in the ***περὶ νόμων καὶ ἐθῶν*** (On Laws and Customs) this law is recited: *"Concerning the slaying of living creatures, which are to be killed and which are not to be killed, the matter standeth thus: Those which do injury, or seek to do it, whosoever shall kill them shall be guiltless; nay, what is more, such are rather to be slain than not. All are to be put to death with all diligence which inflict injury contrary to right, and he who shall kill them, he, whether for his own desire's sake or for his right's, ought in the whole world to have the better part of confidence and possession, and the privilege,"* etc. This matter therefore standeth chiefly in prescription and in the edict of Princes, which, as we have said, is not to be denied them nor altered, if it can be done and borne without hurt of the Subjects.

Now when any of the Subjects transgresseth this edict, he doth it either by chance, or by necessity, or of his will, or out of contempt of the Law and edict. If he doth it by chance, no punishment can be appointed; if constrained by necessity, whether of defence or by poverty, what ought to be determined all men of sound mind understand: for defence is just and lawful, as well against men as against wild beasts. But poverty requireth the highest ***ἐπιείκειαν*** (equity), which becometh godly and honourable Princes, who ought of right to succour the poor and needy, lest it be needful for them, if the Princes will have their wild beasts to be safe from others, to slaughter these for the driving away of hunger. If he doth it for pleasure, seeing that by this Law it is forbidden, it is all one, if knowingly and willingly he transgress the edict, as if out of contempt of the Law he obey not. Here therefore, after the deed, he is either to be admonished to abstain, or to be amerced with a fine in money: but if he abstain not, now hath he not harmed the wild beasts, but he despiseth the Law of the Magistrate, and so the matter is drawn away from the wild beasts to the contempt of his Magistrate, and to contumacy. <!-- p.189 --> And so by Law the punishment is appointed, either banishment and expulsion from his goods and from the dominion of the Magistrate, or imprisonment. For of the extreme punishment, and of beheading, the Laws determine nothing; which also pronounce it to be a wrong, to forbid huntings by the threatening of capital punishments: *d. C. Non est*. And the Saxon laws, book 2, article 28, exact three Groats, or Shillings, as they call them, for a wild beast killed. And the Frankish laws appoint a punishment in money. And there is extant another Law of the Jurists: *"If the lord of a place, where hunting is free to all, have the right of the Commonwealth in that same place, and forbid all men, under a penalty proclaimed, to hunt there, the transgressor of the prohibition shall be liable to punishment."*

But as to that which is objected, that it is theft: let it by all means be theft also, if a thing otherwise free by its own nature, and assured to no man, but open to all, be now made the possession of the Magistrate. Yet there is no doubt but that godly Princes consider this, and do not lightly, for the sake of their own pleasures, inflict a punishment greater than the offence.

But that today it is done otherwise, and that there is great ***ἀταξία*** (disorder) in these and other things, which bringeth forth great griefs to many good men, this must be committed unto God, and the causes are to be acknowledged of the public punishments, which roam everywhere, in great blindness, through Magistrates and Subjects. And let these things be said most briefly concerning these matters.

# *De VI. Praecepto* · The Sixth Commandment

### *Non moechaberis* · Thou Shalt Not Commit Adultery

The Sixth Commandment approveth marriages, and conjugal cohabitation. Next, it forbiddeth all wandering copulations, and forbiddeth not only outward deeds, but also impure affections, and the burnings of minds, as Christ <!-- p.190 --> interpreteth: *"Whosoever looketh on a woman to lust after her hath committed adultery already,"* **Matt. 5**.

In the beginning, therefore, we must speak of Marriage.

I. Who instituted marriage.

II. What is to be done by those who either would contract marriage, or do live in marriage.

III. Whether divorce can and ought to be made.

### *De Primo* · On the First Point

Here the chief doctrine concerning marriage is to be repeated. And as touching the author, or efficient cause of marriage, we know that there is no other institutor thereof, save God alone, **Gen. 1**: *"God created male and female, and blessed them, and said: Be fruitful, and multiply."* *Elohim*, that is, Gods, created male and female, to show the institution and commandment of the whole Trinity. For not *Eloha* in the singular, but *Elohim* in the plural is said, yet with a singular word set to it, namely, the verb "made," or "created"; as also, after Eve was made of the rib of the man, it is written: *"And Elohim brought Eve unto Adam,"* that is, the whole Trinity doth the office of the bridesman (*paranymphus*); and it is the Father who leadeth the married pair to the altar, as a godly father leadeth his son to the altar: the Son of God is the high Priest, betrothing the married pair, and blessing them. The Holy Spirit coupleth them with mutual love, maketh them fruitful, and preserveth them. And the allegory is worthy of consideration: The man representeth the divine nature in Christ: the woman denoteth the human nature in Christ. These two natures are coupled by the hypostatic union, as the ancients in the Church have spoken.

Now since it is established concerning so great an author of marriage, let us begin to reverence the conjugal life and chastity, and let us know that not by political men, not by Emperors, Kings, Princes, or others, but by the one only God, hath this most sacred kind of life been instituted, and let us ask of God that in <!-- p.191 --> this kind of life He would mercifully rule, defend and teach us. Let us consider also the other honours, dignities, and ends of marriage, of which we will speak most briefly.

The first dignity is, as we have already said, that GOD with wonderful wisdom Himself instituted and established marriage, and that in Paradise, or the garden of Eden, which God Himself had planted, and that He most severely commandeth all men who have not the gift of continence, that they keep the Laws of chaste wedlock.

The second dignity is, that in this kind of life many things are learned concerning GOD, what manner of God He is, that He is chaste, that He is a lover of His own, that He neglecteth not the wretched lump of mankind, though it be squalid, and defiled with much filthiness, infirm and weak, even as a father and mother cast not away an infirm and squalid offspring, but the more infirm it is, the more they labour to bring it help. Likewise: that God the Father receiveth us for the sake of the Son the Mediator, even as antiquity set forth and presented its sons before Kings and Princes, when it would become a suppliant, as is evident also from **Thucydides**. And there are very many other exercises of righteousness, of truth, of beneficence, of patience, and of many other Virtues in this estate of life, so that **Stigelius** hath most truly said in his verse:

> Marriage is the divine Academy of human life.  

The third dignity is, that out of this kind of life is born the nursery of the Church of God, which both in this life and in all eternity may rightly acknowledge and celebrate God. This is a great consolation to parents, that they know that their offspring, if only it hath been washed from guilt by the blood of the Son of God in Baptism, is already a member of the eternal Church of God, whether it live, or whether it die early. And here let all Monks and Mass-priests be confounded, who condemn this kind of life, and would have the fruits of the Church of God diminished, and offer unto GOD not true-born and lawful, but bastard ones, as **Pighius** most audaciously writeth: That a Priest defiling himself with fornication sinneth less <!-- p.192 --> than one living in marriage. Such gnostics let us abhor and condemn, and rather let us hear the Heathen, who dispute more holily of marriage, among whom **Plato** hath most honourably written, that every man ought to take a wife, that he may always leave after him a worshipper unto God, according to the Law of God.

The fourth dignity is, that we know that all the Patriarchs and Prophets lived in this kind of life: Adam, Enoch, Noah, Abraham, Isaac, Jacob, Joseph, Moses, Isaiah, Hosea, Zechariah, Ezekiel, Daniel, Jeremiah excepted, to whom it was forbidden by a singular and special commandment to take a wife, **Jer. 16**, namely, by reason of the devastation and captivity of the people that was to come. *"For I will lay all things waste, saith the Lord."* Lest therefore Jeremiah be made twice wretched in the time of the captivity, marriage is forbidden him, even as Christ saith, **Luke 23**, that the Jewish women in the Roman captivity should say: *"Blessed are the barren, and the wombs that never bare, and the paps which never gave suck."* For how great a misery it is, to wander about hither and thither in wars and other perils with children, with a wife, and scarce to have wherewith the body may be covered, is to be considered:

> ***Μεγάλη τυραννὶς ἀνδρὶ τέκνα καὶ γυνή.***  
> (A great tyranny unto a man are children and a wife.)  

saith **Euripides**. And that this is the meaning of Jeremiah, the context of the discourse showeth, against all monks and mass-priests. **Ignatius** also writeth, that the most part of the Apostles, though they were true Bishops, were married. That Peter lived in marriage is certain, seeing that Christ healed his wife's mother of a fever, **Matt. 8**. And **Clement of Alexandria** writeth, that the wife of Peter was led to execution, and was strengthened by the voice of her husband, who bade her remember the Lord. If this be true, it seemeth to have been done about that time at which Mary is reported by **Nicephorus** to have departed, being in the fifty-ninth year of her age, in the time of Claudius. Clement also relateth, concerning the forbidding of marriage, that the heretics alleged a conversation of Christ and Salome, which was written in the Gospel of the Egyptians. To Salome asking when <!-- p.193 --> the Kingdom of God should come, Christ answereth: WHEN THE WORK OF THE WOMAN SHALL CEASE. Thence the heretics argued, that marriages should cease after that Christ was born, and the preaching of the Gospel was now begun; whereas Christ nameth the work of the woman, not generation, which is a work of God, ordained to be done by the coupling of male and female, but death and sin, the work of Eve, etc.

The same Clement understandeth that place of Paul, **Phil. 4**, ***σύζυγε γνήσιε*** (true yokefellow), certainly of the wife of the Apostle Paul. And that Luke the Evangelist was married is well established. And this is no small dignity of marriage, that married persons know that they are conversant in such a kind of life as very many Saints have lived in, Patriarchs, Prophets, Apostles, and that this kind of life is not new, not devised by men, but instituted by God 5522 years ago. And here again let the monks and mass-priests be confounded, who forbid marriage to priests, and yet name matrimony a Sacrament. *"Marriage is honourable,"* **Heb. 13**. And: *"Let a Bishop be the husband of one wife,"* **1 Tim. 3** and **Tit. 1**. And it is a doctrine of devils, if any man forbiddeth to marry.

The fifth dignity is, that in this kind of life a remedy is found against wandering lusts, and that the punishments are avoided which by an unchangeable order accompany wandering lusts. So Paul saith in the former to the Corinthians, the seventh chapter: *"To avoid fornication, let every man have his own wife, and let every woman have her own husband."* And **Heb.**, the thirteenth: *"Whoremongers and adulterers God will punish,"* as the examples do show: of the flood, **Gen. 6. 7**;[^P09-2] of the burning of Sodom, **Gen. 19**; of the citizens of Shechem that were slain, **Gen. 34**; of the 23000 slaughtered in the wilderness, **Num. 25**; of the tribe of Benjamin destroyed, **Judg. 20**; of the exile of David, **2 Kings 11. 15**;[^P09-3] of the Babylonian captivity; of the Sons of Eli, **1 Kings 3**; of Samson, **Judg. 16**; of Solomon, **3 Kings 11**. And the histories of other nations also do show the punishments of wickednesses of this kind, as Troy was overthrown on account of Helen, the wife of Menelaus, carried off by Paris the son of Priam. <!-- p.194 --> The whole Roman Commonwealth was changed on account of Lucretia, forced by Sextus the King's Son. Afterward, on account of the wickedness of Appius, who attempted to carry off the daughter of Virginius, the Commonwealth was changed again. **Plutarch** relateth that two maidens were ravished by force by Laconian young men, then slain, and cast into a well, who had been the daughters of Scedasus at Leuctra in Boeotia: but afterward, in the battle of Leuctra, the Lacedaemonians were conquered and destroyed at the tombs of those maidens, that it might appear that God avengeth those ravishings. This history, which is diligently to be considered, is extant in **Plutarch**, in the life of Pelopidas, and in **Pausanias**, in the *Boeotica*.

More examples of punishments are extant everywhere in the histories. Miletus, Sybaris, Athens and other Greek cities, and lastly Rome, were overthrown on account of lusts. Pausanias the Spartan, on account of a maiden carried off and slain at Byzantium, was afterward killed by hunger in a temple, to which his mother had been the first to bring a stone, that he might be shut in and killed; and an image, for several nights together without intermission, a little after the wickedness was committed, recited unto him this verse:

> ***Στεῖχε δίκης ἆσσον, μάλα τοι κακὸν ἀνδράσιν ὕβρις.***  
> Thou must pay the penalty; wrong-doing bringeth harm.  

But I cease to recite more examples, of which experience also exhibiteth too many. And I hasten to the remaining dignities of marriage.

The sixth dignity: **Deut. 24**. God Himself commandeth that they who have lately contracted matrimony be burdened with no public burdens for the space of a whole year, nor go forth to war, but that they remain at home free and glad, and grow accustomed one to another, and live in concord, and without jealousy, until, being confirmed, their minds knit together, they may at length be able to be absent without jealousy.

The seventh dignity is, that the Son of God was not born of a virgin simply, but of the virgin Mary espoused to a man, Joseph, whose betrothed and true wife she was. For the Sacred Scriptures name them wives who are not yet known of their husbands, <!-- p.195 --> but are betrothed, being yet virgins, **Deut. 22**. And worthy of consideration is the explanation of **Basil**, who in the Sermon ***εἰς τὴν ἁγίαν τοῦ Χριστοῦ γέννησιν*** (On the Holy Nativity of Christ) saith that Christ was born of an espoused virgin for these causes: **I.** That both virginity and marriage might be honoured by this nativity, and that so God might show that both kinds of life are approved of Him. **II.** That Mary might have Joseph for a household witness of her virgin chastity. **III.** That the Messiah, being born, might not be recognised by the Prince of the world, the Devil. For whereas the Devil knew that the Messiah was to be born of a virgin, according to the prophecy of Isaiah, and that it was now the time of the nativity, the weeks of Daniel being run out, he was made uncertain whether she could be the mother of the Messiah who was betrothed to a man, and so was constrained to doubt both of the mother of the Messiah and of the Messiah Himself, so that he had nothing certain. Now let it be a great consolation to married persons, that they know that the Son of God was, according to the Law, born in wedlock; and let the same consolation be also to chaste young men and virgins.

The eighth dignity is: that the Son of God, being thirty years old, with His first work and miracle honoureth the conjugal life in Cana of Galilee, while He is Himself present at the marriage, together with His mother Mary, and with His disciples. **Bonaventure** thinketh that John the Evangelist was the bridegroom, and Mary Magdalene the bride. But **Nicephorus** judgeth that the bridegroom was Simon the Canaanite, the son of Mary of James, who was the sister of Mary the mother of Christ. But howsoever it be, it is certain that Mary the mother of the Lord was kin to the bridal pair, seeing that she is herself present as the provider and administrator of the things necessary. Nor is Christ only present, but He presenteth the marriage with no small gift, while He showeth forth the power of His divinity, turning water into wine. One *metreta* containeth thirty-six measures, or cans. Twelve *metretae* therefore held four hundred and thirty-two measures, that is, eight *amphorae*, which today we name *Eimer* (pails), and eight cans or measures. This reckoning is true, if one water-pot contained two *metretae*. For if three, the number groweth <!-- p.196 --> to 1244 cans.[^P09-4] And that the wine was excellent there is no doubt, as is evident from the words of the Governor of the feast; without doubt red, as is also sung in the Hymn of the three Kings:

> The water-pots of water blush red,  

And the holy land brought forth chiefly red wine, as it were of the colour of blood, even as for that cause red wine is called blood, **Gen. 49**. These things we explain to the boys, by whom such things are to be noted. But the consolation for godly married persons is assuredly great, against all the difficulties of marriage, seeing that Christ giveth wine to the bridegroom, that is, through the whole time of their marriage He helpeth them, and driveth away the Devil, by whom chiefly the troubles of wedlock are increased. It is true indeed that there are many and great difficulties in marriage, even as for that cause the Heathen have uttered many sentences concerning the troubles and calamities of marriage, as is this sentence:

> ***Ἀθάνατόν ἐστι κακὸν ἀναγκαῖον γυνή.***  
> (A woman is an immortal, necessary evil.)  

And the verses of **Hipponax** are:

> ***Δύ' ἡμέραι γυναικὸς εἰσὶν ἥδισται,***  
> ***Ὅταν γαμῇ τὶς, κἀκφέρει τεθνηκῦαν.***  
> (Two days of a woman are the sweetest:  
> When a man marrieth her, and when he carrieth her out dead.)  

Likewise:

> He that is bound in wedlock is no longer free;  
> I have married a wife, I have sold my liberty.  

And such sayings are many, especially among the common sort, with the secure and the ungodly. But let the godly know that God is present in their marriage, and preserveth the marriage, and that, as the other afflictions in life, so most of all marriage is made a School for the exercising of faith, of invocation, of confession, of obedience, of love toward the neighbour and toward the Commonwealth.

But above all let this be the consolation, that godly married persons know that they are cared for by Christ, and that He willeth to be at the wedding, and in the marriage, and to be the provider of things necessary, of food and raiment, so that He will even change water into wine before He suffer them to want, that is, He turneth their sorrow and difficulties into gladness, **Ps. 34**: *"The rich have wanted and suffered hunger: but they that seek the Lord <!-- p.197 --> shall not want any good thing."* And **Ps. 37**: *"I have been young, and now am old; yet have I not seen the righteous forsaken, nor his seed begging bread,"* etc.

Let there be considered also, by the way, the picture of the Church, which keepeth the marriage feast, teaching and confessing the doctrine of the Gospel in a village, Cana the lesser, in lower Galilee. Now *Cana* signifieth a reed, when written with *Kuph*, *Kaneh*. For in a reed-bed, that is, in most wretched and ruinous lodgings, in villages, and in Galilee, or in the borders and ends of the world, not in the very cities and castles, doth the Church keep her marriage. *Cana* signifieth also one subdued, made subject and oppressed, when written with *Caph*; and such is the Church in this life, exposed to many calamities, so that she seemeth to be neglected by her bridegroom Christ, nay, when she calleth upon Him, seemeth to be rejected, and not heard, as in this place Mary the mother of Christ. But at the last the best wine is given, which maketh glad the heart.

The ninth dignity of marriage is, that the mutual love between husband and wife is truly an image of the mutual love between Christ and the Church. As Paul interpreteth, **Eph. 6**:[^P09-5] *"Husbands, love your wives, even as Christ also loved the church, and gave himself for it,"* etc. *"This is a great mystery: but I speak concerning Christ and the church."* And that this image may be considered, the duties of the married are to be observed, namely, these six:

I. Mutual love, ardent, true, sweet and sincere.

II. Conjugal faith.

III. Fellowship, or the sharing of goods and of troubles.

IIII. Generation, or the conjugal duty.

V. Defence.

VI. ***ἐπιείκεια*** (forbearance), bearing with infirmities and blemishes for concord's sake.

First, let there be in marriage true friendship, without dissembling and constraint, without peevishness and disdain, without suspicion and *pleonexia* (overreaching), as between Isaac and Rebecca, Jacob and Rachel. So <!-- p.198 --> the Son of God loveth His Church as a bride, with a love ardent, sweet and sincere, so that He even undergoeth death for her. **Valerius Maximus** writeth of Gracchus that, two snakes having been taken in his house, a male and a female, when he had been certified by the soothsayer that, if he killed the female, death was at hand for his wife Cornelia, but if he slew the male, letting the female go, he himself must die, his wife surviving safe and sound, he commanded the male to be killed, and the female to be let go. He himself, three days being past, departed. Such is the Son of God toward His bride the Church.

Next, let there be conjugal Faith, whereby they rest in mutual love, and desire not other men or other women, even as doves are not coupled with more than one, nor forsake their union, unless the one become a widower or a widow. Therefore do doves draw the chariot of Venus. So the Son of God cleaveth unto His Church, heretics and the ungodly being shut out.

Afterward, let there be the fellowship of goods and of troubles, that the one be the help of the other, the staff of his labours, not an ***ἐμπόδιον***, an obstacle and hindrance, not ***ζυγομαχία*** (strife in the yoke), of which it is said: *"With unequal oxen the wain is never well drawn."* And let it be considered that wives are the companions of life, even as for that cause the wife was taken out of the side of the man, not out of the head, lest she be the mistress of the man, nor yet out of the feet, lest she be a bondmaid, but a companion, and that she may be a rib, which may endure labours, and help her husband. And let the history be known of the wives of Guelph the Sixth, and of the nobles besieged by the Emperor Conrad in the castle of *Winßberg* (Weinsberg). For when they were being driven by hunger to the surrender of the castle, and the wives asked of the Emperor that it might be granted them to carry out as much as each could bear upon her shoulders, and obtained it,

> Each beareth (how great is this devotion!) her own husband,  
> And holdeth in her embrace the dear pledges of the marriage-bed;  
> They go forth at the gate: the soldiery, roused from the camp,  
> Runneth together, and is amazed at the strangeness of the thing.  

The Emperor approveth the virtue of the matrons, and layeth aside his anger, thinking:

<!-- p.199 -->

> To spare the vanquished becometh Royal hearts.  

He joineth Guelph and the rest unto himself, and maketh them his chief friends, presenteth the matrons with ample gifts, and hearkeneth not unto others who counsel otherwise. So the Son of God communicateth unto us His goods, wisdom, righteousness, and life, and draweth off our troubles upon Himself, taketh us upon His shoulders, and carrieth us forth safe and sound out of the siege and damnation. *"He that toucheth you,"* saith He, *"toucheth the apple of mine eye." "I carry you in my womb; I will carry the Church also when she waxeth old."*

Fourthly, let there be generation, whereby mankind is propagated by a sure Law and order. Than this nothing is more excellent in life, seeing that out of it the Church of God groweth. It is read of Cornelia, the daughter of the Great Africanus, that when a certain noble matron had come to her, and in the midst of their talk had shown her own necklaces and ornaments, and had challenged Cornelia that she also should show the like, Cornelia, calling her ten children out of the schoolroom, showed them, and said: *"These are my ornaments, which I adorn, and esteem highly."* So the Son of God, of water and the spirit, begetteth the Church, and Sons unto His Father.

Fifthly, let there be defence. The bridegroom leadeth the bride from the altar[^P09-6] as a suppliant, to whom he promiseth defence. So the Son of God promiseth defence to His Church, **Hos. 1**: *"I will save you in the Lord God." "I will be with you even unto the end of the world." "No man shall pluck my sheep out of my hands."*

Sixthly, let there be ***ἐπιείκεια***. **1 Pet. 3**: *"Ye husbands, give unto the woman, as unto the weaker vessel, the honour that is due."* So the Son of God beareth with our infirmities, wipeth them away, forgiveth, burieth them. Let there not therefore be brawlings, hatreds, altercations. And let there be set before matrons the example of Monica, the mother of **Augustine**, who had a husband that was a Heathen, and harsh, who afterward, being moved by the dutifulness and virtue of his wife, was made a Christian. Monica also reproved other wives, who complained of the harshness of their husbands, and persuaded them that by patience and gentleness of manners they should overcome the harshness of their husbands.

<!-- p.200 -->

Let it now suffice to have said these things of the dignities of marriage. Now seeing that its dignities are so great, the Magistrate doth rightly in punishing adulterers and fornicators, without exception and difference. And to the severe punishing of this wickedness the Magistrate is bound by the divine voice; who, when he is over negligent, maketh himself a partaker both of the guilt and of the punishment, and at length that cometh to pass which **Hesiod** saith: *"Oftentimes a whole City or Country payeth the penalty of one evil man."* For God grievously punisheth sins, and negligence in punishments, or overmuch indulgence, and recompenseth the delay with the grievousness of the punishment.

Next, the Magistrate ought not to suffer public brothels. And in no wise is that Magistrate to be thought to be touched with care for the glory of God, and for the salvation of his subjects, or at the least for discipline and honesty of manners, who suffereth public brothels, howsoever, forsooth, that most shameful indulgence be excused.

The harlots stand in the sight of those who come as strangers from other parts, not without great offence. Likewise: in the sight of the youth, which, if these were away, would be less moved to enticements and snares, according to the saying known unto all: *"Objects move the senses"*; *"Opportunity maketh the thief"*; ***ἐκ τοῦ εἰσορᾶν γίγνεται τὸ ἐρᾶν*** (of looking cometh loving); *"Of the unknown there is no desire."* Likewise: they stand also in the open view of those who are either joined in matrimony, or are otherwise studious of honesty, who cannot bear this most shameful indulgence without great indignation toward their superiors, and do even call down I know not what evils, and demand punishments of God.

It is enough, and more than enough, that Princes and Magistrates must sometimes wink at secret fornication; I say nothing of public, which they not only permit to be done, but in many places do even nourish and cherish, and increase it by their maintenance, and honour it as it were with rewards and stipends. No marvel if even the whole neighbourhood round about the stews were corrupted, which is constrained to see fornicators going out and coming in by day and by night, as it is certain is also done in many places.

What? If either a wife, or a poor maiden, <!-- p.201 --> wretched, afflicted, toilsome and overwhelmed with calamities, seeth prostituted harlots rejoicing in, enjoying, and rioting in so great wantonness of the flesh, and idleness, who will not easily believe and grant, that she is very often invited either to renounce the poverty, labours and miseries which otherwise she is constrained to bear, or altogether, being overcome by the licence of such a life and of such pleasures, to give herself over unto this most abandoned kind of life?
