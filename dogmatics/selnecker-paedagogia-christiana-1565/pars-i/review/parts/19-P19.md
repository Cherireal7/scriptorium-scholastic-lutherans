These things being premised concerning the true end of man, and drawn out of the fountains of Israel, it may now easily be perceived what is to be judged and determined of the opinion of the Philosophers. For as the wisest of the Heathen have, taken all together, only certain small portions of the Law, and, as **Paul** speaketh, do not the Law itself, but the things contained in the Law, so in this Question also of the end of man they snatch out of the Law a small portion, which discourseth of virtue and honesty, chiefly outward, or <!-- p.382 --> consisting in outward actions; and according to the rule and measure of this small portion they bring forth opinions various, uncertain, maimed, yet in part honest, and agreeing with the judgement of sound and right reason. Among these the opinion of **Plato** is wont to be set in the first place. For although **Thales**, **Pythagoras**, **Democritus**, **Heraclitus**, **Empedocles**, **Hippocrates**, **Socrates** and others were famous before Plato, of whom it cannot be doubted that they discoursed of the chief good and of the last end of man, yet **Plato**, who flourished in the year of the world 3572, was the first that comprised his opinion of the chief good in certain writings which have come down unto us; and whereas, as **Justin Martyr** and **Clement of Alexandria** do witness, he had read the books of Moses, and made great account of the doctrine of the Egyptians, he followed a sounder and more religious opinion than the rest. And it is thus: The end of man is ***ὁμοιοῦσθαι θεῷ*** (to be made like unto God), and this is virtue, ***ὁμοιοῦσθαι θεῷ***, and the highest blessedness is ***ὁμοιοῦσθαι θεῷ***. And the end of blessedness is, to be and to become immortal as speedily as may be. For the object of wisdom, and of virtue, yea of the whole man and of all his actions, is God, in the contemplation of whom the study of virtue is occupied, and virtue itself and true blessedness do consist and rest. This opinion Plato nobly and clearly maintaineth, whether, as **Clement of Alexandria** saith in book 2 of the ***Στρομ.*** (*Stromata*), as in some sort concurring with the decree of the law (for great natures, and such as are free from perturbation of mind, are, I know not how, carried unto the mark of truth, as saith **Philo the Pythagorean**, describing the acts of Moses), or else as taught by some of the divine oracles which then were, being of them that were ever held with a thirst of learning. For the Law saith: *"Ye shall walk after the Lord your God, and keep my commandments."* And that which Plato calleth ***ἐξομοίωσιν***, a being made like (*assimilatio*), the Law nameth a walking, or a following after and agreement, ***ἀκολουθίαν***. And such an ***ἀκολουθία*** (following), so far as may be, maketh like. *"Be ye,"* saith the Lord, *"merciful, as your heavenly Father is merciful."* Now although this Platonic opinion is worthy of admiration, <!-- p.383 --> and without doubt was received from the Egyptians, as Plato hath many other things which savour of the doctrine of the Patriarchs, as of the ***λόγῳ*** (Word), by whom all things were made, and of the state of souls after this life dwelling with God, yet it is manifest that the kernel and ***τὸ ἔργον*** (the deed itself) is wanting in this opinion, seeing it is not at all shown who is the true God, and how man may come to the knowledge of him, and how he may be reconciled anew unto him whom in all his life he hath so grievously offended, and may obtain and keep his favour; for which cause many do think that Plato himself feigned the Idea, which should be a certain original and common nature, from which all other things do pluck certain small portions. But whether by the Idea Plato understood God himself, or a certain perfection and integrity of virtue, to be conceived and fashioned by the imagination of the mind, and abiding in the mind, according to the knowledge of the Law, wherewith a manner of living framed thereunto ought to be joined, yet nothing certain or solid can be determined of the true end of man, and of the highest blessedness, out of this opinion, religious, as it seemeth, and fair in show, which is legal only, and speaketh of an Idea, unless there be added the heavenly doctrine, which both showeth us God, and declareth the manner how we ought to be made like unto God; of which being made like it is written, **1 John 3**: *"We are the sons of God, and it doth not yet appear what we shall be: but we know that, when he shall appear, we shall be like him, and we shall see him as he is,"* that is, there shall shine in us a most firm knowledge of the whole Trinity, and there shall burn in us the highest love of GOD, of the Angels, and of the heavenly Church, and all other virtues perfect and absolute in all their numbers. This wisdom, and this choir of all virtues, is the likeness and image of God, which the Son of God restoreth in us, and concerning which the words of **Theodoret** are to be observed, who in the exposition of the saying of Paul to the **Philippians, 3**, of the bodies of the Saints which shall be ***συμμόρφοις*** (fashioned like) unto the glorious body of Christ, saith thus: *"The godly shall be partakers of incorruption and immortality and glorification, and <!-- p.384 --> although there shall be a difference in quantity, even so great as is between the Sun and the Stars, and between the lord and the servants, and between him that giveth light and him that receiveth light, yet in quality there shall be agreement and conformity,"* that is, there shall shine in their souls the image of God, glorious, majestical and excellent, and their minds shall be full of the wisdom of God, their affections most pure, their wills in all eternity most ready to render obedience unto God, and a full and perfect obedience shall be rendered unto God, and there shall be a full love of God and of our neighbour.

Now must we speak of the opinion of **Aristotle**, who determineth that virtue is the chief good, to be sought after and put forth by itself and for itself, although it be utterly destitute of all rewards, of glory, authority, honours, dignity and other things wherewith men are able to adorn honesty and the studies thereof, and in place of rewards suffer injuries, envy, slanders, banishments, and other kinds of calamities. This opinion is indeed honest, and, as touching outward actions, not in all points to be disallowed, but it is not sufficient, seeing many things which are required unto perfect blessedness are wanting in it. *[Margin: 1]* For first, Aristotle doth not refer the practice and study of virtue unto God himself, who alone is the chief good, as Plato, being taught by the writings of Moses, referred unto God all that which man is and is able to do. *[Margin: 2]* Next, he is ignorant of the original and perfection of virtue, neither is he able entirely to define any virtue, much less that whole choir of virtues, forasmuch as he is utterly ignorant what and of what sort original righteousness (*iustitia originalis*) was before the fall, and of what sort also after the fall true and entire virtue ought to be, if it should be approved of God. *[Margin: 3]* Afterward, he knoweth not whence the depravation arose, which although he doth in some sort acknowledge and confess, yet is he ignorant of the cause. By the consent indeed of all the sounder Philosophers there is never in man true virtue in this life, nay, there is scarce a shadow and a certain image of virtue, and nowhere can that ***κατόρθωμα*** (perfect right deed), which the Stoics do magnificently extol, be found; but whence the defect is, there is none of the Philosophers <!-- p.385 --> that is able to show. Fourthly, Aristotle, yea and all the Philosophers, are ignorant of the manner of the restoring, how through the Son of God the study and integrity of true virtue is by little and little begun in us in this life, the Holy Ghost being given. Fifthly, all are ignorant of the consummation and the entire *catorthoma*, which shall be in the life eternal. These are the chief things which are wanting in the opinion of Aristotle. And hereunto is added this also, that Aristotle can define nothing of perfect blessedness, forasmuch as blessedness comprehendeth the whole man, in such wise that he which is wretched in one part cannot be perfectly blessed. Seeing therefore that Aristotle saith that virtue, even when it is destitute of rewards, is yet able to give perfect blessedness unto itself and unto him that practiseth it, he is at strife with himself. For he that is destitute of rewards, and wandereth an exile, and is pressed down with injuries, cannot surely be perfectly blessed in this life, seeing blessedness comprehendeth the whole man, and not some part of man only. And let this most true Rule be held: Unto no thing can this glory be truly ascribed, that it maketh a man blessed on every part, unless that thing have five properties; *[Margin: I.]* whereof the first is, that the power of it reach unto the very essence of man, and abide therein. For the chief good must needs, as Aristotle himself affirmeth, be ***οἰκεῖον*** (a thing that is one's own), and not only hover without, as outward goods hover without, as riches, power, fortune, etc.; but it is necessary that that which is to be called the chief good do pierce into the very essence of man, as God is the chief good, because he doth impart himself unto us by the sending of the Son, and the giving of the Holy Ghost. *[Margin: II.]* The second property is: The highest blessedness ought to be proper and peculiar unto man above nature without life and nature brute. *[Margin: III.]* The third: Unto the chief good, as unto the last end, all things must be referred, but itself is to be referred no whither. *[Margin: IV.]* The fourth: The fruition of the chief good ought by its very perpetuity to satisfy the mind of man, which coveteth it with an unsatiable desire, and to be not only ***δυσαφαίρετος*** (hard to be taken away), as Aristotle nameth virtue, because it can hardly be <!-- p.386 --> plucked from a man against his will, and yet is sometimes overthrown, but ***ἀναφαίρετος*** (not to be taken away), so that it be not broken off. *[Margin: V.]* The fifth is: The chief good ought to place the whole man in the best estate, than which man can hold no better. These properties of the chief good being considered, it may easily be seen how much is lacking unto the Aristotelian opinion, which, although it were to be disallowed for no other cause, yet on this account can have no place in the assembly of the godly, that unto faint virtue, and, as we speak more plainly in the Church, unto our works, imperfect and manifoldly defiled, it challengeth and ascribeth the praise and glory of perfection, and of the highest blessedness, whereas notwithstanding it is certain that all our righteousnesses, to use the words of **Isaiah**, are as filthy rags, and that we are all unclean. Wherefore also the Son of God, casting down the Aristotelian opinion with one word, saith: *"When ye shall have done all those things which are commanded you, say, We are unprofitable servants."* This moreover is to be added, that Aristotle saith many things of virtue itself, that it is to be sought and practised by itself and for itself, although it have or receive no rewards, and yet showeth not, if virtue be destitute of rewards, how patience is to be rendered, what consolations are to be held, with what remedies the mind is to be furnished. Seeing he himself is ignorant of these things, he doth even as if a man should discourse much unto a sick man of health and the good estate of the body, or should hold a speech unto old women of the flower of youth, but how good health is to be recovered, and how the disease is to be healed, should keep silence, or should himself not be able to know and to tell.

There remaineth now **Epicurus**, who would not go into the opinion of Aristotle, who a little before had said that virtue is the chief good, although it should lack all rewards, yea and all pleasure of the body and all outward goods; but he affirmed that pleasure itself of the mind and of the body is the chief good and the end of man. This opinion some have praised, some have disallowed and rejected. And there have been some who, considering the words of **Moses** concerning man created, and <!-- p.387 --> placed in the garden of Eden, or the Paradise of pleasure, thought it not absurd to determine that pleasure, which is the agreement of a righteous action and of nature as it was made of God, and is a gladness or sweet motion of the heart, which the whole man feeleth resting in God, and in good things, is and is called the end of man. For *Eden*, as a common noun, signifieth pleasure and delights, whence the Greek word ***ἡδονή*** (pleasure) seemeth to have sprung. But although this form of speaking of pleasure, as of the chief good, may after this manner in some sort be defended, yet man being cast out of the garden of Eden, and, as it is written of Cain, driven into the land of Nod, which is over against Eden, that is, cast for sin into miseries and calamities innumerable (for *Nod* signifieth tossings, waves, burdens and banishments), nothing can rightly be said of that pleasure, which was a part of the life and of original righteousness, and is utterly lost; much less can it be set in the first and highest place or degree of good things. Farewell therefore to this opinion. There be some who first show the reason why Epicurus would not keep the opinion of Aristotle, and then do not only excuse Epicurus, but also defend him, especially seeing Epicurus admitted pleasure none other, nor otherwise, than such as should agree with nature and should be the companion and reward of virtue, rejecting all mad lusts, and affirming that they do fight directly against pleasure and virtue, as **Cicero** and **Laertius** are witnesses. And why Epicurus would not approve the opinion of Aristotle, this cause is shown, namely, that Epicurus considered that the endeavour of virtue, although it be most fair and most stout and most constant, and be in all points approved of man, yet that very endeavour of virtue cannot so rejoice in itself, or so rest in itself, that it should desire nothing besides, which, as it were a reward of its labour, of its virtue and honesty, for the which virtue itself also is to be exercised, it should judge worthy to be sought; as it is also wont to be said in the verses:

<!-- p.388 -->

> The very comeliness of a deed rightly done, if rewards be wanting,  
> Moveth not, and it repenteth a man to be honest for nought.

It is also agreeable that Epicurus saw that virtue is for the most part joined with labour, misery and death, and therefore determined that it seemed absurd and foolish, if the blessed life should be placed in a state full of affliction; as if it should be said that **Regulus** was blessed on this account, that he kept the faith of his oath with peril of his life and goods. For although Epicurus approved such deeds, yet he doubted and was troubled concerning this, how that torment can and ought to be compared to the chief good; and at length he reasoned that there is some other thing, for the which virtue should labour, and which it should require as a reward, and that he said to be pleasure, unto which, as unto a mark, all things should be referred. And whereas pleasure is one of the mind, another of the body, Epicurus said that regard is so to be had of both, that virtue be not violated, and that the pleasure of the body is so to be enjoyed, that it be diligently considered what things agree with nature, lest nature be cast into evils rashly drawn upon it. Likewise: That the pleasure of the mind is so to be followed, that the mind be always quiet, which should be tormented with no perturbation at all, that is, that there be a sound mind in a sound body. After this manner, as touching the body, Epicurus commended the study of temperance, sobriety and frugality, and detested surfeiting, drunkenness, ***ἀσωτίαν*** (riotous living), riot and wantonness, whereby many diseases are brought on, health is broken, the strength is enfeebled, and life is shortened. Next, as touching the mind, he commended quietness, simplicity, candour, courtesy, gravity, constancy, and the other virtues, and detested depraved and mad lusts, amorous lightnesses, troubled affections and boiling passions, the fear of death, superstitions, vain hopes, faintheartedness, envy, wrathfulness, hatred of mankind, impatience, intemperance, and the rest of the vices, which do hurt unto the mind, the life, the fame, the goods and fortune of a man, and cast a man into hatred, infamy, <!-- p.389 --> contempt, and at the last stir up torments of conscience also. This was the opinion of Epicurus concerning pleasure, which he would have to be the end of man. But if it was objected unto him that the life of man is subject to many miseries, although a man lead a most honest life, he answered that there are certain remedies and antidotes or reasons, whereby those miseries may be so brought under the power of man, that they may easily be amended by art. And that those remedies are these:

**I.** That every man ought to bring forth those things which are the better, and to hide, lay aside, and commit to forgetfulness those things which are less pleasing. This precept Epicurus took out of **Pindar**, who flourished in the year of the world 3470, and in the 3rd Ode of the Pythians set down this sentence: ***Εν παρ' ἐσθλὸν, πήματα σὺν δύο δαίονται βροτοῖς ἀθάνατοι. τὰ μὲν ὦν οὐ δύνανται νήπιοι κόσμῳ φέρειν, ἀλλ' ἀγαθοὶ, τὰ καλὰ τρέψαντες ἔξω*** (For one good thing the immortals deal out unto mortals two sorrows together. These then fools cannot bear in seemly wise, but the good can, turning the fair side outward).

**II.** That we must dwell upon the remembrance of those things which delight the mind, all things being sent away which are hard, unpleasing and grievous.

**III.** That blessedness consisteth not in the quantity of time, but that there is no less of pleasure in a small time than in a long and infinite.

**IIII.** That for living well there sufficeth the pleasure ***καταστηματικὴν*** (of settled state), or freedom from pain (*indolentia*), or the privation and removing of pain, although it have not the pleasure in motion, or, as **Cicero** turneth it, the pleasant motion of pleasure.

**V.** That every man hath so much as wherewith he may fill the necessary and natural appetites, although the things not necessary cannot be had.

**VI.** That in death nothing is to be feared, seeing death is the end of labours, which by the remembrance of a sweet life, as of a banquet from which a man departeth filled, may easily be admitted and borne.

**VII.** That in all pleasure there is need of prudence and circumspection, and that regard is to be had of the true good, which is virtue, and the reward of virtue, lest under pretence of pleasure <!-- p.390 --> evils be drawn on, according to that saying: *"O fellows, for how small a pleasure how great good things have we lost?"*

**VIII.** That in the condition of life and the administration of affairs a man ought to follow the leading of nature, and not to take up a burden greater than his strength, nor to vex his mind with cares and anxieties; but that in all things that saying is best: ***λάθε βιώσας*** (live unnoticed).

These are the remedies which Epicurus judged might be set against human adversity, setting a shadow in place of the body, and husks in place of the grain and kernel, and in his whole speculation of the end of man straying infinitely from the truth. Many surely are ***φύσει νοσεροί*** (sickly by nature), many are born in the fourth Moon,[^P19-1] void of all pleasures, and full of labours; they sweat for others, to themselves they are unprofitable, and, whatsoever they do, they wrestle with poverty and other griefs so long as they live. Experience also witnesseth that there is a great ***ζυγομαχίαν*** (strife of yoke-fellows) of the mind and of the body, and that man cannot, as it were ***ἐπαμφοτερίζοντα*** (holding with both sides), easily comply with both. Here neither can a true concord of the mind and of the body be established in all points, nor can the remedies set forth by Epicurus satisfy a man, nor can the life of man be free from labour and pain. And so that whole wisdom of Epicurus falleth to the ground, and, how fair soever it seem, it lacketh sinews and strength, and is at strife with itself, especially seeing he saith that death is therefore to be despised, because it is the end of the evils of life, which life nevertheless he determineth may be made blessed. But if life be such, that because of its discommodities, if at any time it be broken off and ended, there is cause to rejoice, how can it be blessed? But if it be blessed, how and when shall not every man wish with full desire to keep it, seeing there is none among men who doth not most greedily covet an infinite fruition of blessedness? Epicurus therefore slayeth himself with his own sword, and withal showeth that he is of this opinion, that after the miseries of this life there remaineth no blessedness. Wherefore on this one account, if there were nothing else, the figment of Epicurus concerning the end of man is to be refused. And it appeareth that the rest of the Epicureans took handle and occasion thence to feign <!-- p.391 --> that, seeing nothing remaineth after this life, it is best even now in this life to indulge nature, to enjoy pleasures of what sort soever, and to satisfy the appetites. Hence are sprung those sayings:

> Eat, drink, play: after death there is no pleasure.

Now the opinions of the chief Philosophers concerning the chief good and the end of man being rehearsed, let us now, that we may put an end to this whole disputation, consider in order these five heads in place of a Coronis and Colophon,[^P19-2] and study to perform them.

*[Margin: I]* First, let us refuse the opinions, various, jarring, and contrary one to another, of the Philosophers and of others who have lived, or do even now live, without the Church, which are neither sufficient, nor do attain the mark of truth. It is rightly said: The speech of truth is simple. Likewise: Every best thing is the truest, according to the old verse:

> ***Εσθλὸν μὲν γὰρ ἁπλῶς παντοδαπῶς δὲ κακὸν.*** (For the good is of one sort simply, but the evil of every sort.)

And the true and right is always simple and of one manner, because it is good: but the false and crooked is manifold, because it is evil.

*[Margin: II]* Secondly, let us reject the opinions and the folly of the common people, which thinketh that felicity is placed in the goods either of the body or of fortune, which strike upon the eyes, as in prosperous health, in greatness and strength of body, in authority, nobleness of birth, riches, pleasures, liberty and other things which are required unto the splendour and glory of this life, the ordering of persons and the dignity of offices, and unto delights. For they who admire such things as the chief felicity, and determine that others, who lack goods of that sort, are unhappy, and despise them, are truly ***κεχηνότες***, gapers and fools, as **Aristophanes** calleth them, admirers of outward things, which, seeing the whole man is nothing but a bubble, ***ἐφήμερος καὶ σκιᾶς ὄναρ*** (a creature of a day and the dream of a shadow), one day, one hour, and oftentimes one moment is able to take away, as it is said: *"Whom the day, departing, saw proud, him the day, coming, saw laid low."* Likewise: *"It standeth fast, and is set by eternal law, that nothing which is begotten standeth fast."* Let us therefore always be mindful <!-- p.392 --> of the saying of the **Son of Sirach**: *"Why is earth and ashes proud?"* And of the sentence of **Prosper**, which **Augustine** setteth down in *Of Nature and Grace*:

> Thou flourishest in riches, and boastest thyself of the nobility of thine ancestors, and exultest in thy country and in the beauty of thy body and in the honours which are paid thee of men: look upon thyself, for thou art mortal, and for thou art earth, and unto earth shalt thou go. Look round upon them which before thee shone with like splendours: Where are they whom the powers of the citizens did court? Where are the invincible[^P19-3] Emperors? Where are they that ordered assemblies and feasts? Where are the splendid breeders of horses? the captains of armies? the Satraps? the Tyrants? Are not all dust? are not all ashes? is not the memory of their life in a few bones? Look upon the sepulchres, and see who is the servant, who the lord, who the poor man, who the rich. Discern, if thou canst, the bondman from the King, the strong from the weak, the fair from the deformed. Being mindful therefore of thy nature, be not at any time lifted up. And mindful shalt thou be, if thou look upon thyself.

Unto this sentence let us join the judgement of **Pliny**, who saith:

> It moveth pity and even shame in him that considereth how trifling is the beginning of the proudest of living creatures, seeing that oftentimes the smell from the putting out of lamps becometh a cause of miscarriage. Thou that trustest in the strength of thy body, thou that embracest the gifts of fortune, and countest thyself not so much as her nursling, but her own offspring, thou whose mind is ever upon victory, thou that believest thyself a God, being puffed up with some success: at so small a price mightest thou have perished, and even today thou mayest at a smaller, by the little tooth of a serpent's bite, or even, as the Poet **Anacreon**, by the stone of a dried grape; as **Fabius** the Senator, strangled by one hair in a draught of milk which he drank. He, and none other, shall surely weigh life in an even balance, who shall always be mindful of human frailty.

It is honest indeed to acknowledge and to admire the gifts of God, even the outward, health, wealth, excellency and nobleness of birth, authority, and success in affairs, and by all means a distinction and reverence of degrees and orders or estates in life is to be had and rendered; but yet they who are higher, stronger, mightier, richer, and happier than others ought neither to be puffed up with a prerogative that is fading and <!-- p.393 --> frail, nor to despise others in comparison of themselves, nor to arrogate anything to themselves. For God followeth the proud, a conqueror at their back, whose property it is to pull down the crests of the swollen. And let the sentence of **Aristotle** be observed: ***Δοκεῖ ἐν τοῖς τιμῶσι μᾶλλον εἶναι τὴν τιμὴν, ἢ ἐν τῷ τιμωμένῳ***, honour seemeth to be placed rather in them that pay honour, than in him that is honoured; that is, honour is a certain outward thing, unto the rendering of which, or the showing of it unto others, no man can be compelled, but it is rendered of free will and of the good will and subjection of him that honoureth, who acknowledgeth that he oweth reverence unto them whom he honoureth.

*[Margin: III.]* Thirdly, let us acknowledge the frailty and misery of our life, which is surely so great, that no man can arrogate and ascribe blessedness to himself in this life, how great soever he be. It is written that **Aristotle** defined man after this manner: ***Τί ἐστὶν ἄνθρωπος, ἀσθενείας ὑπόδειγμα, καιροῦ λάφυρον, τύχης παίγνιον, μεταπτώσεως εἰκὼν, φθόνου καὶ τῆς συμφορᾶς πλάστιγξ: τὸ δὲ λοιπὸν φλέγμα καὶ χολή*** (What is man? A pattern of weakness, the spoil of time, the plaything of fortune, an image of change, the balance of envy and of calamity: and for the rest, phlegm and bile). And there are often rehearsed concerning human frailty the verses of **Euripides**:

> ***Οὐκ ἐστὶν οὐδὲν δεινὸν, ὧδ' εἰπεῖν ἔπος,***  
> ***Οὐδὲ πάθος, οὐδὲ ξυμφορὰ θεήλατος,***  
> ***Ἧς οὐκ ἂν ἄραιτ' ἄχθος ἀνθρώπου φύσις.***

There is no speech so dreadful in the telling,[^P19-4] nor calamity, nor affliction coming from heaven, the weight whereof mankind doth not sustain. Hence **Cicero**, in the 4th of the *Tusculans*: Not without cause, when **Euripides** was bringing forth the play *Orestes*, **Socrates** is said to have called for the first three verses again:

> Neither is there any speech so terrible to bear,  
> Nor lot, nor evil brought on by the wrath of the heavenly ones,  
> Which the nature of man may not by suffering bear.

And **D. Wolfius**:

> Is there aught hateful to tell and fearful to bear,  
> Is there any plague sent from an angered Deity,  
> Whereof the frail nature of men feeleth not the labours,  
> And endureth not the hard burden?

<!-- p.394 -->

And that saying of **Hippocrates** is famous: *"The whole man from his birth is a disease. While he is reared, he is unprofitable, and imploreth the help of another: while he groweth, wanton, and to be restrained by a schoolmaster: while he is in his vigour, bold and swollen: while he declineth, pitiable, when he unwisely calleth to mind and vaunteth his labours."* And **Pliny** maketh a reckoning of the shortness of our life, saying: *"By the account of the rest of the night every man liveth but the half space of his life: an equal part is spent like unto death, or unto punishment, if rest befall not: neither are the years of infancy reckoned, which lack sense, nor those of old age, which liveth long unto its own punishment. So many kinds of perils, so many diseases, so many fears, so many cares, death so often called upon, that no wish is more frequent."*

But all these things **David** expresseth more rightly and more fully, saying: *"Thou hast made my days as an handbreadth; and mine age is as nothing before thee."* Likewise, *"As well the wise as the foolish die, and abide not in honour. The sons of men are vanity, and the sons of the great are a lie; being laid in the balances, they are all together lighter than nothing."*[^P19-5] Surely a thousand years are before God as one day. But no man liveth a thousand years. And one day, being divided into a thousand parts, will leave a proportion in no wise to be compared with even one small part of a day before God.

Of the inconstancy of fortune all and every one can speak, whosoever do daily consider the chances of men. **Sesostris**, King of Egypt, heareth this word from a captive King, who, yoked with three other Kings, drew his golden chariot, that fortune is deservedly compared to a wheel, wherein the highest things are now made the lowest, and the lowest in turn the highest. **Severus** crieth out: *"I have been all things, and it profiteth me nothing."* **Saladin**, King of Asia, Syria and Egypt, commandeth a linen cloth to be fastened to a spear, and in this to be beheld the power of the great King Saladin, who carrieth away nothing with him out of this life, save a linen cloth. And most fair and most true are the verses of **Gerson**:

<!-- p.395 -->

> I have seen a bladder swell with a little wind,  
> Which rattled when four peas were put therein:  
> This one boy touched with a needle; burst on a sudden,  
> Worthless and without sound, it sank down limp.

Of the favour and grace of men, how inconstant and changeable it is, every man's experience can bear witness. Rightly therefore do we say:

> Trust thou in God; no trust in the world availeth aught.

Of death it is certain, that it is sure, and that at a certain moment, unknown to us, it overwhelmeth us, and looseth the soul from the body.

*[Margin: IV.]* Seeing therefore these things are so, and not otherwise, let the fourth thing now to be considered be the study of the true doctrine concerning the end of man and the chief good, of which David saith, **Ps. 39**: *"And now, Lord, what wait I for? my hope is in thee,"* that is, seeing all things are so frail, vain, and fading, thou alone, O God, art my haven and my fair wind, showing me the medicine of so great an evil in thy word, the Son being sent, in whom I believe, and who turneth me unto righteousness and unto life eternal. Wherefore the chief good of man is, to be a member of the true Church of Christ, to acknowledge, call upon, and praise the Father of our Lord Jesus Christ, to have remission of sins by and for the Son, to be endued and governed with the Holy Ghost, and in silence and hope, or in patience, to possess his soul, and so to wait for the removing out of this life into the life and blessedness eternal and heavenly.

*[Margin: V.]* Fifthly, let us learn to prepare ourselves unto the obtaining of the heavenly chief good, laid up for us in the life eternal, that we may be prepared and ready unto the passage, of which it is said in the verses:

> The life of the wayfarer is as a passing; all things have an end,  
> Whatsoever here the unclean world doth honour.  
> Honour passeth, fortune passeth, money passeth,  
> Like unto God in mind, in body man passeth.  
> The fathers have passed; together shall we all pass hence;  
> He hath a country in heaven, who passeth well.

<!-- p.396 -->

And we shall prepare our minds unto this passage, if we shall be daily mindful of our weakness, unworthiness and frailty, of punishments, perils, diseases, of death and of eternal damnation: then of the greatness of the love of God, who gave the Son for us, of the obedience and merit of Christ, of the promise of life and salvation eternal, praying God that for the Son's sake he would have mercy upon us, and govern us with his Holy Spirit, and at the last graciously receive us, as it is said in the most sweet verses of **Vida**:

> Grant, Father, when the last hour of life shall come,  
> That I may die with thee present, no shades affrighting me:  
> And into thine embraces pour forth my rejoicing soul.  
> This only unto me; add more to others that desire it.

### *Quaestio, an finis hominis sit Legalis, an Euangelicus?* · Question, whether the End of Man be Legal, or Evangelical?

God himself is the end of man and the chief good, so long as man rendereth obedience unto the Law. But seeing man is torn away from God, and there is no ***ἀνακεφαλαίωσις*** (gathering together again under one head) otherwise than through Christ, neither doth God receive any, nor communicate himself to any, save by and for Christ, the end of man is rightly called Evangelical. In respect therefore of the manner whereby we are again coupled with God, the end of man is Evangelical, although in this very end there is withal the Legal obedience, which is begun in this life by them that believe, but in the other life shall be made perfect in all its numbers. And these things we have been minded to say of the Decalogue, and of those things which are required unto the exposition thereof; and we pray God that henceforward also he would teach and govern us, that we may think, speak and do the things that are wholesome for the Church.

AMEN.
