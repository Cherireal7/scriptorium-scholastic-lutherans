Now there is made in the sacred Scriptures, **Matt. 12** and **1 John 5**, mention of a peculiar sin, which is against the Holy Ghost, and unto death. This sin **Augustine** understandeth of final impenitence and despair, which denieth and refuseth the grace of God. And this judgement of Augustine is true and godly, and judgeth of this sin *a posteriori*, that is, it ascribeth the sin unto death to those who, departing out of this life, despise the Son of God and grace, or remain finally impenitent, as Pharaoh, Saul, Judas. And in the *Sentences*, **book 2, dist. 43**, it is rightly said: that the sin against the Holy Ghost is committed by the final contempt of the divine goodness and grace. These things concerning sins in general, which are reproved in the Law of God, it hath seemed good to set down beforehand.

Now we must speak of the sins against the first Commandment. With faith these sins are at strife: **I.** Epicurean and Academic doubtings. **II.** Distrust (*diffidentia*). **III.** Despair. <!-- p.82 --> Of these we have spoken above. And these are sins ***κατ᾽ ἔκλειψιν ἢ ἔνδειαν*** (by failing or want). But ***κατὰ δὲ ὑπερβολὴν*** (by excess) are these: **IIII.** Confidence or presumption, trusting in a man's own powers, wisdom and learning, as Ajax, Nebuchadnezzar. **V.** Trust in human defences, which is censured in **Jer. 17** and **Isa. 8**. Idolatry, which consisteth in these four kinds: **I.** To feign that something is God which is not God, as to worship statues, the worship whereof is reported to have taken its beginning from Ninus the son of Belus, in Assyria. For after the death of his father Belus, Ninus set up a great image with great privileges, that they who should adore and worship it might enjoy the same. Hence there was made from time to time a great resort thither. And the sport of the Devil more and more imposed upon men, even as in our own age, in the Papacy, there was a great resort to statues, to the Mary of Regensburg, and elsewhere. **Lyra** thinketh that hence are the names Bel, Baal, Beelzebub. Now mention is often made in the Scriptures of Baal, which word signifieth *Lord*. Whence are *Baalpeor*, the God of the Moabites, so called from opening, whom the heathen, as it seemeth, the name being a little changed, called Priapus, and who, as **Matthesius** thinketh, was so called as being the father of fruitfulness. *Beelzebub*, the prince or lord of flies, the god of the Ekronites, to whom King Ahaziah sent, inquiring whether he should recover of his sickness, **2 Kings 1**. *Baalberith*, a God of the heathen, whom the people of Israel worshipped after Gideon was dead, **Judg. 8**. It is exceeding marvellous that all these Idols were worshipped by the Jews. So easily doth the wit of man slide away from the true God. But dreadful punishments from God laid hold on this Idolatry in the people of Israel, **2 Kings 1**, **3 Kings 18 [1 Kings 18]**. The heathen worshipped many images of this sort. The Babylonians and Assyrians, Belus: the Egyptians, Osiris and Isis: the Africans, Neptune: the Moors, Juba: the Rhodians and Massagetae, the Sun: Samos, Juno: Lemnos, Vulcan: Paphos, Venus: Delphi, Apollo: Rome, Quirinus: Athens, Minerva. And **Terentius Varro** writeth that the heathen had three hundred Jupiters. And **Oenomaus** and **D. Augustine** write that 30000 idols were adored by the heathen. These things are horrible, and therefore the <!-- p.83 --> more horrible, because even in the assembly of Christians and in the Papacy, in our own age, the same idols, and often more, have everywhere been worshipped. To God be praise and glory, who hath brought back again the light of the Gospel. To this first kind belong they also who turned into Gods the outward[^P04-1] signs which were divinely added to the promise of the Gospel, as the Chaldeans and Persians feigned fire to be God, when they had heard that the sacrifices were kindled by fire fallen from heaven, as is said in **Gen. 11**. And the Turks also in like manner call upon a God who is not God. For the creation of things is common and proper to the three persons, whom these men acknowledge not. Thus many divine worships were, by a dreadful ***κακοζηλίᾳ*** (perverse imitation), given to strange gods, as the promise of the seed was referred to Priapus, **Numbers 25**: *"The people began to commit whoredom with the Moabites, and was joined unto Baalpeor, and Jehovah was kindled against Israel."* The promise of the sacrifice, or of the Son of God to be slain for mankind, was referred to Moloch, to whom parents offered their children to be burnt, which idol Solomon also served, **3 Kings 11 [1 Kings 11]**. Now Moloch was a hollow image with seven chambers: in one a door was opened for fine flour to be offered, the second for turtle-doves, the third for a sheep, the fourth for a ram, the fifth for a calf, the sixth for an ox, the seventh for a son. The face of this idol was as the face of a calf, the hands flat, and disposed to receive from them that stood by: and they danced the while,[^P04-2] whilst the child was burnt in the idol with the fire kindled beneath, beating Timbrels, that the parents might not hear the wailing of the infants. Hence also the word *Tophet* arose. For *Toph* signifieth a Timbrel. The place was in the valley of Hinnom, whence *Gehinnon*, that is, the valley of Hinnom, and *Gehenna*, a place appointed for the dead, **Isaiah 30**; likewise, hell.

The second kind of idolatry is to give divine honour to things or creatures of whatsoever sort, as the covetous man is an idolater.

The third kind is to bind God to things which He hath not set forth by His own word, as the adoration of Statues, in which Christ or the Saints are thought to be found. Whence pilgrimages hither and thither arose. For although the use of godly Statues, for <!-- p.84 --> remembrance' sake, in a Church rightly ordered, neither can nor ought to be forbidden, yet God is not to be bound to them.

The fourth kind is to choose worships without the word of God, and to trust in them, etc.

Furthermore, Magic is at strife with faith, that is, as we use this word at this day, a covenant made with the Devil, of what sort soever and concerning what thing soever. Now it is to be known that the word *Magia* is Persian, perhaps from the Hebrew *Mahaegim*, that is, Meditators or Philosophers, *die Gelehrten* (the learned). And **Plato** writeth in the *Alcibiades* that the Persian Kings delivered a son, when he was fourteen years old, to be instructed by four Magi, most excellent men: namely, the wisest, who should teach the doctrine concerning God and the divine worships; next, the most just, who should faithfully show the things that pertain to the government of the Commonwealth; afterward, the most temperate, who should frame his manners and private life; lastly, the most valiant, who should set forth the arts of war, and render the Prince great of soul. This was a discipline not like to our courts, in which Princes are for the most part made companions of ***στρατιώταις*** (soldiers), from whom they learn blasphemies, surfeitings, and the contempt of honest things. But since both the name and the thing have come into abuse, that is now called Magic which is an ungodly covenant struck with Devils, auguries, magical lots, *pythons*, that is, Spirits which dwell in frantic persons, and being asked do prophesy.

In the sacred Scriptures there are extant the names of these kinds.

**I.** *Ob*, **1 Sam. 28**, out of the history of King Saul. The woman, consulting Demons, prophesieth, *Ob* being consulted, or a python answering them that consult. Now *Ob* signifieth a skin-bottle or flagon, from the swelling of them that are possessed; in Greek ***ἐγγαστρίμυθον*** (one that speaketh from the belly), from ***γαστὴρ***, that is, the belly, as it were a vessel blown up. And such, for the most part, are they who at this time consult the Devil in a crystal, images being shown therein.

**II.** *Iedeuni*, that is, to know, to foretell, to have knowledge of things to come, to be enchanted, when others by certain words and images are bewitched, <!-- p.85 --> hurt, or deceived, as when wasting ulcers are wrought in others by enchantment; even as it is read in the life of **Macarius**, that there was a maiden who, through the spite of her lover, to whom she had refused wantonness, was by an ungodly Jew changed into the form of a horse, so that she was taken by all for a horse. This enchantment was reported to Macarius, who commanded the maiden to be brought unto him. For the holy man could not in this matter be deceived by the Devil, as other secure men were: but Macarius prayed unto God, and so the maiden was delivered from her enchantment; whom he admonished that thenceforth she should be godly, and should often hear the word of God, and that then there would be no more danger from the evil. And **Hermes** saith godly: *"The one safeguard is godliness. For a godly man neither an evil Demon nor fate holdeth, for God delivereth the godly from all evil."* Now in this manner women have often been transformed into men, and men into women. And such without doubt are the ***ἀρκτόλυκοι*** (bear-wolves), whom we name *Beerwolff* (werewolves).

**III.** *Meonem*, they who superstitiously choose and shun certain days, so called from cloud or vapour, as in the ***ἡμέραις*** (*Days*) of **Hesiod**. Likewise: they who fear Saturn's night, or choose Jove's day for the searching out of secret things, which are done everywhere.

**IIII.** *Menaches*, soothsayers by lot, who divine by figures, or numbers, or lots.

**V.** *Micaseph*, a Sorcerer, who by Devilish juggleries bringeth something to pass, and blindeth men, so that nothing is seen, or that a thing is seen to be such as it is not; as that virgin at Bologna, who conversed as though alive with her parents and others, and was brought to dances that she might play upon the cithern, whereas she was yet a dead body; which thing another sorcerer afterward detected, having taken away the charm which she carried under her arms. Such are the works of the Egyptian Magicians, Jannes and Jambres, who wrought the appearance of serpents.

**VI.** *Hober*, a companion. The abuse of the doctrine of the stars, especially in particulars.

These and more kinds are all accursed, and in the sacred <!-- p.86 --> Scriptures are most severely forbidden of God, especially in the twentieth of **Leviticus**: *"A man or a woman, in whom there is a Pythonic spirit, or enchantment, let them be put to death."*

Let these things suffice in this place concerning the sins at strife with faith.

With love are at strife, in defect: the hatred of God, murmuring and indignation against the judgement of God, or impatience in afflictions; the dullness of hearts in the secure, who feel not the burning flames of love. But in ***ὑπερβολῇ*** (excess): ***ἀταξία*** (disorder) of love, of gladness, of the appetites and powers in man.

With the fear of God is at strife, in defect: security, or to be without care, and without faith and love, as is the greatest part of mankind; the Epicurean and Cyclopean contempt of God. In ***ὑπερβολῇ*** (excess): superstition, trembling with a fear not filial but servile, when there ought to be no fear there, as hypocrites fearing lest they profane Friday and Saturday when they eat flesh. Now superstition is to ascribe some power to anything whatsoever without divine ordinance, and without natural causes, and without the experience of men of sound mind, as to wear about the neck certain figures, which should heal us.

With humility are at strife feigned humility, **Col. 2**, and pride, of which it hath been spoken above.

With patience are at strife impatience in afflictions, and the hypocritical endurance of torments drawn upon oneself of one's own accord, as in the Flagellants[^P04-3] and Hermits.

With perseverance are at strife ***ἀποστασία*** (apostasy), or falling away from the truth once acknowledged; and stubbornness in opinions, as in Servetus, the Anabaptists, and the Sacramentarians. And these are the sins which are at strife with the first Commandment.

### *Quae autem est poena violati primi Praecepti* · But What Is the Punishment of the First Commandment Broken

The breaking of the Decalogue is punished with eternal death, **Matt. 5**: <!-- p.87 --> *"Except your righteousness shall exceed."* Likewise: *"If thou wilt enter into life, keep the commandments."*

**II.** It is punished with temporal punishments. And to the first Commandment there is added a peculiar threatening, punishing unto the third and fourth generation. For this threatening is understood of temporal things, as it is also said in the verse:

> ***εὐσεβέων παίδεσσι τὰ λώϊα, δυσσεβέων δ᾽ οὔ.***  
> (To the children of the godly the better things, but to those of the ungodly, not.)

And in the **Psalm**: *"The generation of the upright shall be blessed."* And contrariwise: The generation of the ungodly shall be cursed, as the examples show. **1 Kings 40**:[^P04-4] For the idolatry of Solomon the kingdom of Israel was rent asunder, and there followed a perpetual discord of the two kingdoms. And **2 Kings 24 [2 Sam. 24]**, when David, against the commandment of God, had numbered the people, the choice of three punishments was set before him, of war, pestilence and famine. And the King, seeing that it was more tolerable to fall into the hands of the Lord than of enemies, chose the pestilence, and there fell within three days seventy thousand men. Likewise, **Judg. 20**, for the Levite's wife, forced and slain, two and twenty thousand Israelites were slaughtered in one day. In **Num. 25** it is shown that four and twenty thousand men fell for fornication, which history Paul allegeth in **1 Cor. 10**. For the sins of Manasseh the whole tribe of Judah is rooted out. For the sin of Achan his Sons also and his wife, and all that he had, are burnt, **Josh. 7**. In **3 Kings 11 [1 Kings 11]** God saith: *"I will rend the kingdom from thee, and will give it to thy servant. Notwithstanding in thy days I will not do it, for David thy Father's sake: but I will rend it out of the hand of thy Son."* **3 Kings 14 and 15 [1 Kings 14 and 15]**: *"The Lord shall give Israel up unto a curse, because of the sins of Jeroboam, who did sin, and who made Israel to sin."* **3 Kings 16 [1 Kings 16]**: *"I will root out the posterity of Baasha."* **4 Kings 8 [2 Kings 8]**: *"The Lord would not destroy Judah, for David his servant's sake."* And these examples often meet us, even in daily experience, as it is wont to be said:

> In goods ill gotten the third heir hath no joy.  

And **Solomon**: *"Oftentimes a whole city payeth for the evil of one man."* And **Hesiod**:

<!-- p.88 -->

> ***πολλάκι καὶ ξύμπασα πόλις κακοῦ ἀνδρὸς ἐπαυρεῖ,***  
> ***ὅστις ἀλιτραίνει, καὶ ἀτάσθαλα μηχανάαται,***  
> (Oftentimes even a whole city reapeth the fruit of an evil man,  
> who sinneth, and deviseth presumptuous things,)  

Hence it may be seen that God punisheth the line of the seed which hath sinned: and often spareth the man who hath sinned, and turneth the punishment upon his posterity, as in **3 Kings 21 [1 Kings 21]** He saith unto Elijah: *"Seest thou how Ahab is humbled before me? because therefore he is humbled for my sake, I will not bring the evil in his days: but in his son's days will I bring the evil upon his house."*

God often punisheth whole kingdoms for the offences of the magistrate, or of certain other men, as the history of the daughters of Scedasus showeth, and our own age aboundeth with examples.

These things are marvellous, but too true. But let us learn to live in the fear of God, who spareth His own, and mitigateth punishments. Let us know this also, that God forgiveth sins unto them that repent, and that temporal punishments are mitigated, and that no man is punished with eternal punishment save for his own sin, as the voice of the Gospel, which is here to be brought unto the threatenings of the Law, manifestly teacheth. From these things this objection ought to be resolved: *"The son shall not bear the iniquity of the father"*, namely, in eternal punishments, and in civil judgement. For temporal punishments from God range through many. But the Gospel, as hath now been said, delivereth an exception to this sentence of the Law, as Ezekiel teacheth concerning the Gospel. For they are excepted whom the Gospel excepteth. The ungodly are not excepted. This true doctrine is wholesome to the godly, to the ungodly dreadful and terrible.

### *Quae est promissio primi praecepti?* · What Is the Promise of the First Commandment?

**I.** Life eternal.

**II.** Temporal rewards.

For godliness is profitable unto all things, having promise of the life that now is, and of that which is to come. And the promise is diligently to be considered: *"Shewing mercy unto thousands of them that love me."* For it showeth withal that the mercy of God is greater <!-- p.89 --> than the wrath of God, even the just wrath. For in the threatening mention is made only of the third and fourth generation. But here, in the promise, it is spoken of many thousands. So much doth the grace of God abound above sin, as Paul testifieth.

But seeing that the promises are conditional, and that the Law is not satisfied, what is to be held concerning good works, and concerning their rewards, hath been sufficiently said above. Works please God, even though imperfect in us, and they please for Christ's sake, and have their rewards in this life and in that which is to come, as Christ clearly saith: *"Great is your reward in heaven."* Likewise: *"Them that honour me I will honour, etc."*

# *Secundum Praeceptum* · The Second Commandment

The first Commandment teacheth of the inward worship of the mind. The Second, of the outward signification, which is made by invocation and doctrine. This order is most fitting.

### *Cultus secundi praecepti sunt* · The Worships of the Second Commandment Are

**I.** The true invocation of God.

**II.** True thanksgiving.

**III.** True preaching (*praedicatio*).

**IIII.** Confession.

**V.** The oath.

### *De invocatione Dei, et gratiarum actione* · Of the Invocation of God, and of Thanksgiving

Of these two worships we must speak in the exposition of the Lord's Prayer. But let true invocation be distinguished from the invocation of the Jews and Turks, who are ignorant both of the essence and of the will of God.

<!-- p.90 -->

Unto thanksgiving for benefits spiritual and bodily let these move us: **I.** The commandments of God, who requireth thankfulness of us, as He Himself saith: *"Call upon me in the day of trouble: and I will deliver thee, and thou shalt glorify me."* **II.** Because God is most of all delighted with thanksgiving, as **Psalm 116** testifieth: *"What shall I render unto the Lord for all his benefits toward me? I will take the cup of salvation, and will proclaim the name of the Lord."* **III.** Because in this manner we win God unto ourselves, and, as it were taking hold of Him beforehand, possess Him, so that His benefits continually increase. For *"whosoever hath"*, that is, whosoever acknowledgeth that he hath from God, *"to him shall be given."* **IIII.** Because, if there be no thanksgiving, even that which is had is taken away, as from Jeroboam, Saul, and others. **V.** Thanksgiving keepeth every man in true humility. For he that giveth thanks declareth that all good things are from God. The proud give not thanks, as Nebuchadnezzar, ascribing all things to his own power. **VI.** Thanksgiving serveth to this, that every man live content with those things which he hath received from God.

### *De vera praedicatione* · Of True Preaching

True preaching looketh upon the essence and will of God. This is laid open in the Law, and in the Gospel. **Plato** spake thus: God, fearing lest the whole race of men should perish, gave unto men ***αἰδῶ τε καὶ δίκην***, shame and justice. These things indeed the Divines have referred to the Law and the Gospel. For the Law worketh shame, showing sins, so that the sinner dareth not lift up his eyes unto heaven. But the Gospel raiseth up, and pointeth out righteousness. By these two mankind is sustained. But these things, not understood by Plato, let us leave.

True preaching concerning the essence and will of God requireth three things: **I.** The knowledge of the doctrine concerning God. **II.** Constancy. **III.** Diligence in all the parts of doctrine: in doctrine, in the use of the Sacraments, in examples of godliness, or of the fruits of faith.

<!-- p.91 -->

In these things we all have need of the prayer of the Blessed **Fulgentius**, which is after this manner:

> *"Of the one Master, and our Lord, Jesus Christ, I ask with my whole heart to be taught far more things which I know not, of whom I have received the few things which I know. Him I beseech that, His mercy going before and following after, whatsoever things wholesome to be known I know not, He would teach me. In those things which I know to be true, may He keep me; in those wherein, as a man, I am deceived, may He correct me. In those true things wherein I waver, may He confirm me: and from things false and hurtful may He deliver me; that in my thoughts and speeches He may find that which He wholesomely giveth, and may cause those things to proceed out of my mouth which are chiefly pleasing in His own sight, and so may be made acceptable to all the faithful. Amen."*

### *De confessione* · Of Confession

We have said that constancy pertaineth unto preaching. This is the true confession and profession of the true doctrine concerning God, which can be quenched or changed by no terrors, hatreds, or torments.

**Cyprian**: *"As often as I drink the blood of Christ, so often ought I to be ready for the shedding of mine own blood for Christ."* Consider therefore, whosoever thou art that hast been baptized, that thou art bound unto obedience and confession.

Now true confession is made in these ways. **I.** By the godliness and honesty of our life, of which Christ saith: *"Let your light shine before men."* **II.** By the furtherance of the ministry in Churches and Schools. For this is to nourish the Prophets of GOD. **III.** By frequent presence in the assembly where the true doctrine is set forth, **Psalm 84**: *"How amiable are thy tabernacles, O Lord of hosts."* **IIII.** By the profession of the true doctrine, as often as there is need, as it is written, **1 Pet. 3**: *"Be ready always to give an answer to every man that asketh you a reason of the hope that is in you, with meekness and reverence."* **V.** By constancy and Perseverance even unto the last Breath of life. **Matt. 10**: *"Whosoever shall confess me before men, <!-- p.92 --> him will I confess also before my Father which is in heaven."*

Here are to be considered the examples of the Apostles, confessors, martyrs, and others.

### *De iureiurando* · Of the Oath

An oath is nothing else than the invocation of Him who is the highest, that is, of God, who ought to be the witness of our speech, and ought to punish them that deceive. *"To swear is to call God to witness"*, saith **Jerome**. For we ask that God may be witness that we speak true things, and that He may punish, if wittingly we deceive. And so the oath is akin to the invocation of God, and often in Scripture the word *oath* is taken for invocation, as: *"All in that day shall swear by my name"*, that is, shall call upon it.

**Here it is asked, whether it be lawful for Christians to swear, seeing that Christ saith, Matt. 5: *"I say unto you, Swear not at all"*?**

**I answer** first to the sentence in Matthew: The whole sermon of Christ speaketh of each man's private cleanness; it speaketh not of commonwealths, and of the businesses of callings. He disalloweth therefore the oaths which are given without a calling, and without the commandment of the magistrate. Meanwhile He forbiddeth not to use the oath which the magistrate tendereth, or which a man's calling constraineth him to use. If the oath be ***ἐπακτὸν***, that is, laid upon one by the magistrate, to whom obedience is owed, it is just. Likewise: If the oath be made under the constraint of a calling, it is lawful, that we may defend either our own or other men's good name and life. All other oaths, which are not ***ἐπακτὰ***, and which are taken up without the necessity of a calling, let us know to be forbidden by Christ. And examples everywhere show that the oath is granted unto Christians. Paul sweareth divers times. **2 Cor. 1**: *"I call God for a record upon my soul."* **Rom. 1**: *"God is my witness, that I am always mindful of you."* **Heb. 6**: *"An oath is the end of all strife."* And the Law saith: *"Thou shalt swear by the name of thy God."* **Jer. 12**: *"They shall swear, The Lord liveth, and not by Baal."* God Himself also sweareth by <!-- p.93 --> Himself, **Ezek. 33**: *"As I live, I will not the death of a sinner."* So also Christ: *"Verily, verily, I say unto you."* And examples everywhere meet us in the Scriptures. And in the books of **Kings** the form of the oath is to be observed: *"The Lord do so to me, and more also"*, that is, May God take of me the most grievous punishments, if I shall deceive. It is an invocation, and an imprecation, whereby both God is cited as the witness of the things said, and a man is bound over unto punishment, if we deceive.

### *Quomodo se quis parare et gerere debet in iureiurando* · How a Man Ought to Prepare and Bear Himself in the Oath

**I.** The name of God alone is to be cited and called upon. **Deut. 6**: *"Thou shalt swear by the name of God"*, not by strange Gods, or by creatures. **Joshua 23**: *"Swear not by the name of the gods of the heathen."* **Jer. 12**: *"They shall swear, The Lord liveth, and not by Baal."* And in **Matt. 5** Christ most severely forbiddeth strange forms by creatures, by heaven and earth, to which invocation is not owed. Therefore the adversaries do ungodly, swearing by God and by all the Saints, whereas yet to swear is nothing else than to call upon God.

**II.** One must not forswear oneself against conscience. **Lev. 19**: *"Thou shalt not swear by my name falsely."* And there are many sentences of the Heathen, as **Phocylides**:

> ***Μήτ᾽ ἐπιορκήσεις μητ᾽ ἀγνῶς, μήτε ἑκώντι.***  
> Neither purely, or knowingly, nor of thine own will shalt thou forswear thyself.  

And out of **Homer** **Tibullus** taketh these verses, most worthy to be remembered:

> Ah wretch, even though a man at first conceal his perjuries,  
> Yet Punishment cometh late, with silent feet.  

And there is no doubt that dreadful punishments hang over men for perjuries, whereby both the glory of God is hurt, when God is cited as though a false witness, and God is despised by the perjurer, as though He would not avenge that dreadful ungodliness. Families <!-- p.94 --> are oftentimes blotted out for perjuries, as even the Heathen have observed, as **Juvenal**, *Satire* 13:

> To a certain Spartan the Pythian prophetess made answer, etc.  

It is reported that on a time a Jew disputed with a Christian, and that, the disputation having been long held, at length the Jew said: How cometh it to pass that more lame, blind and beggars are found among Christians than anywhere among the nations? When the Christian answered that he knew not, the other said: The Christians offend their God with too many perjuries, more than any other nations. Therefore also they are more punished by their God, because of perjuries and blasphemies. And since at this day perjuries are very common, grievous punishments, as well public as private, hang over us.

Now I will relate a notable example of perjury punished, out of the English history. When the English, after the death of King Canute the third, had cast off from their necks the servile yoke of the Danes,[^P04-5] and, the Danes themselves being partly slain and partly driven out of the Island, the Council had straightway thereafter sent for Alfred, the son of King Ethelred, to take the Kingdom — who at that time was in exile with his brother Edward at the court of William, Duke of Normandy — and Alfred, having received the message of the will of the Council, had hastily returned home, and it had pleased the English fathers that Earl Godwin himself should go to meet Alfred, now landed in England — a man factious, treacherous, deceitful and ambitious, but of great authority — it came to pass that, since he held it for certain that Alfred, a man of ready wit, would be a hindrance to him, that he should not excel in authority among the rest of the nobles, he determined that he must be taken out of the way. Having therefore gathered a very great band of armed men, he went forth to meet him, and slew the young man, who suspected no such thing, upon the way, with almost his whole company; and being returned to London he cleared himself to the Council of the deed in wondrous ways, and withal began to exhort that at the first day possible Edward, the brother of Alfred, should be sent for, and made King: for this he liked the better, because Edward was a good man, and a lover of simple truth, and in no wise shrewd, whom he reckoned <!-- p.95 --> would be such a Prince as he might with no trouble stand in favour with. Not very long after, Edward is called back into his country, who came to the wish of all, and was made King. After these things it so fell out that the King was on a time at dinner, and Earl Godwin sat at meat next unto him, when Harold, the Earl's Son, a young man far the most flourishing both in age and in virtue, who mixed the King's cup, gave Edward to drink, and, one foot by chance stumbling, came as near as might be to falling, and stayed himself by the help of the other foot, the wine being in no wise spilt out of the Goblet. Then his father Godwin, not without a jest: *Now*, saith he, *brother hath helped brother.* At that word the King, suddenly touched with longing for his brother Alfred, turning to the Earl, saith: *So would my brother have helped me his brother at all times, if thou alone hadst suffered it.* But in Godwin, from the conscience of his sin, there ariseth a sudden fear; who therefore excuseth himself by forswearing, saying: *If I have been the cause of thy brother's slaying, most holy King, I pray that by the will of God this bread may choke me.* Which words being spoken, he confidently taketh the bread, and forthwith, his throat being stopped, and death come upon him, he falleth to the ground. These things we have recited out of **Polydore**. And like examples are everywhere read in histories. The history is known of Rudolph, Duke of Swabia, who, having lost his hand in the battle at Merseburg, cried out: *This was the right hand wherewith I made oath to the Emperor Henry, which I was now the first to break.* This happened in the year of Christ 1080. How horrible is the history of the Overthrow at Varna, before which the Turk himself is reported to have cried out toward the cross: *Christ, if thou art the true Son of God, show now that the perjury of thy people pleaseth thee not.* The Turk therefore came off conqueror, and Vladislaus, King of Hungary, was killed, and the army of the Christians cut down with a miserable slaughter, in the year of Christ 1444. More examples, done even in our own age, I now pass over. But let us know that in every oath there is an imprecation. For God, who is cited as witness and approver, is cited also as the judge of the oath, and the punisher and avenger of perjury. *If I swear falsely, may God punish me.* This imprecation is in every oath <!-- p.96 --> which either the glory of God, or the highest necessity, or the love of our neighbour requireth. Whence also it is read that the Romans sware, using this rite: He that was to swear held a flint stone in his hand, saying: *If wittingly I deceive, then may Diespiter, the city and citadel being safe, cast me out from my goods, as I cast this stone.*

Now in the oath let us think first of the true faith, whereunto we were bound in Baptism, and of the true doctrine, in which we ought to be constant even unto death: Next, of godliness of life, lest by indulging in wickedness we do against the oath which we have made unto God: Afterward, of the duties of mutual love toward our neighbour: Fourthly, of our calling. For we have each a certain calling, to which we are bound by oath, that we may be faithful Stewards. These things can be copiously set forth in their own place.

**III.** One must not swear lightly, nor in slight causes. **Sirach 23**: *"Accustom not thy mouth to swearing; he that sweareth lightly is not without sin, even as a servant that is often beaten is not without scars."* And here the magistrate ought to deal warily, that he lay not an oath upon any man lightly for any matter whatsoever, but let him know that an oath is a thing to be reverenced.

**Augustine** saith thus: *"He that provoketh a man to swear, and knoweth that he will swear falsely, surpasseth a murderer: because the murderer killeth the body, but he the soul, nay, two souls, both the soul of him whom he provoked to swear, and his own."*

**IIII.** The oath is to be kept, not broken, if it be not against God, and if it be possible; and indeed it is to be kept even with enemies: nay, with robbers and evil men. As Joshua keepeth faith with Rahab, because of the oath which the spies themselves had made, **Joshua 6**. Likewise: He keepeth faith with the Gibeonites, by whom nevertheless he had been deceived, **Joshua 9**. Therefore the Germans name it *Eyd* (oath), from the Hebrew *Ed*, that is, a firm covenant. And the Latins *Iuramentum*, from *Iuro*, which is from *ius* (right), that is, if the oath be just, and be not against God, and if the thing which is promised can be done.

<!-- p.97 -->

But when the oath is against God and His word, it is altogether to be broken. And rightly say the **Canonists**: *"In promises which cannot be performed without sin, the covenants are to be rescinded."* And the **Jurists** say: *"Let not an oath be a bond of iniquity."* As Saul would have done unjustly, if he had slain his son Jonathan because of the oath. For Jonathan knew nothing of his father's oath, and therefore did eat of the honey, **1 Sam. 14**. So Jephthah was not bound by his oath to sacrifice his daughter, **Judg. 11**. And the Wise Men return not unto Herod, although they had promised him that they would return. And Herod ought not to have kept the oath concerning the slaying of John. Therefore **Hugo** saith: *"He that sweareth that he will do an evil thing sinneth, and it is better then to break the oath than to fulfil it."* And this same thing we see that even the Heathen observed. Hippolytus, the son of Theseus, being asked by his stepmother, who was sick, to swear that he would do those things which she should lay upon him, and when he had sworn, and his stepmother sought to have her will of him, answered: *"With my tongue I sware, but my mind is unsworn."*

Here it is easily plain what is to be held concerning monastic vows. For all ungodly vows, and such as are made against the commandment of God, are void, do hurt the glory of Christ, and transfer the benefit of Christ unto works devised by men.

### *De peccatis contra II. Praeceptum* · Of the Sins against the Second Commandment

With invocation is at strife: **I.** The omission of invocation, and the security of drunken men, covetous men, etc., who pray neither for themselves nor for others. **II.** The false invocation of them who persevere in sins. Rightly saith **Isidore**: *"As no medicine availeth in a wound, if the iron be yet in it: so nothing availeth the prayer of him in whose mind guile, or in whose breast hatred, yet abideth."* **Prov. 15**: *"The Lord is far from the wicked, and heareth not the prayers of the wicked."* **III.** The invocation which is Heathen, Turkish, Jewish, the Idolatrous invocation of holy <!-- p.98 --> men that are dead, or the Papistical. For God alone is ***καρδιογνώστης*** (the knower of hearts), **Paralip. 6 [2 Chron. 6]**. And there is one only Mediator between God and mankind. And there is nothing commanded in the word of God concerning the invocation of the dead: whence also faith, determining that the invocation pleaseth God, cannot go before to give light. Therefore it is idolatry to call upon the dead. The Turks, although they say that they call upon God the Creator of heaven and earth, and of men, yet call not upon the Father of our Lord Jesus Christ, and so they call not upon the true God, the Creator of heaven and earth. The true God is Father, Son and Holy Ghost. This one and triune God the Turks neither acknowledge nor call upon, albeit, against the judgement of their own reason, in the beginning of every work they recite the mystery of the Trinity, which they have set before every chapter of the Alcoran in this manner: *Bisem Allahe, el Rahmane, el Ruoahim.* That is, *In the name of God, of the God of mercy, of God their Spirit.*

Moreover, with Thanksgiving is at strife: **I.** Unthankfulness toward the benefits of God, spiritual and bodily, even as we are all unthankful, one more, another less; as the most wise King of Naples, **Alphonsus**, on a time said with a groan, when he had experience of the uttermost unthankfulness of his own, whom he had raised up and adorned with many benefits: *Why do I complain of the unthankfulness of others toward me, who toward so many and so great benefits of God am too unthankful?* Acknowledge the benefits of God, and give Him thanks, and shun sins. For he is unthankful who feareth not God. *"And in this every man becometh the more blameworthy, the more acceptable he is unto God, even as Adam sinned the more, because he abounded in every good"*, saith **Augustine**. And rightly **Chrysostom**: *"By how much the greater benefits are appointed unto men, by so much the heavier are the judgements upon them that sin."* Woe one day unto us Germans, whom hitherto God hath of His boundless goodness endowed with so many benefits, both spiritual and bodily, with the light of His Gospel, with the studies of the tongues and arts, with quiet dwellings. *"Evil shall not depart from the house of the unthankful."* How few are there who truly give thanks for the benefits bestowed by God upon themselves, much less for the benefits common to all?

<!-- p.99 -->

**II.** Feigned and hypocritical thankfulness, such as is that of the Pharisee, **Luke 18**, without the true fear of God, without the knowledge of his own self. We ought rather to consider what is wanting to us, than what is present; not that the gifts in us are not to be acknowledged, but that we ought not to fall into arrogance and pride. For there is nothing of ourselves that is good, which we have not received. To be wanting is of ourselves. But to be present is of God, if it be good.

With true Preaching are at strife all corruptions of doctrine, heresies. *Ketzerey* (heresy), from *Kezar*, that is, to separate. Likewise: Negligence in teaching, and curiosity in handling ***παρέργοις*** (things beside the purpose), unprofitable and unnecessary matters. And by every man who hath a care of godliness this Rule is to be observed: Whatsoever looketh not to the glory of God, and to thine own salvation or that of other men, is to be omitted in the doctrine of religion. For it is not necessary that many questions be moved concerning it, etc. Likewise: Whatsoever in the Church is at strife with any Commandment of the Decalogue, or Article of the Creed, howsoever neat and plausible it be, is to be detested and fled from.

With confession are at strife: **I.** Apostasy, or falling away, or denial, as of the Emperor Julian falling away to the Heathen, of Ecebolius the Rhetorician, of Francis Spira, and of others. **II.** The ungodly stubbornness of fanatical men under punishments, as of Servetus, and of the Anabaptists. Now Michael Servetus was burnt at Geneva in the year 1552, who, when he came to the place of execution, William Farel exhorting him, would not call upon Christ the eternal Son of God, and though he gave no sign of repentance, yet did not at all defend his cause unto the people. Rightly saith **Cyprian**: *"The cause maketh the Martyr, not the martyrdom."* And **Apollinaris**: *"Where the truth of Christ is not, there is not the truth of martyrdom."*

With the oath are at strife, first, Sophistical and cavilling interpretations of oaths, as Hatto, Archbishop of Mainz, sweareth to Adelbert, Margrave of Bamberg, who for seven whole years had rebelled against King Louis, <!-- p.100 --> and could not be overcome by any force — he sweareth, I say, that he will bring him to the King for the making of peace, and will bring him back safe to his own home at Bamberg. The Margrave, being credulous, assenteth to the crafty Hatto. The next day Hatto, before they set forth, is bidden to dinner by the Prince, which he craftily refuseth, lest Adelbert also should take dinner, but that so, having mounted their horses, they may straightway enter upon the journey. Scarce were they gone forth, when Hatto saith that he is hungry, and that he hath not done wisely in neglecting the dinner that was offered, since he could not finish the whole journey fasting. He is therefore called back to dinner by the Margrave; they return into the castle out of which they had gone, and dine. Afterward they pursue the journey begun. When they come to the King, Adelbert is straightway condemned to lose his head; who, as he went to the place of execution, is reported to have put Hatto in mind of his promises and of his oath. This man answered that he was loosed from the oath, and had satisfied it; for that he had brought him back safe to his own home from the journey, to dinner, and that he had promised nothing of two bringings back. So the noble hero Adelbert is deprived of his head. This happened in the year of Christ 910. And hither belongeth almost all cavilling and sophistry, ***καὶ εὐτραπελία*** (and jesting) that is dishonest.

**II.** Ungodly oaths, whereby things dishonest, and forbidden of God, are promised.

**III.** Perjuries, blasphemies, abuses of the divine name, such as are those of magistrates, merchants, courtiers, soldiers, who, even as they walk with their hose slashed and torn, so with their tongue, which is the stamp of the mind, do slash and tear Christ, and His saving wounds.

We are all perjured before God. We all break the oath which we gave unto God in Baptism. God alone is true, to whose oath, *"As I live, I will not the death of a sinner"*, we must flee for refuge. Our perjuries we heal by the oath of God. Let these things be spoken to godly minds, not to the secure, and to them that are perjured against conscience without conversion, or true repentance.
