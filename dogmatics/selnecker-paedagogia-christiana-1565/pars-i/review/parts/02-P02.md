# *Recitatio Decalogi* · The Recitation of the Decalogue

**Exod. 20. Deut. 5.**

**I.**

*"I am the Lord thy God, which brought thee out of the land of Egypt, from the house of bondage. Thou shalt have none other gods before me. Thou shalt not make thee any graven image, or any likeness of any thing that is in heaven above, or that is in the earth beneath, or that is in the waters beneath the earth: Thou shalt not bow down thyself unto them, nor serve them: for I the Lord thy God am a jealous God, visiting the iniquity of the fathers upon the children unto the third and fourth generation of them that hate me, and shewing mercy unto thousands of them that love me and keep my commandments."*

**II.**

*"Thou shalt not take the name of the Lord thy God in vain: for the Lord thy God will not hold him guiltless that taketh his name in vain."*

**III.**

*"Keep the sabbath day to sanctify it, as the Lord thy God hath commanded thee. Six days thou shalt labour, and do all thy work: but the seventh day is the sabbath of the Lord thy God: in it thou shalt not do any work, thou, nor thy son, nor thy daughter, nor thy manservant, nor thy maidservant, nor thine ox, nor <!-- p.42 --> thine ass, nor any of thy cattle, nor thy stranger that is within thy gates; that thy manservant and thy maidservant may rest as well as thou. And remember that thou wast a servant in the land of Egypt, and that the Lord thy God brought thee out thence through a mighty hand and by a stretched out arm: therefore the Lord thy God commanded thee to keep the sabbath day. For in six days the Lord made heaven and earth, the sea, and all that in them is, and rested the seventh day: wherefore the Lord blessed the sabbath day, and hallowed it."*

**IIII.**

*"Honour thy father and thy mother, as the Lord thy God hath commanded thee; that it may go well with thee, and that thy days may be prolonged upon the land which the Lord thy God giveth thee."*

**V.**

*"Thou shalt not kill."*

**VI.**

*"Thou shalt not commit adultery."*

**VII.**

*"Thou shalt not steal."*

**VIII.**

*"Thou shalt not bear false witness against thy neighbour."*

**IX. and X.**

*"Thou shalt not covet thy neighbour's house: neither shalt thou desire thy neighbour's wife, nor his field, nor his manservant, nor his maidservant, nor his ox, nor his ass, nor any thing that is thy neighbour's."*

*"These words the Lord spake out of the midst of the fire, of the cloud, and of the thick darkness, with a great voice, writing <!-- p.43 --> them in two tables of stone,"* etc.

There are therefore two tables of the Decalogue, and ten Commandments, **Deut. 4. 9. and 10.**, written with the finger of God; of which the first table containeth the works whereby we deal immediately, or properly, with God, namely, the chief worship of God, inward and outward; the second containeth the works toward men, and is, as Christ saith, **Matt. 22**, like unto the former. For the Commandment concerning love is by no means to be neglected, and the obligation both of the first and of the second table is like and equal, under pain of eternal damnation; and the obedience also becometh worship of God, even as the worship of the first commandment, and is in the same manner necessary.

Concerning the order of the commandments there is no cause that we should dispute anxiously. **Josephus** ordereth them thus: I. That God is, and that He alone is to be worshipped. II. That no image is to be made which should be adored. III. That a man must not forswear himself. IIII. That the Sabbath is to be kept. V. That parents are to be honoured. VI. That a man must not kill. VII. That a man must not commit adultery. VIII. That a man must not steal. IX. That false witness is not to be borne. X. That nothing which is another's is to be coveted. Others order them otherwise, as the sports of men's wits are various. We retain the approved order. Now seeing that in the exposition of the Decalogue we must speak of good Works, the doctrine of good works is to be held fast at the outset; which, for the sake of teaching, is wont to be distributed into these members:

I. What works are to be done.

II. How they can be done.

III. How they please.

IIII. Why they are to be done, ***Διὰ τί*** (wherefore).

V. Whether sins remain in the Saints, and what the difference of sins is.

**Of the first** we know, by the benefit of God, that the works commanded by God in the Decalogue are to be done, inward and outward, as it is said, **Ezek. 20**: *"Walk in my statutes."* And these works are understood according to the expositions delivered by the Prophets and Apostles. For works not commanded by God are not worship of God, as it is said: *"In vain they do worship me with the commandments of men."* <!-- p.44 --> Likewise: ***ἁμαρτία ἐστὶν ἀνομία***, that is, whatsoever is not commanded in the Law, and whatsoever is done against the Law, this is sin, saith John. And **Num. 15**: *"Let them not follow after their own thoughts and their whoring eyes, but, mindful of the commandments of the Lord, let them do them, and be holy unto God."* Away, therefore, and farewell to the workers of supererogation; away also with the Antinomians; and let the Rule be kept in view, to be fixed in the inmost mind: *"Thy word is a lamp unto my feet."* So falleth all superstition, and idolatry, and a great heap of heresies.

**Of the second** we know that, faith shining before — that is, the acknowledgment of, and trust in, the Mediator — and the Mediator quickening us through the Gospel, and pouring out the Holy Spirit into us, good works are done which please God. For there can be no love and gladness in God, unless mercy and reconciliation for the Mediator's sake be first apprehended by faith.

**But it is asked, whether then no men can perform good works, unless they first believe, whereas yet the Heathen have done many good works?**

**I answer:** As touching outward manners, which can after a sort be governed by human powers, it is altogether to be confessed that good works can be done; and God requireth this diligence, as it is written: *"The Law is laid down for the unrighteous."* But here, when it is asked how good works can be done, the speech is of inward obedience, which without the acknowledgment of the Gospel and without the Holy Spirit cannot be begun. It is necessary, therefore, that faith, or trust in mercy, go before.

**It is asked also, whether this proposition be true: All works without faith are sins, as Paul saith:** ***"Whatsoever is not of faith is sin"?***

**I answer:** The proposition is altogether true; yet not by reason of the work, which is good, but by reason of the person, which pleaseth not God. Seeing therefore that the person is not reconciled to God, much less can the good work which is performed by the person please God, although it be good in itself, and pertain to discipline in this life. And here are diligently to be distinguished the Good of creation (*Bonum creationis*) and the Good of salvation (*Bonum salutis*), or the Good of the Law and the Good of grace. The Good of creation, <!-- p.45 --> or natural good, is the whole order in nature instituted by God at the creation, pleasing God in its own place, and appointed to certain uses that should profit His creature. And in this good is to be included that which in the Schools is named moral good, which is virtue agreeing with the judgement of right reason; which reason hath been implanted in man by God. Hither, therefore, pertaineth discipline also, and all good works, even of the Heathen, which please God in their own kind and place; even as the study of honesty, though it be but outward, pleaseth God, and is preserved and helped by Him, for the preservation of the order in nature instituted by Himself at the first creation. For God willeth the society of mankind to be in this manner preserved and propagated, which without discipline can neither be preserved nor propagated. And the causes of discipline have been set forth above.

The Good of grace is revealed in the doctrine of the Gospel, beyond all human reason, and is a far other thing than that former good. For seeing that man hath not continued in his righteousness and holiness, wherein he was created, although God preserveth and helpeth the order which yet remaineth in nature, as the Heroical motions in the Heathen oftentimes evidently show, nevertheless this order cannot stand before the judgement of God, since it is polluted by the contagion of the person, and is in no other case than an evil tree, which sometimes bringeth forth a fair apple, as cometh to pass at the lake Asphaltites; which apple, though it please, yet proveth not the tree to be good. And memorable is the saying of **Augustine**: *"Human righteousness compared with divine righteousness is unrighteousness; for a lamp also is seen to shine in darkness, but set in the beam of the Sun it is darkened."* Another good, therefore, the good of salvation, pertaineth hither, which may truly and wholly please God, namely, faith itself in the Son of God; which so doth good works, that they please God not only according to that maimed order which yet remaineth in the nature of man, so far as the preservation of this life is regarded, but that they are wholly and simply accepted of God, since they flow from the fountain of salvation, namely, from faith; as **Ambrose** godly saith: *"Not these are righteous, <!-- p.46 --> who hear the Law; but they who believe in Christ, whom the Law hath promised, these do the Law."*

**But how do good works please God?** This is the third question, to which we answer, that as, faith shining before, good works can be done, so faith is the cause why good works please God, as Peter saith: *"Offer up unto God spiritual sacrifices, acceptable to God,"* for the sake of Christ, who both is the propitiator, and maketh intercession for us, as He Himself saith: *"I pray, that the love wherewith thou lovest me may be in them."* And in this place these three things are always to be joined together. I. It is to be known that the person is reconciled to God freely, by faith alone, for Christ's sake. II. Our uncleanness is to be acknowledged, whereby we are far off from the perfection of the Law. III. It is to be known that God altogether requireth begun obedience according to the Decalogue, and that in the regenerate it pleaseth God, by faith alone, for the sake of Christ the propitiator and continual intercessor. These three things are always to be thought upon by us, that we may daily exercise faith, which is exercised in works in two ways: first, when we hold that obedience pleaseth God for the sake of the promised mercy; next, when we ask help, and so labour in our calling as much as we are able, according to that saying: *"Commit thy way unto God, and he shall bring it to pass; be subject unto God, and pray unto him."*

**The fourth question** is of the causes why good works are to be done. Here it is necessary first to reprove the blasphemy of the enemies of the cross of Christ, as Paul nameth them, who ascribe to the merit of works the merit of Christ, that is, remission of sins, righteousness, the Holy Spirit, and salvation, or eternal life. For we know that we are not otherwise justified and saved than by faith alone in Christ, and that by no human works can the wrath of God against sin be appeased. Therefore we say plainly: By faith alone we are justified, freely, without the works of the Law; that is, faith alone is the instrument and means whereby we apply to ourselves the benefits of the Son of God, and apprehend His obedience, resting in Him as in <!-- p.47 --> our Saviour, and through Him crying: *"Abba, Father."* And here let the causes be rehearsed, why it is necessary to retain the exclusive particle (*exclusiva*), "By faith alone," or "Freely." I. That the honour which is due may be given to the Son of God, that He alone is the sacrifice, or ***λύτρον*** (ransom), for us. II. That Consciences may have firm consolation, or that the promise may be firm. For remission of sins hanging upon the worthiness of our works is uncertain. III. That the difference of the Law and the Gospel may be seen. IIII. That true invocation may be made, which is hindered by the sight of our infirmity. Therefore we must draw near unto God in trust of the Mediator alone.

**But if good works neither justify nor save, why are they to be done?**

I. The first cause is the necessity of the commandment, as Christ saith: *"This is my commandment, That ye love one another."* And **1 Thess. 4**: *"This is the will of God, even your sanctification, that ye should abstain from fornication,"* etc.

II. The second cause is the necessity of debt, or of order, whereby the rational creature oweth obedience to God the creator, as Paul saith, **Rom. 8**: *"We are debtors to God, not to the flesh."* And constraint is not understood, as certain foolish men interpret it of obedience wrung out by fear; but there is signified the immutable and eternal order, that the creature should willingly obey God.

III. The third cause is the necessity of conversion. For the formal part (*formale*) of regeneration is the beginning of new life. And without conversion a man is not quickened by faith through the Holy Spirit.

IIII. The fourth is the necessity of not losing faith. For the Holy Spirit is grieved and driven away, when sins against conscience are committed, **1 John 3**: *"He that committeth sin is of the devil."* **1 Tim. 5**: *"If any provide not for his own, and specially for those of his own house, he hath denied the faith, and is worse than an infidel."*

V. The fifth is the necessity of avoiding punishments, **Ps. 39**: *"Thou with rebukes dost correct man for iniquity."* For the wrath of God is to be acknowledged in the greatness of human calamities, and we must beware of the snares of the devil, who as a roaring lion walketh about, seeking whom he may devour. There is to be considered <!-- p.48 --> also the dignity of good works, whereof Paul speaketh, **1 Tim. 4**: *"Godliness hath promise of the life that now is, and of that which is to come"*: not that for his virtues the person hath Remission of sins, which is proper to faith alone, but that in the reconciled good works please God by faith for the Mediator's sake; and because they so please, they also merit rewards spiritual and bodily, in this life and after this life, as the Parable of the traders showeth: *"Unto him that hath shall be given,"* etc. And Paul saith: *"We shall be clothed upon, if so be that we shall not be found naked."* And yet this remaineth true: *"In thy sight shall no man living be justified."* Likewise: *"The gift of God is eternal life."* For faith resteth not upon two things, the Mediator and our works. And of works it is spoken in this place as of the fruits of faith, which bringeth us, who are gone out from the Law, back unto the Law, not as unto merit, but as unto the standard and rule of life and obedience, and unto a judge showing what is wanting in us, and where we may find the supply of our defect, namely, in the Son of God, and that we ourselves also may live rightly. For that our works, whether going before, or present, or following after, contribute as merit unto salvation or unto righteousness, none of the sounder Doctors hath ever said, neither doth any man say it, unless he be plainly mad, and slide back into the old ***κυβείαν*** (sleight, dice-play).

**The fifth question** is of the sins that remain in the Saints in this life. Now it is necessary that it be discerned what manner of sins remain in the regenerate, and what manner grieve and drive out the Holy Spirit, for which a man becometh again guilty of eternal wrath, and rusheth into eternal punishments, unless he be converted again unto GOD.

Now the usual names are: mortal sin, and venial sin. Mortal is every sin, original and actual, inward and outward, in the unregenerate man. Likewise: it is such a sin, for which even the regenerate man loseth grace and the Holy Spirit, and becometh again guilty of eternal wrath, unless he be converted unto God.

<!-- p.49 -->

But here a distinction is to be held. All sins in the unregenerate are mortal, as is manifestly said in these words: *"He that believeth not, the wrath of God abideth on him."* But concerning the regenerate it is to be known that there are two degrees of sins. The one is sin serving, not reigning, or venial sin, such as is the original evil. Likewise: many defects, and depraved inclinations, and doubtings, sins of ignorance and of omission, depraved affections — but in those who are regenerate, and retain faith and the fear of God by the help of the Holy Spirit, and fight against depraved affections, and rush not against conscience. Such sins cleave in the regenerate in this life, and fight with the Law, so that for them a man would be guilty of eternal punishment, unless reconciliation were given him by faith for the Mediator's sake. For these sins do not shake out faith, nor drive out the Holy Spirit, in those who grieve for this filthiness, and believe that it is remitted unto them for the Mediator's sake, and do not indulge it against conscience. The other is sin having dominion, that is, error in the foundation, and a fall against conscience, as when David, knowing and willing, taketh away another man's wife, and becometh a murderer: for such sins a man loseth again grace, the Holy Spirit, and faith, and becometh actually guilty of eternal punishment, and, unless conversion be made, rusheth into eternal destruction; even as Saul is damned, not being converted unto God, and David is not damned, being converted unto God. Error in the foundation is, as when godly men, who had thought rightly, embrace ungodly doctrines, whether by error or wittingly; as many who before had thought rightly embraced the Arian impiety, and as at this day the examples of apostates everywhere show, who fall away from the acknowledged truth to manifest errors, Anabaptistical, Sacramentarian, and the like. These Questions having been most briefly unfolded, let us now come to the Commandments of the Decalogue.

# *Primum Praeceptum* · The First Commandment

The first Commandment beginneth from the Acknowledgment of God. For all the virtues, whereof the Decalogue is the fountain and standard, arise from knowledge. Now the acknowledgment of God (*agnitio Dei*) is understood of the essence and will of God, as God Himself hath revealed Himself, in the Law and the Gospel, and in the sure and illustrious testimonies of miracles.

## *De Noticia et Agnitione Dei* · Of the Knowledge and Acknowledgment of God

Here at the outset let the difference be considered between the Philosophical knowledge (*noticia*) of God and the Christian. All the Heathen that are of sound mind affirm that there is a God, the cause of good in nature; but of the one essence and three persons they can know plainly nothing, neither indeed ought they to know anything of this mystery outside the word. Next, they are ignorant of the will of God revealed in the Gospel, which is, that he who seeth the Son, and believeth on Him, may have everlasting life. Then, they have certain small parts of the Law; they have not the whole Law. They know nothing of inward obedience, of inward uncleanness, of concupiscence; and although they see and bewail it, as Medea saith:

> I see the better things, and approve them; the worse I follow:  

yet they do not hold it to be sin, that is, a thing condemned by God; even as Paul also saith: *"I had not known that concupiscence is sin, except the Law had said, Thou shalt not covet."* Likewise: *"I was alive without the Law once."* There remaineth therefore unto the Heathen a small portion of the knowledge of God, namely, so far as the human mind, and the light of the divine Law implanted in the mind, and the whole nature of things, do show it, as a testimony concerning God; even as Paul also preacheth of this natural knowledge, **Rom. 1**. And hither pertain the notable sentences:

> And every man is a pattern of God in a little image.  

And:

<!-- p.51 -->

> God, filling all things, hath poured Himself into the whole mass.  

But those notions are very dim, and are easily shaken out of men's minds, especially when contrary objects meet the mind; even as **Plato** also saith: *"The natural notions are like things which are seen in water, and which by reason of the moving of the wavering water offer no firm and stable image to him that looketh on."*

That God is, reason testifieth from the consideration of the circumstances of nature, which are: I. The wonderful disposition of the whole world, and of the parts thereof. II. The preservation of the kinds. III. The perpetual likeness of the kinds in generation. IIII. The changes of the seasons. V. The ordinary revolutions of the heavenly lights. VI. The illustrious notions, and the gifts, in every several man. VII. The order and series of causes. VIII. The presages of things to come. IX. The rewards of the good, and the punishments of the wicked. X. The testimonies of consciences in men that are not perverted.

What God is, the Heathen know not wholly, who only rehearse the properties and general attributes of God, namely, that God is an eternal mind, the cause of good in nature. But of the distinction of the persons they know nothing at all. That there is a providence, they hardly affirm. For they doubt, when they see that it often goeth ill with the good, and well with the evil. And in this article human reason without the word of God is either Epicurean, that is, it thinketh that calamities fall out by chance, according to the verse:

> All things are dust, are laughter, and all things are nothing;  
> For blind and headlong chance driveth all things.  

Or it is Stoic, that is, it seeketh the causes in matter, and in the position of the stars; or it is Academic, that is, it only marvelleth why God hath laden this weak nature with so great miseries.

The will of God they are utterly ignorant of, and judge of God only according to the shadow of the virtues,[^P02-1] without the true light.

The resurrection of bodies they know not. The immortality of the soul they hardly hold, as Antonius complaineth in <!-- p.52 --> the *Tusculans*, where Marcus saith: *"Read over diligently the book of Plato which is of the Soul; there will be nothing more that thou canst desire."* Antonius answereth: *"I have done so, in good sooth, and that often; but I know not how, while I read, I assent; when I have laid the book aside,[^P02-2] and have begun to think with myself of the immortality of souls, all that assent slippeth away."*

So utterly is there nothing sound in human reason. Whence then should any man say that the Heathen are saved by their own Law, as Thamer, Latomus, and others have thought? It remaineth that we look unto the Law and the testimony, **Rom. 10**: *"The word is nigh thee, even in thy mouth, the word of faith, which we preach."*

Christians know the essence and will of God from the revelation (*patefactio*) of God in the word, and in the testimonies added for the confirming of the word and the applying of it unto us.[^P02-3] Therefore in the first Commandment the manner is straightway shown, whereby God willeth to be acknowledged and apprehended, namely, by the word, and by some testimony of His. The word is: *"I am thy God,"* that is, thou shalt acknowledge and worship me as God, who set this word before thee. The testimony is: *"Which brought thee out of the land of Egypt."* So ought we to acknowledge God by the word which is given unto us, and by the testimony, namely, that Christ hath been given and shown unto us. This God, who is the Father of our Lord Jesus Christ, we ought to acknowledge and to worship, **John 6**. For as God brought the people out of the bodily Egypt by Moses, so out of the spiritual Egypt He hath brought us by Christ into a land flowing with milk and honey, that is, into eternal life. Now the word *Egypt*, as **Nazianzen** and **Osiander** interpret, signifieth darkness and persecution. Here let both our misery and blindness without the acknowledgment of Christ, and the multitude and power of our enemies, the devil, sin and death, be considered.

Moses bringeth the people out, but bringeth them not into the promised land. For the Law cannot render the true and spiritual good things: Joshua, the true Jesus, bringeth us in, Moses being dead. These things are to be diligently considered.

Now God Jehovah is to be acknowledged in His essence, <!-- p.53 --> in the unity of the Trinity, and the Trinity of the unity. *Jod* is the beginning of the number ten, and signifieth the first person. *He* is the name of essence, and denoteth the Son, by whom all things were created, and have their essence. *Vau* is the copula, the signification of the Holy Spirit. And *He* is doubled because of the two natures, the divine and the human, inseparably united in the one person of Christ. And Christ oftentimes useth this word of Himself. He saith unto Moses: *"I will be that I will be."* And straightway: *"Thus shalt thou say unto the children of Israel, I WILL BE hath sent me unto you."* For *Jehovah* is from the verb *Haiah*, from *to be*. And afterward in the new Testament He unfoldeth the future, and showeth that the promise is fulfilled, when He saith, **John 8**: *"The Beginning, who also speak unto you."* And straightway: *"Before Abraham was, I am."* This name *Jehovah* belongeth to the essence of God alone, and is given to no creature. And God began to use this name toward His creature first at the Creation of man, when man was now made, **Gen. 2**. For it was necessary that there should be the perfect naming of God there, where the perfect work was. The other names of God are sometimes given also to rational creatures, as *Elohim*: *"I have said,[^P02-4] Ye are gods."* Where nevertheless this is to be observed: when the names that are to be given unto God are joined either with verbs or with other predicates, then the one is plural, the other singular. As: *"In the beginning Gods created the heaven and the earth"* (*creavit Dii*). For *Elohim* is of the plural number, from the singular *Eloha*. So by the plural noun the Trinity, and by the singular predicate the unity, is declared. There is one only place in the whole Scripture, **Gen. 35**, where the verb also is of the plural number, by which the three persons really distinct are expressly set forth: *"He called the place El Beth El, because there Elohim were revealed unto him."* But when those names of God are given to created persons, that is not wont to be done, but the subject and the predicate are always put in one number.

## *Cultus Primi Praecepti* · The Worships of the First Commandment

The worships are: Faith, love of God, and fear of God. These are <!-- p.54 --> the true and first inward worships. And so the first Commandment delivereth both the manner of acknowledging God, and the true worships. Now unto Faith is joined Hope; unto the fear of God, Humility, Patience and Perseverance.

### *De Primo, videlicet de Fide* · Of the First, namely of Faith

It is not to be anxiously disputed whether true Faith pertain to the Law, seeing that it is proper to the Gospel. The first Commandment requireth trust in God, that we believe Him in all things, and simply cleave unto His word, wherein He hath revealed unto us His essence and will. The Gospel afterward declareth the will of God concerning the manner and way of our salvation, who cannot satisfy the Law. And there is a great Emphasis concerning faith in the little word, *"thy God,"* that is, caring for thee. And faith is required also in these words: *"I will shew mercy unto them that love me."* But how this mercy is performed unto us feeble men, the voice of the Gospel unfoldeth and determineth.

So then in general the first Commandment requireth faith, that is, that we trust in God alone, and rest in Him, according to the word and the promise, and be certain of the will of God revealed in the whole word, that is, both in the Law and in the Gospel, as well in that which pertaineth to bodily objects, as in that which pertaineth to spiritual and eternal ones; provided this be considered, that the former are both to be asked and to be expected with a condition, but the latter are asked and expected without condition, as is said elsewhere.

Here weak minds are to be comforted, which complain of the feebleness of their faith. For we have not all a firm faith, such as Jacob had, wrestling with the Son of God, and Daniel, cast among the lions. And let the weak consider the weak faith of him, **Mark 9**, who saith: *"Lord, I believe; help thou my weakness."* Likewise that of **Phil. 2**: *"It is God which worketh in us to will and to perform, that something may be done which is well pleasing unto Him."* For there is in these words great consolation for <!-- p.55 --> all that are weak in faith. All ought to hold that, if but a spark of faith shineth in them, it hath been kindled by the Holy Spirit, and pleaseth God. If thou art willing, believe that this thy willing is not without God, but hath sprung from God, although it be feeble.

There are also minds to be reproved, which either after the Epicurean and Academic manner doubt of providence and of the certainty of doctrine, or distrust God in perils, or despair, as Cain: *"Mine iniquity is greater than that it may be forgiven me."* **Augustine** answereth: *"Thou liest, Cain; greater is the mercy of God than the misery of all sinners."* Likewise: Saul, Judas, Francis Spiera, and very many others. Likewise: let all idolatry, Heathen and Papistical, be reproved, which can be comprised in these, as it were, kinds.

I. When something is feigned to be God, and is worshipped as God, and is not God; as the adoration and worship of Statues as of deities, both among the Heathen and among the Papists, which is horrible idolatry. Likewise: to turn outward signs, divinely commanded, to ungodly uses, or into false deities; as the Chaldeans and Persians feigned fire to be God, since the victims had been burned with fire fallen from heaven, as is said, **Gen. 11**, of Ur of the Chaldees, or, as the Persians named it, of *Orimasda*. And the Papists carry about the bread, or God, and adore it. The Heathen feign deities: the Thebans Jupiter and Bacchus, the men of Attica Ceres, the men of Lampsacus Priapus. Likewise: Mars, Minerva, etc. The Papists worship the Saints for true deities, Barbara, Peter, Roch, Sebastian, and others without number. Such worships were there among the Jews: the dancing before the lamb, or calf, set up in the absence of Moses, **Exod. 32**. *"Moses saw that the people were naked, and that Aaron had stripped them unto their shame,"* that is, he saw the people now wandering without God, as the nations wandered, unto the shame or reproach of God. Such was the worship in the valley of Hinnom, whence is the name *Gehenna*, the article concerning the lamb to come, and the seed of the woman, being depraved.

There are many histories, very dreadful, both Heathen and others. But let a notable history be observed of the Indian Devil. <!-- p.56 --> The Devil in India the rich, or the happy, had mingled himself with a certain Statue, out of which he had given answers, and had healed all the sick, whom nevertheless he himself had before cast into their diseases. He was therefore worshipped with great and many sacrifices. But when Bartholomew the Apostle came thither, the Devil Ascharoth ceased to answer and to heal the sick. The men therefore went away unto another Devil who gave answers elsewhere, Berioth, and asked him concerning the cause of the departure of that Ascharoth. He answered that the cause was the coming and presence of Bartholomew; for that Ascharoth was now taken and held fast. And he said that Bartholomew was a friend of the living God. But who that Bartholomew was, the people knew not, until a certain man possessed cried out at the sight of him: *"Bartholomew, Apostle of Christ, thy commandment doth utterly burn and consume me."* Straightway the Devil went out at the Apostle's bidding. The matter was carried unto Polimius the King, who had a lunatic daughter, whom the Apostle easily delivereth from the Devil and from her torments, and calleth Polimius unto Baptism, and revealeth unto him the snares of the Devil. Polimius appointeth an hour, at which they should come together at the Statue of the Devil. Bartholomew commandeth the Devil to speak again out of his Statue; who straightway uttereth these words unto his sacrificing priests and unto the people: *"Cease, ye most wretched, from the sacrifices offered unto me. I am bound with fiery chains by the Angels of Christ crucified, who holdeth our prefect, namely death, and our Princes captive in fiery chains, and sendeth forth His Apostles with the sign of His cross to drive us out, of whom this Bartholomew also is one. Entreat him, I pray you, that he let me go into another Region."* Bartholomew in his turn saith unto Satan: *"Tell whence these who lie here have been made sick."* He answereth: *"Our prefect sendeth us forth, that we may hurt the bodies of men. Their bodies being hurt, they seek health from some quarter. Then do we work miracles of healing. So they run unto us, and in this manner we gain their souls also. They think that health is from us, who yet are the authors of the diseases."* These things done, Bartholomew <!-- p.57 --> teacheth the people concerning the true God, and dedicateth the temple unto Christ, and baptizeth them that believe. The King commandeth that the Statue be pulled down with ropes; which when the people could not do, the Apostle saith unto the Devil: *"Come thou out thyself, and cast down the Statue, and break it in pieces; get thee hence into desert places."* The Devil obeyeth the commandment of the Apostle, and breaketh and destroyeth the Statue and the other images. The Apostle and the people praise God, and call upon Christ, and so there is made a true Church in India.

A like history is read of **Gregory**, a Bishop in Cappadocia. He, journeying through the Italian mountains, came unto a temple-keeper who worshipped an idol, out of which a Devil had been wont to answer him concerning things to come. And the temple-keeper got his living thereby. Now when the Bishop lodged there that night, the Devil fled away. In the morning the Bishop departeth. The temple-keeper findeth his Devil no more. But in the night the Devil betaketh himself unto the temple-keeper's bed, and declareth that the temple-keeper himself is to blame that he hath departed, because he had received the Bishop to lodge. The temple-keeper straightway goeth unto the Bishop, and declareth unto him his case. The Bishop, smiling, giveth the temple-keeper a little writing: *"Gregory unto Apollo, greeting. I permit thee to do thy business in thy former place."* The temple-keeper layeth the writing by the Statue; the Devil returneth. But the temple-keeper thinketh with himself: What manner of God is this, who is overcome by the presence of one man? He departeth therefore unto the Bishop, forsaking the Devil, asketh to be instructed, is baptized, and, the Bishop being dead, succeedeth him in the ministry. There are many other examples.

The second kind of Idolatry is, when divine honour, or the worship of the first table, is given unto any things whatsoever which are not God. As: *"The covetous man is an idolater,"* **Eph. 5**. For, as **Tauler** rightly saith: *"The image of a thing entering into thee is thy God, and hindereth thee from the whole God. When the image entereth in, God goeth out."* The invocation of Saints is idolatry. Trust in good works is idolatry. Likewise, to choose worships without the word of God: masses, fastings, pilgrimages, monkeries, etc. But yet of the sins against the first commandment it shall be spoken a little after. Here we have only wished to set forth, as it were by Antithesis, what manner of trust in God is required.

<!-- p.58 -->

Now unto faith is joined Hope, whereby we expect the promise from God. And this is the difference between faith and hope: Faith embraceth the present benefit, or *the word of the thing* (*verbum rei*). Hope receiveth, or expecteth, the future benefit, or *the thing of the word* (*rem verbi*), that is, the thing promised. These things are unfolded at large elsewhere. Now hope is directed both to this life and to the life to come. In this life it expecteth the bodily good things which it asketh of God. Next, it expecteth eternal deliverance, as is said in the first epistle of John, **1 John 5**: *"We have eternal life in Christ, and we know that we are heard, when we ask any thing of God."* Here let it be spoken of the Papistical doubting, which is to be refuted by these sayings: *"As I live, I desire not the death of a sinner."* *"O wretched we, if we believe not God even when He sweareth."* And in the first epistle of John, **1 John 5**: The whole Trinity testifieth that we are His care and near His heart, by water, blood and spirit. Let it be spoken also of distrust. We not only abuse the means which are to be used in our necessities, but assuredly we trust God too little, as we see in wars, and when pestilence and plague are raging. We ought to remember that saying of **Tauler**: *"Where nature ceaseth, there God beginneth"*: that is, human help being taken away, divine help is always at hand unto them that believe in the Son of God, if not according to our will, yet surely unto eternal salvation.

### *De Secundo, videlicet de Dilectione Dei* · Of the Second, namely of the Love of God

The Love of God is understood first passively, that is, of the love wherewith God embraceth us. God hath so loved us: I. He made us, when we were nothing. II. He redeemed us, when we had perished. III. He healeth us, and upholdeth us by His Holy Spirit, since we are feeble and weak.

I. Out of nothing He created us, who were nothing, and endowed us with the breath of life, that is, with His wisdom and His virtues, after His own image, that we might be, and be called, the image of God.

<!-- p.59 -->

II. He redeemed us through the Son, with unmeasurable love toward us, that whosoever believeth in Him should not perish, but have everlasting life, **John 3**. **Rom. 5**: *"God commendeth his love toward us, in that, while we were yet sinners, Christ died for us."* And in the first epistle of John, **1 John 4**: *"In this was manifested the love of God toward us, not that we loved God, but that he loved us, and sent his only begotten Son into the world, to be the propitiation for our sins."*

Created out of a positive nothing[^P02-5] unto something, and indeed unto the image of God, we were made again, by the snares of the Devil and by our own will, a privative nothing. And this would have remained true: *"And that returneth to nothing, which before was nothing."* From the image of God and a polished mirror we were made an instrument of the Devil, bespattered with mire. But the Son of God sprinkleth us anew with His own red blood, and cleanseth us, as is said in the first epistle of John, **1 John 1**: *"The blood of Jesus Christ cleanseth us from all sin,"* as well of that which is past as of that which is to come, as **Augustine** saith. For He is *Asam*, **Isa. 53**, that is, a sacrifice for all sins, original and actual.

It is reported that even to this day in Africa, among certain nations, especially with *Preteianes*, that most mighty successor of Queen Candace (**Acts 8**), whom by a corrupted word they name *Praestanus* and *Presbyter John*, it is the custom at their solemn feasts to carry about a golden cup, filled within with mire, and bespattered with it round about, yet so that the excellency of the gold shineth forth; and at the right hand of the cup the cross of Christ hanging, and shedding His blood. This would be a fair image, if it were understood by those nations.

III. But seeing that, as long as we are in this life, we are still feeble and weak, as **Gregory** hath very godly said: *"We are all weak; but weaker is he who knoweth not his own weakness"*: therefore the Holy Spirit is sent, the Paraclete and upholder, ruling and keeping, through our whole life, <!-- p.60 --> His own, that is, all who believe in the Son of God, and rush not against conscience. Wherefore we sing:

> Without Thy divine power,  
> There is nothing in man,  
> Nothing that is harmless.  

So **Bernard**, on **Ps. 116**, *"What shall I render unto the Lord for all his benefits toward me?"*: *"When I was ignorant, He instructed me; when I went astray, He brought me back; when I sinned, He corrected me; when I stood, He held me; when I fell, He raised me up; when I went, He led me; when I came unto Him, He received me. Neither have I for all these things anything to repay, save only that I love."* And there is no man who doth not find by experience that even in temporal things he is wonderfully upheld, and often divinely delivered out of great evils.

So standeth the conclusion of the Patriarch Jacob, **Gen. 32**: *"I am less than all thy deliverances."* And **Ps. 116**: *"What shall I render unto the Lord for all his benefits toward me?"*

Next, the love of God is understood actively, namely, of the Love wherewith we in our turn ought to love God as our Father and our God, and subject ourselves unto Him, and keep His commandments with a gladness that resteth in God. Nature was so created at the beginning, that the will and the heart should rest in God, and rejoice in Him as in their chief good; even as to this day the heart, when it rejoiceth, is wont to be enlarged, being so created that first and before all things it should receive God, as is said in the verse:

> Gladness that enlargeth the breast hath the Godhead within.  

And **1 John 4**: *"He that dwelleth in love dwelleth in God, and God in him."* Next, nature was so created, that mankind should be coupled together by the bonds of love, **John 17**: *"That they all may be one in God."* And in most sweet verses it is said:

> And that some might teach others concerning religion,  
> Piety bade them have houses that joined together.  

That is (for so we are pleased to take it here also), that we might be one in God, God willed that the bonds of love should be in us.
