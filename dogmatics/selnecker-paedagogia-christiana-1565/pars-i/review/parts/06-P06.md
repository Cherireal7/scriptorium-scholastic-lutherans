From this admonition let the younger sort learn to answer both a customary argument and a question. The argument is this:

> Laws instituted by the express will and voice of God are not to be changed.  
> The ceremonies, and also the judicial laws of Moses, were instituted by the express voice of God.  
> Therefore they were not to be changed.

**I answer to the minor.** The ceremonies and the other Mosaic laws were so instituted by God, that when Christ was exhibited they should be abrogated: therefore it behoved them to be changed, as hath been said before. ***οἱ γὰρ τύποι ἐν νόμῳ, ἡ δὲ ἀλήθεια ἐν εὐαγγελίῳ*** (for the types are in the Law, but the truth in the Gospel), saith **Epiphanius**. The question is this: Wherefore doth God so often by the Prophets reprove sacrifices, when yet He Himself instituted them, as in **Ps. 40. 50. 51. Isa. 1. 61. 66. Jer. 6. Hos. 6. Prov. 21. Matt. 15. Acts 15. Rom. 2. 14. Col. 2.** and elsewhere? **I answer.** God doth not reprove sacrifices in themselves, or the Mosaic ceremonies, which He Himself had instituted; but He reproveth the abuses and the false persuasions concerning sacrifices: that they either merited remission of sins and appeased the wrath of God, or were righteousness before God, or were worship of themselves, that is, by the work wrought (*ex opere operato*), as the Papists speak at this day, or were things necessary to the righteousness of the Gospel and to salvation, or were still to be retained after Christ was exhibited, as the Jews, and at this day the Anabaptists, have dreamed. These false opinions, therefore, the Prophets censure, even as we at this day reprove the errors of the Papistical Mass.

Now there were seven causes for which the Mosaic ceremonies were instituted by God. Of these the first was, that they might be the sinews and bonds of the ministry and of the public assemblies, in which the promise of the Messiah and the true doctrine should be set forth and propagated. The second, that they might be types, figures, shadows, pictures and allegories of the benefits of Christ, which have been exhibited in the New Testament. The third, that they might be tokens <!-- p.122 --> or signs of profession, which should distinguish the Church of the Jewish people from other nations. IIII. That they might be exercises of obedience, of confession, of invocation, and of other virtues. V. That to the regenerate they might be testimonies, pledges and ***σφραγίδες*** (seals) of the promise, which should stir up and confirm faith. VI. That to the unregenerate also they might be exercises of discipline, and might bridle them, lest either in outward conversation they should be shut out from the polity of Moses, or should too much indulge their own lusts. VII. That out of the sacrifices the Priests might have their living, and ***ἱκανὰ*** (things sufficient) for the sustaining of life, **1 Cor. 9. Deut. 18.**

Moreover, it pleaseth me to add here, at the end of the doctrine of traditions, certain sayings which the Papists cite out of the ecclesiastical doctors for the confirming of their ***ἐθελοθρησκείας*** (self-chosen worships), that, the meaning of those doctors being rightly known, it may no more be able to cast any scruple into the simpler sort. Now thus saith **Basil**, in chapter 27 *Of the Holy Spirit*: *"Some doctrines we have in the Church from the written teaching, some from the tradition of the Apostles, delivered to us as it were by hand, in a mystery and secretly; which both have equal force unto the Christian religion, or unto godliness; and no man will gainsay them, who is but skilled in the rites of the Church. For if we should go about to reject the unwritten customs as things of small moment, we shall contract the doctrine and preaching of the Gospel itself to a bare name, and shall maim it of its estimation and reverence."* Likewise **Chrysostom** saith, in homily 4 on 2 Thess.: *"Many things have been delivered unto us without writing, which are equally worthy of credit with those which we have received by epistle."* And like sayings are met with elsewhere. But it is certain that neither Chrysostom, nor Basil, nor the other orthodox Fathers ever approved or admitted customs and constitutions that fight with the word of God, but spake only of the ecclesiastical rites which were instituted for the sake of good order, that the doctrine of the Gospel may be the more conveniently set forth; which doctrine, if it want order of time and godly ceremonies, beginneth to grow cheap among the rude common people. Wherefore godly traditions, and such as fight not with the word of God, we in no wise disallow, but judge them to be necessary, according to the rules set forth by us before, <!-- p.123 --> provided, namely, that superstition be absent, and the opinion of worship and of merit.

I will add also a question worthy of remembrance. In the year 1530, at the Diet of Augsburg, when our Confession was presented to the Emperor Charles, and there was disputing concerning traditions, this question was propounded to **Luther**, then abiding at Coburg: Whether Bernard, now thinking rightly, may choose for himself a certain observance at his own pleasure, and say: *"Let this be a worship of God, the glory and praise of God"*; or let it at least be a token of thankfulness, whereby a man giveth thanks unto God. To this question, oftentimes repeated, Luther made clear and weighty answer in these words:

> This case is plainly impossible, implying a manifest contradiction. For to say, "Let this be a worship of God, or *latria*," belongeth not to Bernard, but to God, one and alone. Wherefore Bernard, thinking rightly, will never be able to say at his own pleasure, "Let this be a worship of God"; or else he will cease to think rightly. For what else were this, than to exalt himself above God? And what else do the First Commandment and all the prophets forbid, than such a worship of works, etc.?

To this answer of Luther there was opposed the consideration of final causes, of thanksgiving, of discipline, and the like. To this objection Luther answered:

> Let the final causes be, if you will, altogether divine and lawful; yet by this addition ("Let it be a worship of God"), made by a man, they become the highest blasphemies and sacrileges, as though, forsooth, a man would teach God after what manner He is to be worshipped. I know therefore assuredly, that with that addition it is idolatry, and that without that addition it is only an exercise. For I will have the efficient cause considered; which if it be not present, the final cause is of necessity a chimera. I add this also, that that very chosen work (*electitium opus*), whether of Bernard or of others, even without the addition, is by its very singularity perilous, and well-nigh like unto an offence: as though, indeed, all that is ours were not already sufficiently ordained, by the general commandment of God, unto the worship of God, so that we should do, speak, bear and live all things unto the glory of God, unless some singular fellow come with a chosen work, without any necessity, without the word of God, and make a show of himself before us, as one about to exhibit something better than are the rest, which, already appointed from heaven, <!-- p.124 --> we have; whereas nothing can or ought to be reckoned like unto them, whatsoever a man may devise.
>
> Wherefore I say thus: It is nothing that any man boasteth of final causes, if there be no efficient cause. Now neither Princes nor Bishops have power to appoint traditions in the Church at their own pleasure: therefore it is nothing that they boast that the thing is godly, that it is lawful, that it is an exercise, that it is eucharist or thanksgiving, that it is discipline. The efficient cause being taken away, there can be no withstanding of any abominations of Antichrist, but that he will say, "This is godly, this is lawful," etc. For who shall here be judge? who shall determine? Therefore from the efficient cause I depart not. Examples are at hand, which press hard, and easily carry the day, if thou set aside the efficient cause, or the universal commandment of God. It was a godly thing to sacrifice a son, after the example of Abraham. It was a godly thing to slay the Kings of Canaan and the Amorites: therefore let us slay the magistrate, as Müntzer raved. And this is the first point, concerning the efficient cause, from which I depart not.
>
> Next I say that the final causes which are alleged are all impossible. For if I ask what those godly or lawful things are, that are to be appointed by traditions, it is answered me: thanksgiving, and discipline, etc. But these things are already appointed by the divine word. For God hath commanded to pray, to preach, to give thanks, to exercise the flesh, to instruct the people and the children. Let some work therefore be named that is subject to traditions: is it purgatory? pilgrimages? the worship of saints? These, forsooth, are without the word of God, and withal ungodly too. Seeing therefore there is no work which tradition can appoint anew, it followeth that it must lay hold of a work already commanded from heaven, and presuppose it as the predicament of substance, which it may afterward solemnly bring in, and clothe with quantity, quality, where, when, relation: as, to give thanks is a work of the divine commandment; but it becometh a work of tradition, when tradition dictateth: "At this hour, in this place, with this voice, for so long a space, in this habit, with this gesture, we will have it done." But these predicaments of accidents God hath willed to be free in His works, and to be truly accidents, and in no wise the substance. Now therefore the disputation returneth to its own circle, <!-- p.125 --> namely, that not even those accidents can any man lay upon others, unless he be commanded, and be an efficient cause called of God, as it is written: *"If any man speak and minister, let it be as by the word and power of God."* These things I hold of traditions in general.
>
> But whereas ***ἐθελοθρησκεία*** (self-chosen worship) is mingled with traditions, as when Bernard chooseth a certain fast for the giving of thanks, this I call not a tradition appointed by authority, but the chosen working of a private man; concerning which I hold that it is not lawful to choose such a fast or order. For this singularity would derogate from the other orders and works of God, seeing that all that is ours ought to be done in the name of Christ and to the glory of God; but that singularity would darken all these by a perilous example, and by an offence hurtful to the unskilful, and, as the Scripture speaketh, by a stumblingblock before the blind. Albeit, if this offence also were tolerated, tempered with this remedy, that it were taught withal (as by all means must be said) that any order of God whatsoever is much holier — as marriage, service, the order of parents and children, the magistracy, etc. — and much fitter for the giving of thanks, and a thousand times more approved of God, than that singular and chosen one, the peril were not to be feared, neither would that *ethelothreskia* prevail.
>
> But the sum is briefly this: that no ***λατρείαν*** (worship) is to be chosen without the express commandment and word of God. The word of God maketh the efficient cause, without which the final cause is nothing. The same is to be pronounced concerning the laws of the Church. The efficient cause is God, or the word of God. Besides Him none hath right to make laws in the Church. The formal cause is faith, charity, the cross, the Decalogue, the commandments, the promises. For nothing can be ordained better and more fitly than hath been ordained by the word of God, which hath set all things in weight, measure, number and order. The matter is good things spiritual and eternal; it is not perishing things, and the outward goods of the world. The final cause also is wanting to laws that are to be established by private pleasure, because all things are already commanded and appointed which are godly, lawful, honest, and necessary to salvation. Wherefore the final cause of laws in the Church ought to be eternal life. The material cause, <!-- p.126 --> the sinner to be justified, believing, and righteousness before God. The efficient, the Lord of eternal life. The formal, the vocal word taught and apprehended in the spirit, not marked only on paper or by the voice. For what is the law of the Church? It is the word delivered by God, believed in the spirit unto eternal life. And these things agree not at all with outward laws.
>
> But they urge that some traditions may be retained for thankfulness' sake, and chiefly private Masses. This pretext I regard not at all. Hezekiah brake the brasen serpent, nothing regarding that it was said to be kept for a memorial and praise of the divine work. The godly King had more respect to the certain abuse and evil occasion, than to the doubtful and uncertain fruit of praise. But of these things more largely elsewhere. For let the doctrine of faith and of works be had entire, that is, the Gospel and the ministry, and concerning ceremonies and traditions it will be easy to treat and to agree.

Thus far Luther, whose words, for most weighty causes, we have thought good to set down here for the sake of students, for the instructing of their judgement concerning hard matters. Now concerning traditions let students read the profitable treatise of **D. Chemnitz** in the first book of the *Examination of the Council of Trent*, and the books written of traditions by the Theologian **Hamelmann**. Now let us return to the third commandment.

## *Cultus Tertii Praecepti* · The Worships of the Third Commandment

When we speak of the worships of this Commandment, it is profitable at the outset to hold fast this admonition: That some commandments of the Decalogue are absolute, which require the obedience of a man toward God privately, even when the man is separated from the common society of men, as are the First and Second Commandment; but that other Commandments are relative, which cannot be exercised outside the society of men, and are occupied in the mutual relation of certain persons, as this third, the Fourth, and the other Commandments.

Now what the true worships of this third Commandment are, is shown <!-- p.127 --> by the word *to sanctify*, that is, to hallow, to dedicate, to give unto God. For the things which God hath made for our profit, the same He willeth us in turn to use unto the glory of God. So God sanctified the Sabbath, that is, chose it for our benefit, as a thing ratified, holy and sure, for the ceasing and rest of our labours. This same Sabbath He willeth us in turn to use unto the glory of God; He willeth the same to be sanctified by us, that day to be given and dedicated unto God for the declaring of the works of the Lord, for the preservation of the ecclesiastical ministry. Therefore a peculiar word is added to this Commandment: *Remember.*

Now hither pertaineth the entire doctrine of the duties both of the teachers themselves and ministers of the word of God, and of the hearers; the comparing of which may briefly be made after this manner.

The whole of the duties of godly teachers and ministers of the Church consisteth in faithfulness, **1 Cor. 4**: *"No more is required in dispensers, or stewards, than that they be faithful."* Now this faithfulness is stirred up, when godly ministers consider whom they serve, in what office they are and of what kind it is, and what is the end of their office. They who consider these three things can be faithful, and ought to be.

Whom we serve, Paul declareth, saying: *"We are ministers of Christ, and stewards of the mysteries of God."* Likewise: ***θεοῦ ἐσμεν συνεργοί*** (we are labourers together with God). We serve God, the Father, the Son, and the Holy Spirit. And the ministry is not of man, nor drawn forth out of human reason, but is a dispensing of the mysteries of God, those things which are hidden and secret before human reason, yea, foolishness before the judgement of men. The mysteries of God are comprehended in the Gospel, or in all the Articles of the faith. Hence the Apostles call themselves servants of Jesus Christ. And so all godly ministers may glory that they are servants, not of the world, but of God, and of the Lord Jesus Christ. And this is a great consolation to godly ministers, which deservedly sharpeneth and nourisheth faithfulness.

Of what kind the office is, appeareth from the definition of the ministry. For the ministry of the Gospel is the divine commandment of teaching the word of God, the Law and the Gospel, of administering <!-- p.128 --> the Sacraments, of declaring the Remission of sins, to many or to single persons, of ordaining ministers of the Church, of excommunicating the contumacious that persevere in manifest crimes, and of absolving and receiving them that repent. All these things are done, and are, singularly wonderful, and wonderfully singular.

The end of this ministry, so secret and so great, lieth in the hearers: namely, that teachable and believing souls be brought unto God by the voice of the Gospel, and not otherwise; or, that the Church of God be in this manner gathered and preserved. And so the godly teacher is as it were a certain middle person between God and those who are to be taught — namely, as touching the doctrine, which the godly teacher taketh from God, and setteth before men and his hearers. These three things deservedly stir up faithfulness, especially since it is certain that an account must one day be rendered of the administration of this office, than which there is none harder, none grander, none more splendid, none more perilous, none more lasting, none more acceptable to God, if it be rightly administered.

The Son of God saith: *"Where two or three are gathered together in my name, there will I be in the midst of them."* Let all who teach and all who hear the voice and doctrine of the Gospel think upon this saying; let them know that in their assembly the Son of God is present, and heareth what things are said, and with what mind, and seeth how the things heard are received.

Now how a godly and faithful minister of the Church may rightly discharge his office, these three things show: I. Knowledge of the true doctrine concerning God. II. Constancy. III. Diligence.

Of the knowledge of doctrine it hath been said above, in the second Commandment. But since in this life there is not, nor can be, a perfect knowledge of the mysteries of God, all godly teachers ought to acknowledge their own ignorance, and the weakness of human reason, and to ask help of God, and to think with themselves that the greatest part of those things which they know is the least part of those which they know not. And let them be instant in reading and <!-- p.129 --> frequent study, knowing that to be most true which is wont to be said:

> And what a man knoweth too little, none is able to teach.  

And in this manner let them allay pride and arrogance, and acknowledge themselves to be learners of the alphabet (*alphabetarios*); and yet let them not omit always to give thanks unto God for those few things which they have learned and are able to teach. Many things assuredly we understand, which we cannot attain to in words; and many things we utter, which in this life we cannot sufficiently understand.

By constancy is understood constant speech in doctrine, and also a constant life, so far as may be, in sobriety, in concord without envy, without ambition, suspicion, grudge, drunkenness and hatred. And this constancy is understood both spiritually and civilly in the whole life: namely, that the minister of the Church be not vain, light, inconstant, ***δίγλωττος*** (double-tongued), a dissembler, headlong in speaking, passing judgement upon all matters which yet he hath never so much as tasted with the tip of his lips, as is often wont to be done at this day. Likewise that he be not ***κενόδοξος*** (vainglorious), a talker of great things, a talker of vain things, nor be easily moved either by praises or by the dispraise of himself. Rightly saith **Augustine**: *"We ought neither greatly to rejoice when we are praised, nor to be made sorrowful when we are dispraised; because neither can another's injury deprave, nor another's praise crown us."* And elsewhere he saith that it is better for a godly minister of the word to be dispraised than to be praised: for when he is dispraised, this is then done without peril to himself; but when he is praised, it is done with peril, lest, namely, he thereupon begin to wax proud, and that befall him which befell Aesop's crow. So also **Gregory** saith godly: *"What can it hurt, if all men detract, and conscience defend?"* But chiefly let there be constancy in concord with one's colleagues and with the true Church, according to the Psalm: *"Behold, how good and how pleasant it is for brethren to dwell together in unity!"* And the Son of God prayeth that all the godly may be one, as He and the Father are one.

Hither pertain the sentences, **in the former to Timothy, the fourth**: *"Be thou an example of the believers, in doctrine, in integrity of manners, in charity, in spirit, in faith, in purity."* And **Tit. 2**: *"In all things shew thyself a pattern of good works."* **Matt. 5**: *"Ye are the salt of the earth,"* <!-- p.130 --> to wit, by teaching the Law. *"Ye are the light of the world,"* by teaching the doctrine of the Gospel. *"Let your light so shine before men, that they may see your good works, and that your heavenly Father may be glorified."*

Diligence is understood in all the parts of the ministry, that is, in the true preaching of the Law and of the Gospel, in godly meditation and frequent reading, in the lawful administration of the Sacraments, of Baptism and of the Lord's Supper, in the declaring of the Remission of sins to them that repent, in comforting the sick, in the excommunication of the contumacious and the wicked.

Moreover, the duty of hearers and learners consisteth in these three: in Diligence, in Reverence, and in Obedience.

Diligence is understood in upholding all the parts of the ministry, by hearing, learning, embracing and confessing the true doctrine of the Gospel, in the lawful use of the Sacraments, and in adorning the profession of the doctrine with godliness, and with honesty of manners and of life.

Reverence is to acknowledge the dispensers and Stewards of the mysteries of God, that is, to know assuredly that the ministers of the Church discharge a divine ministry, and to give thanks unto God that by the voice of ministers He gathereth unto Himself an eternal Church.

Obedience is understood in those things which pertain to the ministry, where the word of God requireth obedience. When the Commandments of God are set forth, and when exhortations are made unto good works, godly hearers ought to obey. For, *"He that heareth you heareth me; he that despiseth you despiseth me,"* saith the Son of God.

When godly hearers perform these three things, there must needs of its own accord follow ***ἐπιείκειαν*** (equity, or gentleness), and the contributing ***τῶν ὑπαρχόντων*** (of one's substance) for the preservation of the ministry. Now ***ἐπιείκεια*** is not slanderously to wrest the things rightly said and done of godly ministers, and to forgive them some infirmities of manners which are not enormous, and to know that they are not yet Angels, but men. The contribution of means for the maintaining of those who teach in churches and in <!-- p.131 --> Schools pertaineth hither; and in this third Commandment mention ought by all means to be made of the contribution of means to the ministry, concerning which Paul discourseth at length in **the former to the Corinthians, the ninth**: *"The Lord hath ordained that they which preach the Gospel should live of the Gospel."* And **1 Tim. 5**: *"They that rule well are worthy of double honour,"* that is, of maintenance and of reverence. And the common little books recite three works of the Sabbath: to be present at the public ceremonies, prayer, and oblation; where oblation is nothing else than when each man offereth some gift, and some part is given to the ministers, and the rest is distributed to the poor. That this was the custom, both histories, and the old canons, and many footprints testify: as the word *Collect*, or ***συμβολῆς*** (contribution), which is yet extant in the Church; and tithes, of which some proportion is of the law of nature, as the sounder Jurists speak. And perhaps the name of the *Mass* was made from thence, which among the Hebrews signifieth tribute and contribution, **Deuteronomy the sixteenth**: *"Thou shalt make a freewill* Missa *of thine hand,"* that is, a contribution according to thy means. And ***ἀγάπη*** (love-feast) signifieth alms of this kind, which were contributed in the Synaxis. And the example flowed from the people of Israel, among whom he that was about to sacrifice brought a victim, and gave a part to the Priests. And therefore it is written: *"Thou shalt not appear before me empty."* Rightly therefore is oblation numbered among the works of the Sabbath. But let the end of the contribution be, to help the ministers, and to succour the poor, and not to render unto God a peculiar worship, and to merit grace by this work.

It is wont to be said in a proverb: *"He that loveth God honoureth also the Priests"*; and this he doth in consideration of their office, which is plainly divine. And it is certain that the haters of the Priests are for the most part ungodly, and are at length wonderfully and horribly punished.

A just complaint doth **Christoph Scheurl** of Nuremberg take up in a certain oration of his, saying:[^P06-4]

> While I turn over annals, monuments and histories, I find that there never was any time, nor any age, in which the Priests <!-- p.132 --> were so illiberally and unworthily entreated as in this our time.
>
> Among the Egyptians, when all the revenues were divided most equally into three parts, the Priests claimed the first part for themselves, and were of so great authority among them, that all things were done by their counsel and direction; and, being exempt and altogether free from every burden, they held the second place of dignity after the Kings.
>
> The Indians, when they divided the whole people into seven tribes, appointed the tribe of the Priests the first; which, free moreover from every burden, they deemed worthy of the highest honour.
>
> How fair, wise and godly is that saying of **Constantine the Great**: *"If with mine own eyes I had seen a Priest of God sinning, I would cover him with my cloak and purple, lest he should be seen of any, and lest his sin should become known to others."*
>
> At this day scarce anything is more often heard than the accusing of the Priests, who do themselves also traduce and accuse one another, hate and persecute one another in horrible ways. But the godly and sound deservedly bewail this state, and long for some Constantine, who may burn the slanderous libels, and say to the ministers of the divine word: *"Go, and settle your causes among yourselves, and accuse not one another, exposing yourselves to mockery among the men of State; for it is not meet that we should judge them whose lips ought to keep knowledge, and at whose mouth others ought to seek the Law, seeing they are the Angels of the Lord of hosts."*
>
> It is well known that **Alexander the Great**, being wroth with Judea for denying him aid against Tyre and the Persians, came into Judea to lay it waste. But when Jaddus the high Priest, arrayed in the priestly habit, together with the whole company of the Priests, had gone forth to meet Alexander, and had humbly sued for peace, Alexander straightway, at the very sight of him, lighted down from his horse, reverently saluted and received Jaddus, and, <!-- p.133 --> prostrate on the ground, and become a suppliant to Jaddus, graciously promised peace. When his Captains were held with wonder at this thing, Alexander said: That in Macedonia, before the war, he had seen in a dream such a likeness of a deity, arrayed in like habit, by which he had been both called into Asia, and commanded to undertake war against the Persians, and assured of obtaining the victory; for that likeness had stretched forth its right hand to him, and had promised defence and success. Since therefore he now saw a Priest like unto that likeness, he concluded both that this people was a care unto God, and that he himself had been helped by the true God of this people, and therefore that he would spare the people and do them good. Which thing Alexander liberally performed, especially when the prophecy of Daniel concerning the Macedonians had been shown him; and he bestowed many and notable gifts upon the temple, and willed the whole nation of the Jews to be for seven years free and exempt from every burden of tributes.
>
> The same Alexander, being yet very young in years, when in sacrificing he was offering frankincense and precious perfumes with both hands, and Leonidas said to him, *"O Alexander, when thou shalt bear rule over the lands that bear spices, then mayest thou send up smoke after this manner so largely and lavishly,"* answered: *"O Leonidas, this I chiefly desire of thee, that, if thou wishest for prosperity, thou cease to be niggardly with the sacred deities."*
>
> It is reported of **Rudolph, Count of Habsburg** and Landgrave of Upper Alsace, the ancestor of Maximilian the First, that once, riding abroad, having lighted upon a Priest who by chance came to meet him, and was minded to go to comfort the sick and give them the communion, he asked him why he made his journey on foot; and, having understood the man's poverty, gave him for a gift the horse on which he himself sat; by which example another Count, who alone was present, offered his own also for a gift to the sacristan. This reverence toward the ministry is written to have gotten for the Habsburger all happiness, eminence and success for thirty years.
>
> Of **Maximilian the First** it is certain that he everywhere <!-- p.134 --> bound his soldiers by an oath, that in war they should abstain from Churches and Sacred things, and from the ministers of the Church, and should keep their hands unpolluted; which thing also my Nurembergers have been accustomed to do by decree of the Senate.
>
> These examples ought to move us to help the ministry with reverence. For if Princes, even Heathen, and ignorant of the true God, and serving gods of wood and stone, yielded so great honour to their Priests, with what care, with what diligence are our Priests to be honoured by us, and the ministers of God, who with perpetual study give themselves to the divine worship, and take care for our salvation, and eat our sins, and for us, as touching their office, shall render account in the divine examination? whom the Saviour Himself willed to be called now Christs, now Kings, commanding also in the old Testament that they who would not obey them should be put to death. But if God willed so much to be yielded of old to them who handled burnt offerings and victims, what, I pray, shall we think is to be yielded to ours? who distribute to the faithful the food of eternal life, and, when they are scarce received from their mothers' wombs, straightway wash and purify them from the stain of conception, and ingraft them into Christ, and, that I may so speak, incorporate them; without whom we cannot so much as be Christians, nor rejoice in any Sacred thing? None of these things can any of Kings, any of Emperors, effect by reason of his office. Therefore **Ambrose** saith godly: *"Gold is not so much more precious than lead, as the Priestly order is higher than Kingly power."*

But since in this age we speak to bellies, and all things that are said are wrongfully wrested to the worst part, I cease, mindful of the saying: *"Ye shall be hated of all men for my name's sake."* For the world is not worthy of the title and praise of Thankfulness, whereby the cares and labours of godly Teachers and ministers of the Church should be recompensed. Let us therefore look for rewards from Him who said: *"Your reward shall be great in heaven."* In the mean time let us be patient, and commend ourselves unto God, who will nourish us, defend us, preserve us, save us. <!-- p.135 --> Let us also take heed that we give not offences to the weak, as is easily wont to be done, especially when the ministers of the Church are covetous, lustful, filthy in speech, unseemly in gestures, contentious, drunken, impatient, domineering, slanderous. Let those enormities be far from godly and sound Teachers, which at length provoke not men only, but God also, and burden and accuse the conscience, and manifoldly heap up and draw on many other evils, public and private, as the examples of our most miserable age do testify. God have mercy upon us in His clemency.

## *De Peccatis contra III. Praeceptum* · Of the Sins against the Third Commandment

Let it first be spoken of the error of the Jews, who contend that no outward work is to be done on the Sabbath day. For the Maccabees did not violate the Sabbath by fighting. Nor the disciples of Christ, plucking the ears of corn. And rightly doth Christ teach us, saying: *"I desire mercy rather than sacrifice."* The duties of love are above ceremonies. And all duties which are not of evil example on feast days are not to be reproved, provided only that we yield to the public ministry its due honour, **Matthew the twelfth**. Therefore the superstitious observation of the Sabbath, which is feigned by the Jews, is in no wise to be approved.

The Jurists have an honest Law, **C. *de Iudiciis***: *"It hath seemed good that in all things chief regard be had to justice and written equity, rather than to strict law."* And **Menander** saith: ***καλὸν οἱ νόμοι σφόδρα, ὁ δ᾽ ὁρῶν τοὺς νόμους λίαν ἀκριβῶς, συκοφάντης γίνεται*** (the laws are a right good thing; but he that looketh into the laws too narrowly becometh a false accuser). Now that is named strict law (*strictum ius*), which is a cavilling and superstitious interpretation of the written Law, whereby the words of the Law are referred to a case foreign to the principal intention of the Law: as when the Jews turn the words concerning the Sabbath into a superstitious idleness, so that not only do they not cook on that day, nor kindle fires in their ovens, but do not so much as pluck <!-- p.136 --> the snuff from the lamps with their own hand. They allege for cause, that these duties are to be esteemed not by the greatness or smallness of the things, but by the words of the Laws. This is strict law, that is, cavilling law. For the principal intention of the Law is to forbid servile works, such as are continual, that is, such as draw men away from the divine ceremonies, and hinder the ministry. The Law doth not comprise the other cases, or the works of household necessity, which draw not away from the ceremonies and from the ministry. Christ, in **Matt. 12**, refuteth the Jews by five arguments. I. By the example of David, **1 Kings 21** [1 Sam. 21], eating the shewbread, when, fleeing the wrath of Saul, he asked provision for the way of Abimelech the Priest, and no other food was at hand. II. On the Sabbath it is lawful to sacrifice victims, to circumcise infants, and to administer other things which further the ministry of the word. Therefore the duties of godliness fight not among themselves. III. In **Hos. 6** it is said: *"I desire mercy,"* that is, the duties of charity, *"rather than sacrifices,"* that is, the outward worship of the Law. IIII. Christ is Lord of the Sabbath, and of the whole Law. Therefore, as the other ceremonies, so also this ceremony of the Sabbath He is able to change. V. From the less to the greater. If it is lawful to save a beast on the Sabbath, much more a man. Mark addeth a sixth Argument: God instituted the Sabbath for man, and did not create man for the Sabbath. Therefore they do ungodly who apply not the Sabbath to the profit of man.

These things being considered, let now the remaining sins be recited which fight with the third Commandment; as are: To contemn the ministry of the Gospel. Never or seldom to hear the Gospel. Not to use the sacraments, or to apply them to ungodly uses, to gain, or to ungodliness. To be negligent in teaching. To institute ungodly ceremonies. To draw others away by example from the public ceremonies, and to give occasion that they think more lightly of them. To bestow the Feast Days upon banquets, and games, upon wrestling-places, huntings, and vain shows, shooting-matches, and not upon godly meditation. On a Feast Day to ply works that hinder the ministry of the word and of the ceremonies. To shun the ministry <!-- p.137 --> of the word, because both the dignity and the means of ministers are slender. To be sparing, sordid and close-fisted in helping the studies and labours of the ministry. To abolish the studies that are profitable for the preservation of the ministry, etc.

## *Secunda Tabula Decalogi* · The Second Table of the Decalogue

The Second Table containeth the Laws which are the bonds of the Civil life, in which God willeth us to be occupied, that one may teach another concerning Religion, and that there may be exercises of all the worships which are comprehended in the first Table.

For the fellowship of men was instituted and ordained by God for these causes: I. That this order may be a testimony concerning God and concerning Providence. II. That the human race may in this manner be preserved safe and quiet. III. That one may teach another concerning God and concerning good things, and that with common voice they may celebrate God, and one show to another examples of virtues. IIII. That in common labours faith, invocation and confession may be exercised. V. That each man by his duties may declare love to his neighbour, and render acceptable worships unto God, who earnestly requireth the works of love, according to that saying: *"I desire mercy rather than sacrifice."* Likewise: *"Let your light shine before men,"* etc. And the Son of God saith that the commandment of the love of our neighbour is like unto the first commandment of the love of God, **Matt. 22**. And He nameth it *like* for three causes: I. Because the obedience is a worship of God, even as the worship of the first Commandment. II. Because the obligation is like, under pain of eternal damnation. III. That the error may be refuted of the Pharisees and others, who think that the love of our neighbour may be neglected, and that ceremonies and oblation are better than it; even as the Papists once deemed it a greater sin to eat flesh on the sixth day of the week than to kill a man. And many of the Pharisees lived in sins, and afterward would make amends for their sins <!-- p.138 --> by sacrifices, as is still the opinion of the Papists concerning the Mass. Therefore Christ showeth that this Commandment of love is not to be neglected, and saith that it is like unto the first. And this is to be set against the Monks and other Papists, who feign works of supererogation; concerning which **Tauler**, who was himself also a Monk, and flourished in the year of Christ 1340, writeth in his sermon *Of the Wise Men*: *"Many men are not content with that myrrh which is given them of God, but will needs lay more upon themselves, and they weaken the head, and are overwhelmed with phantasies, and have suffered long and much, and yet do not rightly, and little grace redoundeth unto them: for they lean upon their own ordinances, whether in penance, or in abstinence, or in prayer, or in devotion. Here God is constrained to wait upon their leisure,[^P06-1] until they finish their own things; but nothing cometh of it. For God assuredly so doth, that He rewardeth nothing but His own work. In heaven He crowneth nothing for ever, but His own work, and not thy works."* Thus Tauler.

# *Quartum Praeceptum* · The Fourth Commandment

The worships of the fourth Commandment consist in the duties of superior and inferior men, or of those persons who bear rule and who obey. We must therefore speak first of the duty of parents and of children; then of the duties of husbands and of wives; of the duties of magistrates and of subjects; of the duties of masters and of servants.

But before we speak of these, there is first to be considered the wonderful counsel of God, who implanteth in men ***στοργὰς φυσικὰς*** (natural affections), the natural love of parents toward children, of children toward parents, of kinsfolk toward one another, etc. For nothing is more wonderful than these, and nothing sweeter; nothing also more bitter, than when any evil befalleth those whom ***στοργὴ φυσικὴ*** (natural affection) embraceth; as many examples show, as in Absalom, the son of King David — how bitter was the grief of David, uttering <!-- p.139 --> these words: *"Absalom my son, my son Absalom, my son, who granteth that I might die for thee, Absalom my son, my son?"*

Now there is no doubt but that David considered not so much the death of his son as his damnation, and for this bore himself so mournfully, seeing and knowing that the fruit of his own flesh, of his own blood, or of his own seed, was rejected and damned of God. For how great a grief is it for good parents to see their children haled to punishment in this life? But how much greater a grief is it for the godly to know that the fruits of their own body are damned? Considering this, David crieth out: *"My son."*

How great a ***στοργὴ*** (affection) is there in the words of Abraham, when he was about to sacrifice his son Isaac, **Gen. 22**: *"Isaac spake unto Abraham his father"* — he said, I say, *"My father"*; and he answered, *"Here am I, my son."* He said: *"Behold the fire and the wood: but where is the lamb for a burnt offering?"* Abraham answered: *"God will provide himself a lamb for a burnt offering, my son."*

How brotherly a ***στοργὴ*** is seen in the example of Joseph, **Gen. 45**, which cannot be read without tears. And **Gen. 46**, when Jacob cometh to Joseph his son, Joseph falleth on his father's neck, and weepeth on his neck. And Israel saith: *"Now let me die, since I have seen thy face, because thou art yet alive."* There are many other examples, which daily experience setteth forth in our own life also.

Now the causes are to be considered for which God hath implanted ***στοργὰς φυσικὰς*** in the breasts of men. The causes are three: I. That the *storgae* may be the bonds of human society, or of the Household, which is the seed-plot of the Church of God and of the Commonwealth. II. That they may be the seeds of beneficence and of the other virtues necessary for the preservation and defence of the human race. III. That they may be admonitions and images of the true and burning ***στοργῆς*** of God the Father toward the Son, and toward us, who are beloved in the Beloved, **Ephes. the first**. And **Matt. the third**: *"This is my beloved Son, in whom I am well pleased."* **Ps. 103**: *"Like as a father pitieth his children, so hath the Lord had pity on us."* **Isaiah the forty-ninth**: *"Zion saith, The Lord God <!-- p.140 --> hath forsaken me, the Lord hath forgotten me. Can a mother forget her infant, that she should not have compassion on the son of her womb? And though she should forget her son, yet will I not forget thee. For I have graven thee upon my hands."* This ***Διαλογισμὸν*** (dialogue) **Stigel** hath expressed in most sweet verses, which I will set down here for children to learn:

> With plaintive mouth thus hath fair Sion spoken,  
> When she was sad with a fear that sought for God:  
> "Hast thou then left me, bearing so many hard evils,  
> And art thou not mindful of the afflicted one, O God, as before?"  
> To her God: "Shall any mother be able to lay aside care for her offspring,  
> Having no pity on that which she hath borne?  
> Yet if there be any so hard, and so implacable,  
> That she can be unmindful of her own issue,  
> I myself will never, forgetting thee, change my love;  
> Thou shalt ever be a beloved daughter unto me.  
> Behold, I keep thy walls, portrayed upon my hand,  
> That I may cover thee safe with a sure defence.  
> Behold, I carry thee in mine eyes; to me, who see all things,  
> As thou art nearest now, nearest shalt thou ever be."  

It is assuredly a foul thing to feign that the *storgae* are only opinions conceived in the mind from objects shown through sense, or inclinations of wills sprung from longer conversation[^P06-2] and closer custom, as the Stoics doted; whereas yet it is certain that, when nothing appeareth outwardly that may delight us — nay, the more infants are subject to diseases, and befouled, so that by others they can scarce be looked upon without loathing — so much the more are they loved and cherished by their parents: a thing which beareth the image of the immeasurable love of God toward us, who are befouled and defiled with manifold sins. But let us hold that which is true, that the *storgae* are natural motions of the heart, which is so framed from heaven by the wonderful counsel of God, and by His goodness and unspeakable wisdom, that it is affected by a sense of kindred either of blood or of spirit, and is enlarged with gladness when it perceiveth something welcome to befall kindred blood, and again is drawn together with sorrow in the calamity <!-- p.141 --> of a man joined by blood,[^P06-3] although he be, as is often wont to happen, far away, as experience testifieth. Oftentimes the heart is made sorrowful, when anything adverse befalleth thy parents, children or kinsfolk who are far absent, or when they die. For it is so ordered by nature, that like tendeth with great endeavour toward another thing of its own kind: even as the flame from a burning lamp leapeth most swiftly over into that which is nearest to the nature of fire, as into a fat and airy smoke, or other like matter; so it is likely that the animal spirit, plucked from one body by generation, and propagated into other bodies, seeketh conjunction, and is so often moved and stirred toward fellowship, as either the remembrance of kindred blood moveth the mind, or an outward appearance the sight, or report the hearing; nay, as hath been said, sometimes without such helps, by a certain peculiar motion of its own, and one plainly divine, the animal spirit is roused, so as to move the heart to sadness or to gladness, at the time when it goeth notably ill or well with a kinsman far absent. And it is certain that the better the natures are, and the finer the spirits, the greater are the *storgae* and the sense of them: even as all Heroical natures have notable *storgae* toward their offspring and kindred; whence the Germans have elegantly said: *Treffliche Leut haben Kinderlieb* (excellent folk are fond of children). Men of excellence are ***φιλόστοργοι*** (tenderly affectionate) toward infants:

> And a noble mind readily taketh the motions of feeling.
