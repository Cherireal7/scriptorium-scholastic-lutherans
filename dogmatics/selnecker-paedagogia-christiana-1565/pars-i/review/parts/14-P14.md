And in the funeral Oration upon Basil, **Nazianzen** — as **Nicephorus** citeth him, book 10, chap. 26 — refuting the witless speech of Festus and of such as he, saith thus: *"I suppose that all who are of sounder mind confess this, that learning is the chief among the good things which are ours: not only that more excellent learning of our own, which, neglecting all elegance and ostentation of speech, cleaveth to salvation alone and to the beauty of the things that are to be understood, but that outward learning also, which many Christians despise and look down upon as a thing that lieth in wait, deceitful, and leading farther away from God — by a persuasion verily perverse and perilous. For the more liberal learning is not to be despised; but they are to be accounted depraved and <!-- p.283 --> rude who despise it, who would have all others be like unto themselves, that in the common throng of such as are like them they may lie hid, and escape the reproof of their own ignorance."* **Augustine** also, in book 2 *Of Christian Doctrine*, chap. 40, saith thus: *"As the Israelites carry off the idols, and the vessels of gold and of silver, of the Egyptians, and apply them to uses righteous and pleasing unto God: so Christians ought not utterly to forsake Philosophy and the liberal disciplines which are extant in the books of the Heathen, for this cause, that the Heathen impiously abused them, but ought to transfer them to the rightful use of preaching the Gospel."* Let these sayings of godly antiquity suffice, although more also might be added out of others. And it is certain that Paul, **Coloss. 2**, speaketh of the abuse and transformation of the heavenly doctrine into Philosophy, and of the confounding of the kinds of doctrine, not of demonstrations, which are truth divinely shown. Let us therefore pray God that He would defend, govern and preserve godly Schools, as the fountains of doctrines, and bend the minds of godly Princes, that they may be nursing fathers of Churches and of well-ordered Schools; and withal let us not mingle together the kinds of doctrine, or Philosophy with the Gospel, in those things which are of the Spirit, but let us bring every understanding into captivity to the obedience of Christ, and let us rejoice if by the benefit of God we have gotten unto ourselves some small learning, how little soever, and a knowledge of the necessary arts and tongues: that we may be able to use it rightly and soberly, both for the understanding of many places of the heavenly Scripture and for the illustrating of true doctrine, and to bring it as a handmaid and waiting-woman unto the Queen. Let us not be enemies of the gifts of God, but let us admire them and make much of them, and hold them set in their own place, that there may be some use of them when need shall be. Rightly indeed did **G. Nyssen** say, that we ought not to desire the monuments of men who were not comprehended in the true assembly of God upon earth, because it is written: *"My sheep hear my voice."* These things are true concerning the certainty of our doctrine, life and salvation, neither doth any man think or <!-- p.284 --> speak otherwise, who thinketh rightly at all. But that doctrine should be adorned, unfolded, and learnedly set forth — can that be unpleasing to God? ***τὸ καλὸν ὅπου ἂν ποθ' εὑρεθείη τῆς ἀληθείας ἴδιον ἐστι***, said one: to wit, that which is good, wheresoever it be found, ought to be accounted the proper possession of the truth. And Philosophers, Poets and Physicians also are able, in their own manner and place, to frame a hymn unto God the author of nature, and to offer gifts unto Christ, as did the Persian Magi: gold, as to the King of Kings; frankincense, as to God; and myrrh, as to the Priest and the Victim, or the Lamb and the suffering Man. But let us now break off these things, and go forward to the third object of truth.

Thirdly, the objects of truth are covenants, honest leagues, honest contracts, judgements, testimonies, safe-conducts; lest that befall which standeth in the common rhymes:

> Many years being now gone by,  
> There is no faith in covenants;  
> Honey in the mouth, words of milk,  
> Gall in the heart, fraud in the deeds.

Fourthly, there is the signifying of one's will toward others, namely, to be candid, not to lay snares, and frankly to say, when there is need, what thou thinkest.

That godly Princes and Magistrates may be able to propagate, defend and preserve this truth, to the glory of God and to the profit of the Church and of the Commonwealth, let them diligently attend to these three words, which are:

- Flattery (*Adulatio*),
- Calumny (*Calumnia*),
- The Master's Eye (*Oculus Domini*).

Of which the two former destroy the Truth; the third, consisting in judgement and piety, helpeth and furthereth the truth.

Flattery (*adulatio*) is so called as though it were *adaulatio*, because it pertaineth chiefly to courts (*aulae*), and is most of all found in them. For most true are the verses of **Menander**:

> ***Ὁ βίος μάλιστα τοῖς πονηροῖς ἥδεται,***  
> ***Πράττει δ' ὁ κόλαξ ἄριστα πάντων, δεύτερος***  
> <!-- p.285 -->***Ὁ συκοφάντης, ὁ δὲ κακοήθης τρίτατος.***
>
> This life delighteth chiefly in the wicked,  
> But of them all the flatterer holdeth the first place,  
> And liveth by far the most pleasantly of all.  
> Next, the second place is the sycophant's:  
> For to hear calumnies is exceeding sweet.  
> But the third place is granted to the envious man,  
> Who most craftily knoweth the arts of doing hurt.

Such flatterers observe only what things please their masters and what displease, and accommodate themselves, and their manners and actions, to the will of their masters, even as a mirror receiveth into itself any images whatsoever, and as the Chameleon receiveth into itself all colours save the colour white — men estranged from all truth. *"Whatsoever they say, I praise; if again they deny it, I praise that also; they say Yea, I say Yea."* And **Juvenal**, Satire 3:

> — Thou laughest: with a greater guffaw  
> He is shaken; he weepeth, when he hath seen the tears of his friend,  
> Yet grieveth not; if in the winter season thou ask for a little fire,  
> He taketh his cloak; if thou say, I am hot, he sweateth.

If their masters love to drink, they are named liberal and bountiful. If their masters love, go a-whoring and commit adultery, these men avouch that all is done most handsomely, saying: Heroes of this sort ought not to be monks, but to follow the footsteps of David and Solomon, who were not content with one wife. If their masters are overmuch given to hunting, these men come running, and recount and show this thing and that. If their masters seek after pomps, the flatterers stoutly furnish them forth. If they are men of war, they will not leave off until they both drain their masters dry and bring them to the slaughter. If their masters are wroth with any, these men also are angry, and by calumnies increase the hatred; or, if they be the more humane, they are altogether silent, and defend not the innocent. If their masters favour the doctrine which they call Lutheran, they too are Lutherans. But if otherwise, they also change their opinion: even as, when George, Duke of Saxony, was opposed to **Luther**, the most part were Papists; but when Henry succeeded George, on a sudden <!-- p.286 --> all had now become Lutherans. And at this day, in the doctrine of the Lord's Supper, many nourish something monstrous, and wait for the death of their Princes, whom, as being strangers to that faction, they now in some sort fear; and they feign that they think rightly. When their masters make music, they are Apollos, although they bellow. When they wrestle, they are Hercules. When they are drunken, they are Bacchuses.

The familiars of King Alexander had copied his bent neck, and in speaking the harshness of his voice. For the King had gone with such a neck, and had had a somewhat harsh voice. When King Dionysius was dim of sight, his flatterers, running one against another, and throwing down the dishes[^P14-1] at supper, had themselves also feigned dimness of sight.

Flatterers of this kind serve only themselves and their own belly — of all men the most lying, and most hurtful both to masters and to subjects. They suck out the blood like flies, as Nicesias had said to Alexander. And how great evils have always arisen out of flattery, the histories everywhere show. And **Socrates** rightly answered one who asked which were the worst of all living creatures, saying: *"If thou meanest among the wild, the tyrant; if among the tame, the flatterer."* And **Diogenes** judged it better to go ***πρὸς κόρακας, ἢ πρὸς κόλακας*** (to the crows than to the flatterers), seeing that crows tear and devour dead men, but flatterers the living.

Let masters learn to know such flatterers, and shun as a plague those ***συγκατανευσιφάγους***, or nodding trencher-mates (*connuepulones*). *"For the tongue of the flatterer persecuteth more than the hand of the persecutor,"* saith **Augustine**. No flatterer is faithful to his master. And nowhere are there greater vices than where flattery hath place. And at this day flatterers cast their masters into these three things chiefly: into rashness, into perils or losses, and into hatred. These three are elegantly depicted in the book written concerning the Emperor Maximilian, the Title whereof is *Timiocharis*, as some call it.[^P14-2]

Darius, King of the Persians, began a war with the Greeks, which he could not finish. After his death his son <!-- p.287 --> Xerxes renewed the same, arming seven hundred thousand out of his own Kingdom, and three hundred thousand of auxiliaries, so that it hath not undeservedly been handed down that rivers were dried up by his army, and that all Greece could scarce contain his army; he is said also to have had ships to the number of ten hundred thousand; insomuch that Xerxes himself, beholding his army, began to weep, saying that after an hundred years there would not be alive of them so much as one. Now when Xerxes had long deliberated with his Counsellors what was to be done, all advised that he should go forward, and stirred up yet more the King, who was mad with an overweening estimation of himself — all save one, Demaratus the Lacedaemonian, who dissuaded the war, and said: That that very multitude, wherein he pleased himself, being disordered and unwieldy, was to be feared, and that victory lay not in multitude, etc. But the counsel of Demaratus was of no weight with the King, whom the flatterers were spurring on more and more. When therefore the King was making ready the war, and was now upon the march, Leonidas the Spartan, an excellent Captain, the enemy of Xerxes, joined to himself three hundred men, citizens, excelling in courage and strength, and seized the narrow way of Thermopylae, by which Xerxes must needs go; and so he brought Xerxes — who was threatening fetters to Neptune and darkness to the heaven — to the last despair, his army being scattered and overthrown. Xerxes was at last slain by his own men, and before his death he said: That all his Courtiers had flattered him, save one truthful man, Demaratus.

Alexander the Great, when he had been wounded by an arrow in battle, and his flatterers ran unto him, and named him the son of God, said: *"Ye proclaim me the son of Jupiter, but this wound crieth out that I am a man."*

Aristobulus the historian had written a book of the deeds of Alexander the Great, and in it had feigned many things most flatteringly. Wherefore Alexander, having read the book, sank it in the river Hydaspes, saying: *"Thou wert more worthy to be cast headlong into the same, who alone makest me to fight after this sort."*

Alexander Severus held flatterers in the greatest hatred, and commanded Thurinus Verconius, for his flatteries, to be <!-- p.288 --> bound to a stake, and, smoke being set against him, which he had ordered to be made of stubble and wet wood, he put him to death, the crier proclaiming: *"He is punished with smoke who sold smoke"* — and the incense of the Court, that is, the splendid promises of the Court.

Crates the Philosopher, when he had seen a great crowd of flatterers attending a certain powerful and wealthy young man, said: *"Truly I pity thy solitude."*

But it were overlong to rehearse more examples, whereof experience daily supplieth more than enough. And in the Sacred Scriptures flattery is often forbidden: **Prov. 24, 26, 27**; **Ps. 28, 55**; **Num. 16**, of Korah, Dathan and Abiram; **2 Kings [2 Sam.] 15**, of Absalom; **2 Paral. [2 Chron.] 24**, of the flatterers of King Joash; **2 Kings [2 Sam.] 16**, of the flatterers of David, who persuaded him to seize the goods of Mephibosheth; **Acts 12**, of Herod Agrippa; likewise **24**. And the history is well known of Ahithophel, the flatterer of David. And the fable also of Hercules, whom, after so many labours and victories, women deceived and ruled, putting upon him a woman's robe, and teaching him to work the wool, etc.

But the word *calumny* is derived either from ***καλεῖν***, to summon into judgement, or ***ἀπὸ τοῦ καλὸν μειοῦν***, to diminish and deprave that which is good. For it is nothing else than a depraving of the honest and the good, whether said or done, malicious and crafty, as **Lucian** defineth calumny. The Greeks name it ***Διαβολὴν***, from which the Devil (*Diabolus*) hath his name, that is, the calumniator, who depraveth the divine words and deeds, and interpreteth them for the worse, and perverteth them, as he seduced Eve in Paradise; and the Pharisees say that Christ casteth out devils through Beelzebub; and the adversaries call the Gospel heresy. Now since calumny is chiefly in the Courts of Princes, which it hath chosen out for itself as its own proper seats, as **Polybius** truly saith in book 4, godly Princes and Magistrates ought to take heed that they run not into this wickedness, which verily is not a simple lie, but altogether such a thing wherein there are two who do the wrong, and one who suffereth the wrong, as **Herodotus** wisely saith: *"For the calumniator doth wrong, accusing one who is not <!-- p.289 --> present. And he who believeth the calumniator, before he truly know the matter, doth wrong. But he who is absent suffereth wrong, both from the calumniator, and from him who, lending his ears to the calumniator, reputeth him for an evil man."*

Now these are to be considered, as the heads concerning Calumny:

**I.** Whence Calumny ariseth.

**II.** How great evils it bringeth with it.

**III.** How Calumny is to be resisted, and for what causes chiefly it is to be resisted.

**Of the first.** Seeing that Calumny is so common in every estate and kind of life, it is wont to be asked whence it ariseth. Now he who is the author of all evil is found to be the author of this wickedness also, namely the Devil, the Calumniator. And so all Calumny ariseth either from perversity of disposition and nature, or from evil and ungodly custom, or from the judgement and sense of an evil conscience, or out of envy, or out of inveterate hatred, or also out of envenomed anger and the desire of revenge.

Many are by nature and disposition so perverse and monstrous, that they take delight in calumnies; as that Philosopher, being asked why he spake evil of all men, answered: Because he could not do them evil.

Many also are accustomed to detractions of this kind, of whom the son of Sirach saith, **23**: *"He never becometh better, who hath accustomed himself to detraction."*

Many, when they have an evil cause, detract from those by whom they are reproved, or whom they fear lest they should lay open their deeds, and traduce and accuse them before their superiors.

*"To the envious, detraction is a dainty dish,"* saith **Pindar**: ***ὄψον αἱ κατηγορίαι τοῖς φθονεροῖς***. But that is wont to befall these men which is said in the verse:

> The envious man[^P14-3] pineth away at another's thriving fortunes.

Hither pertaineth the picture of Apelles, whereby he is said to have avenged Calumny. He had been accused before King Ptolemy <!-- p.290 --> of the conspiracy which was made at Tyre, Theodotas being the author of it, whereas he had never seen Tyre, nor known Theodotas. Ptolemy straightway judged that Apelles was to be punished as a faithless man, a traitor and an ingrate. But by chance one of the fellows of the conspiracy, being taken, had pity on the unhappy Apelles, and showeth his innocence, and delivereth Apelles. Ptolemy, coming to himself and changing his sentence, presenteth Apelles with an hundred talents, and delivereth his calumniator Antiphilus unto him for a bondman. Apelles therefore painteth calumny thus. First, there is a man, notable for exceeding long ears, such as Midas is said to have had, and he stretcheth forth his hand to Calumny as she draweth near from afar. This is the hearer of calumny, who lightly believeth. Next, there are about this man two silly women, Ignorance and Suspicion. After that is Calumny herself, exquisitely trimmed, and in countenance and in gesture of body showing forth savage fury, and wrath in a boiling breast. She holdeth in her left hand a torch belching flame. For calumny kindleth a conflagration, and bringeth forth a camel out of a fly. But with her right hand she haleth along with her a young man caught by the hair, who stretcheth his hands to them that are above, and calleth to witness the faith of the immortal God. Fourthly, there goeth before Calumny a pale man, foul to look upon, with a sight of the eyes by no means dull, and like to one in a consumption. And this is Envy. Calumny is followed by certain silly women, who exhort, furnish and deck their mistress. These are Treacheries and Deceits. Behind followeth Repentance, clad in black and all torn, in mourning apparel, who, with her head turned backward, with tears and shame receiveth Truth as she cometh from afar, according to the verse of **Stigelius**:

> — All things give place  
> To Justice, and the truth knoweth not how to be buried in the dust.

In the Fable of **Aesop** the little Fox is accused before the Lion, that she administereth affairs ill, and doeth many wrongs to the poor subjects, the hens and the geese, and oft-times killeth and devoureth them. The Fox cleareth herself, saying that they are seditious against King Lion, and oft-times cry aloud with neck and <!-- p.291 --> throat upraised and stretched wide, and as it were blow the trumpet of seditions, and that therefore she is constrained to restrain them, lest they contrive some mischief against the King.

Such also is the Fable of the Wolf, accusing the lamb that drank at the lower part of the stream, as though he troubled the water. *"Thou availest nothing, sacrilegious lamb,"* saith the Wolf; *"thou art ever in my way: thy father, thy mother, all thy hateful kind doth busily oppose me. Thou shalt pay me the penalty this day."*

Such at this day are many in the Courts of Princes, endued with great authority, especially the covetous and rapacious, or the sponges of Vespasian.

**Of the second.** Rightly said Thearidas, as he was whetting a knife, when he was asked whether it were sharp: *"Sharp indeed, but calumny is sharper."* And in the Proverbs it is said: *"The words of a talebearer are as wounds, and they go down into the innermost parts of the belly."*

**Sirach 5**: *"A thief is a pernicious thing, but a Calumniator is much worse."* And **28**: *"They trouble them that are at peace, they destroy cities and Principalities."*

How great an evil calumny stirreth up in the Church, this age showeth enough and more than enough. And the matter is come to this, that scarce any thing can any longer be so well said, written and declared, but that it must on many accounts lie open to the calumnious censure even of the worst of men, the judgement of the godly and the sound being despised. This wantonness of a perverse age, ***καὶ κριτικὴ ἀκρισία*** (and censorious want of judgement), doth at this day afflict and waste away the minds of good men beyond measure, according to that saying: *"Calumny troubleth the wise man, and destroyeth the strength of his heart."* In the Commonwealth that is wont to come to pass which Solomon saith, **Prov. 16**: *"A talebearer oft-times stirreth up dissension among Princes."* Calumny dissolveth friendship, and is the poison of concord, and stealeth from a neighbour his name and fame. And of a truth the Theological brawls, as they are called, full of calumnies, are at this day in many places the cause of division, and of grudges and distrust, among the Princes themselves. Let those therefore who are the fans thereof[^P14-4] look to it, that they may one day be able to answer to God and to men.

**Of the third.** At the outset let every man take heed that he himself be not a Calumniator <!-- p.292 --> and talebearer, and let him be moved by these causes: **I.** He who gladly calumniateth is the instrument of the Devil, who hath his name from calumnies. **II.** Because the fraud is at length known, as **Demosthenes** wisely saith: ***Διαβολὴ καιρῷ μὲν ἰσχυρὰν ἐργάζεται τοῖς ἀκούουσι τὴν ὑπόληψιν, χρόνῳ δέ, πάντων ἀσθενεστέρα γίνεται*** (Calumny for a season worketh a strong persuasion in them that hear, but in time it becometh the weakest of all things). **III.** Because punishments overwhelm Calumniators, **Ps. 27 [28]**: *"The Lord shall destroy them."* So Korah, Dathan, Abiram, Doeg, Absalom, Ziba, and the two Judges of Susanna are punished. **IIII.** The commandment of God, **Sirach 5**, **Prov. 4**, etc. Next, let us not be overmuch credulous. For credulity[^P14-5] is a certain foolish lightness that judgeth nothing, of which **Sirach**, chap. **19**, saith: *"He that is hasty to give credit is lightminded."*[^P14-6] And **Epicharmus**: ***Νῆφε, καὶ μέμνησο ἀπιστεῖν, ταῦτα ἄρθρα φρενῶν ἐστὶ***, which **Cicero** thus turneth: *"The joints and sinews of wisdom are, not rashly to believe."* So **Menander**:

> ***Ὅστις διαβολαῖσι πείθεται ταχὺ,***  
> ***Ἤτοι πονηρὸς αὐτὸς ἐστὶ τοὺς τρόπους,***  
> ***Ἢ παντάπασι παιδαρίου γνώμην ἔχει.***
>
> (Whoso is quickly persuaded by calumnies  
> Is either himself wicked in his ways,  
> Or hath altogether the mind of a little child.)

To a fowler, who had let go out of his hands a little bird that he had taken, upon her promise that she would show him a most precious Pearl, the little bird, sitting in a tree, sang this verse (which the Emperor Maximilian was wont often to repeat):

> Believe little, keep thine own, and let go the things that are lost.

In the Turpillian Decree of the Senate, in the 48th book of the Pandects, title 16, a penalty is laid upon Calumniators by the Remmian law. The Law: *"Let the Calumniator suffer that which the accused would, had he been convicted."* So **Isocrates** to Nicocles: *"Visit with the like penalty both them that calumniate and them that commit the crime."* And of old the Heathen banished unjust informers to the Isle of Gyaros.

But as for him who hath been informed against and accused by calumny — although that is true which is said, *"Calumniate boldly, something always sticketh"* — yet there is nothing else for him to do, but that by honesty of life he blot out the suspicion, and keep a good <!-- p.293 --> name, by this Rule: *"War a good warfare; holding faith, and a good conscience."*

Likewise:

> A mind conscious of right laugheth at the lies of rumour.

Likewise:

> — The sweet nurse of life.

And thus far verily we have spoken of Calumny. It remaineth that we add somewhat concerning the Master's Eye.

## *Oculus Domini* · The Master's Eye

That the flatteries of the Phormios,[^P14-7] and Calumnies, which are as it were the sister of flatteries, may be avoided by masters, it is necessary that the eye or presence of the master be at hand in the ordering of most matters. And hither pertain the customary sentences: *"The forehead is before the hinder part of the head."* **Aristotle**, ***α. οἰκονομ.*** (*Economics*, book 1): A Persian, being asked what thing most of all fattened a horse, answered: *"The Master's eye."* The Egyptians so represented the image of a Prince, that they painted a sceptre with an eye. *"What the Sun is in the heaven, that the Prince is among the people. The Sun is the eye of the world, the Prince is the eye of the multitude. What the mind is in a man, that the Prince is in the city. The mind is wise, the body obeyeth."* Likewise: *"It is the greatest praise of a Prince to have eyes before and behind."* ***Εἷς ἐστι δοῦλος οἰκίας, ὁ δεσπότης*** (One there is that is the slave of the house: the master), saith **Menander**. And if this be true in the household, much more in the commonwealth. For when Princes either are not themselves watchful and diligent, or have not Counsellors whom they may safely trust, and who are imitators of Joseph and Naaman, it is verily all over with the safety of kingdoms. Now since at this day there are in Courts more Ahithophels and Zibas than Josephs and Naamans, therefore there is need of the Master's eye, which availeth more than ten others, as the Proverb also showeth: *"One eye-witness is worth more than ten ear-witnesses."* And *"those things never go forward prosperously enough which are done by other men's eyes,"* saith **Livy**.

To the rendering of this diligence Princes ought to have been moved <!-- p.294 --> by the nature of the office which they administer, of which it is said in the Psalm: *"I have said, Ye are gods."* Now God, being present, seeth and heareth all things. Him godly Princes ought to imitate, if they will rightly preside over their office.

And let there be in view the commandment of God, who enjoineth that Princes and Magistrates be diligent in their office. Next, the dignity of the office, which is diminished and disfigured if they themselves are not present and sedulous, as though the office were a slight and light thing. After that, the peril, in many ways, which may briefly be thus reckoned up: The prayer of the subjects unto God is turned away from the Prince, nay, is poured out unto God against the Prince, when they are oppressed, he himself not even knowing of it; as it cometh to pass at this day, when the minds of the subjects, who ought to pray for the safety of the Magistrate, have long since been estranged, and cry aloud against injustice. And such outcries are not vain, as examples show. For *"the voice of the people is the voice of God,"* as antiquity hath said.

Princes themselves, being overmuch credulous, are most easily deceived, and are drained dry by their Phormios, who, if the Princes were more heedful, would leave undone many things which, the Princes knowing nothing of them, these fellows boldly contrive and do. And hither let the history be referred concerning Pleminius, to whom Scipio Africanus, when he had taken the city of Locri, had committed the government of the city, seeing that his life seemed honest and grave, whereas his heart was full of covetousness and wickedness. Straightway therefore, as soon as Scipio, being occupied with other wars, was departed, Pleminius vomited forth the poison of his mind, snatching out of the temples and from elsewhere moneys and other things, such as seemed good to him, and pressing the subjects now with these burdens, now with those, under pretext of the command of Scipio. The Locrians, being constrained, set out for Rome, and lay their calamities before the Senate, which, commanding Pleminius to be brought a prisoner and to plead his cause in bonds, ordereth that, if those things should be true whereof the Locrians complained, he be put to death in the prison, and his goods confiscated, and all that had been taken away restored. And so also was the matter done and finished with Pleminius. And too well known is the covetousness of many when the Master's eye is absent — which, even though it be present, can yet scarce be hindered, nay, can <!-- p.295 --> hardly be assuaged. And the Law of the Romans which is named the Julian law *of extortion* (*repetundarum*) served to this end, that covetousness might be restrained. For it pertained to those things which any man had received in a Magistracy, an embassy, or other office or ministry. And by this Law all things were demanded back that had been taken away, whether by fraud or by force. And hither maketh the notable deed of Epaminondas, who was wont so to fine his covetous men, that he would send unto them some poor and good man, to whom they were to count out a certain sum of money; to them he rendered nothing again. When they complained of this burden laid upon them, Epaminondas answered: *"He, though he is an upright man, is pressed with want; but ye are rich, having plundered much from the public store."* So also Vespasian the father was wont to promote every most rapacious man, that he might presently condemn them when they were enriched: hence he was commonly said to hold his officers for sponges, because, like sponges, he both moistened them when dry, and squeezed them out when wet. This also is true, that when the Master's eye is absent many faithful and good men are by calumnies estranged from their masters, who lightly believe detractions, so that the masters are not able to discern the faithful and good from the flatterers and calumniators. And thus many who have served faithfully are oft-times on a sudden neglected, despised, driven out, and other perverse men are put in their room, who snatch away the things which those had faithfully deserved. Of this misery the son of Sirach writeth, **27**: *"There be two things that grieve me, and the third maketh me angry: namely, when a valiant man is at the last compelled to suffer poverty; and wise Counsellors are in the end despised; and he that falleth away from the true faith."*

There are also other perils without number, both in peace and in wars, when the Master's eye is absent; who assuredly cannot have a good conscience, if he be negligent, and if he give not heed to these things: whether true religion flourisheth among his people with unanimous consent, whether the public highways be secure and safe, whether public crimes be punished, whether justice be rendered to the subjects out of the councils and the Chanceries, whether the Officers, Treasurers and others oppress not the poor, and whether they be content with their wages, etc.

<!-- p.296 -->

But many object: It becometh not a Prince to be busied in public functions. Likewise: It is enough to have Counsellors; and if they administer not matters rightly, upon them, and not upon the Prince, is the bean deservedly threshed.[^P14-8]

But these are most light, nay, even ungodly thoughts, excuses and objections. *"That is the voice of an ox, not of a man,"* had a most wise King of Spain said. Rather is it a most goodly thing for Kings and Princes to be busied in public functions. Next, if he use other men's eyes only, of a certainty the Prince himself is but a painted and a paper Prince, not a living, not a true, not a lawful one; as a certain man at play had said to the Emperor Maximilian, when the rest of the players had boasted that they had two paper Kings: *"I,"* saith he, *"have three Kings"* — showing two of paper, and touching Maximilian with his hand — *"for this one,"* saith he, *"ye account for a paper King."*

## *Quid igitur faciendum est Magistratui et Iudicibus, ut veritatem tueantur et obtineant* · What Then Is to Be Done by the Magistrate and the Judges, That They May Defend and Maintain the Truth

**I.** Let them fear God, as Jehoshaphat admonisheth his Judges, **2 Paral. [2 Chron.] 19**: *"Ye judge not for man, but for the Lord, who is with you in the judgement."* Such was Solomon, who straightway perceived the guile of the one woman; as also the youth Daniel straightway perceived the lies of the Judges concerning Susanna. And the Heathen admonish the same, as **Cicero**: *"When a Judge hath sentence to pronounce, let him remember that he hath God for a witness, that is, his own mind, than which God Himself hath given unto man nothing more divine."*

**II.** Let them be learned, and lovers of honest letters, as the sentences everywhere enjoin: *"Never suffer unskilled men to judge."* Likewise: *"An unlearned Prince is a statue rather than a Prince."* Likewise **Tacitus**: *"Who ruleth better than the lettered man?"* For we see how unhappily, imprudently and turbulently the unlearned reign, especially those who are by nature puffed up <!-- p.297 --> and arrogant, and yet not circumspect, but who are led by others after the manner of Bulls and cattle, and are miserably deceived, and are cast headlong into extremities ***ἑκούσια καὶ αὐθαίρετα σφῇσιν ἀτασθαλίῃσι*** (wilful and self-chosen, by their own reckless follies), as examples show.

**III.** Let them be wise, and furnished with practice and experience of affairs, whence also they are named Senators and Elders, and Consuls, and Judges. **Plato**: *"Wisdom is no otherwise necessary to a Prince than the soul to the body."* The same: *"Blessed are Commonwealths, if they who study wisdom govern them."* **Prov. 6**: *"If ye will reign, honour wisdom."* And in the eighth: *"By wisdom Kings reign, and Counsellors decree justice."* And in the tenth: *"Where there is a prudent Magistrate, there things are done in order."* This wisdom is seen in the examples of Solomon, and of Daniel, and of a certain Spanish Judge, who, when he could not discover the author of a murder, and yet many were in suspicion, of whose number it was certain that some one was guilty, the Judge commanded the breast of each to be made bare, that he might try the beating of the heart, and so discovered the guilty man, whose heart had trembled most. And the history is related both of Gonzaga, governor of Milan, and of Charles of Burgundy, whose daughter the Emperor Maximilian had to wife. A certain nobleman took his enemy captive. The wife of the captive, as a suppliant, made intercession with the nobleman for her husband. The nobleman promised the deliverance of her husband, if she would come together and lie with him. The woman, loving honesty, and yet desiring to deliver her husband out of bonds, goeth to her husband, who straightway permitteth her, nay, entreateth her, to comply with the nobleman, that so he himself might both be set free and remain among the living. The next day, when the nobleman had now fulfilled his desire, he commandeth the captive to be beheaded, and so delivereth him, beheaded, to his wife. The woman bringeth the matter before Gonzaga, who, summoning the nobleman, commandeth him to take the woman to wife; the nuptials being celebrated, he causeth the nobleman to be beheaded, and maketh his wife heir of all the goods which the nobleman had left.

**IIII.** Let them judge and pronounce according to the word of God, and according to the rights or Laws, **Josh. 1**: *"According to the Law <!-- p.298 --> of Moses,"* saith God to Joshua. And **Ambrose**: *"A good Judge doeth nothing of his own arbitrament, or from the purpose of his private will, but pronounceth according to the Laws and rights."*

**V.** Let them hear both parties, and reserve the other ear for the party not yet heard.

**VI.** Let them not lightly or straightway believe, nor hurry sentence and judgement. For *"he hasteneth to repentance, who judgeth quickly."* And: *"One ought to have an ear slow to admit accusations."* Likewise:

> The Judge shall determine nothing, though the plaintiff plead,  
> Unless the cause of the accused defendant be known.

So God Himself judgeth no sooner than He taketh knowledge of the matter, although He beholdeth all things, as the examples show, in Adam, in Cain, in Sodom and Gomorrah, when He saith: *"I will go down, and see whether the matter be so, as it hath been reported unto me."* Likewise, in the building of the tower of Babel. And it is well known that every headlong judgement is evil, as Judah condemning Tamar, Potiphar condemning Joseph, David condemning Mephibosheth. And there are numberless other examples, which experience setteth forth.

**VII.** Let them not have respect of persons, a thing which is often forbidden, **Exod. 23**, **Deut. 1**, **Prov. 18**. God is without ***προσωποληψίᾳ*** (respect of persons), both in condemning and in showing mercy. Such let judges also be. Saul, having regard to the person of King Agag, is punished, being cast out of the kingdom, etc.

**VIII.** Let them not be ***δωροφάγοι*** (devourers of gifts), **Exod. 23**: *"Thou shalt not take gifts, which blind the eyes, and pervert the causes of the righteous."* **Sirach 8**, **Prov. 17**, **Isa. 1**.

**IX.** Let them not judge according to favour, a thing which nevertheless is often done, whence the Proverb is: *"A favourable Judge."* Darius casteth Daniel into the den of Lions, that he may gratify his Princes. Herod taketh Peter, that he may gratify the Jews, whom he saw it pleased that he had killed James. And rightly saith **Aristotle**: *"Love, hatred and private advantage oft-times cause a Judge not to know the truth."* And **Phocylides**: *"Award all things justly, and wrest not judgement unto favour. <!-- p.299 --> If thou judge evilly, God shall afterward judge thee."* Likewise **Cicero**: *"Men judge more things by love, or by desire, or by anger, or by grief, or by hope, or by gladness, or by fear, or by terror, than by truth, or by any rule of right, or by a form of judgement, or by the Laws."*

Now let thus much suffice to have been said concerning the truth which ought to be in commonwealths. We must now come to households, and to the conversation and private life of each man. And hither pertaineth Friendship, of which it was spoken above in the fourth Commandment. And it is profitable that mention be made of it here, that it may be shown that it pertaineth to the eighth Commandment for this cause, that the Rule of true friendship ought to be truth, without which friendship is the poison of life and of welfare. *"Socrates is a friend, Plato is a friend, but truth is more a friend."* And this is what **Pericles** said, that friends are to be gratified, but as far as the altars. And here notable virtues meet together:

- Truth (*Veritas*).
- Faithfulness (*Fidelitas*).
- Simplicity and Teachableness (*Simplicitas et Docilitas*).
- Candour (*Candor*).
- Constancy (*Constantia*).
- Taciturnity (*Taciturnitas*).
- Courtesy and Urbanity (*Comitas et Urbanitas*).

All these pertain to true friendship according to this eighth Commandment, nay, to the whole of life and ***ὁμιλίαν*** (intercourse) with other men. Now of truth enough hath been said hitherto. **Faithfulness** and **constancy** (*Fidelitas et constantia*) are to be joined together, namely, when as well in adversity as in prosperity constancy is shown in true mutual goodwill, without faithlessness, of which **Aristotle** said: ***ὦ φίλοι, οὐδεὶς φίλος*** (O friends, there is no friend). Likewise:

> So long as thou art fortunate, thou shalt number many friends;  
> When the times are clouded, thou shalt be alone.

And King Antigonus asked of God that he might be defended against his friends. Next, constancy is also this, when we say and do things true, just and necessary, <!-- p.300 --> continually after the same manner, without the lightness of Ecebolius[^P14-9] and of Courtiers, and without stubbornness, both Servetian and Anabaptistical.

**Simplicity** (*Simplicitas*) is to say, teach, take and do things true and right, properly and plainly. The Hebrews call it *Tharain*,[^P14-10] integrity; **Paul** calleth it simplicity — not of the understanding, but of a will upright, open, not painted over, not guileful, not seeking anything of its own, but candid and sincere. Of it **Euripides** saith: ***ἁπλοῦς ὁ μῦθος τῆς ἀληθείας ἔφυ*** (Simple is the speech of truth). Likewise: ***ἐσθλὸν σαφὲς ἀεί*** (That which is good is always plain). Hereby is taken away ***διπλότης*** (doubleness), and the feigning of simplicity, obscure speech, ***τὸ ἀκατάληπτον*** (that which cannot be comprehended). Likewise: ***φιλαυτία*** and ***αὐθάδεια*** (self-love and self-will). Likewise: the Academic ***ἐποχή*** (suspension of judgement). Likewise that ***τὸ σκότισον*** (*"Darken it!"*), of which **Quintilian** speaketh. For most true is that which **D. Philip** was often wont to say: *"A monstrous and obscure speech is a most certain token of a monstrous and entangled wit."* **Seneca**, book 10, declamation 1, writeth of a certain man after this manner: *"Oscus spake not amiss, but he did himself harm. For he desired to say nothing without a figure. His speech was not figured, but crooked. And so, not without wit, Pacatus[^P14-11] the Rhetor, when he had met him at Marseilles in the morning, saluted him with a figure: 'I might indeed have said: Hail, Oscus.' He himself was far from eloquence, being born to brand the wits of all men with reproaches. There was none upon whom he did not imprint something which he could not escape,"* etc. Let there be observed here also the sentence of **Pindar**, that a good and just cause hath no need of many words:

> ***Εὐώνυμον ἐς δίκαν***  
> ***τρία ἔπεα διαρκέσει.***
>
> (For a cause of fair name  
> Three words shall suffice.)

**Candour** (*Candor*) is understood in these things: **I.** Openly to show the meaning of the mind without dissembling, as that man in **Ennius** saith: *"Of such a disposition was I born: friendship and enmity I bear ready upon my forehead."*

And **Homer**, ***ἰλιάδ.*** (*Iliad*):

> ***Ἐχθρὸς γάρ μοι κεῖνος ὁμῶς ἀΐδαο πύλῃσιν,***  
> ***ὅς χ' ἕτερον μὲν κεύθει ἐνὶ φρεσὶν, ἄλλο δὲ εἴπῃ.***
>
> For I hate as the gates of Erebus all who speak  
> <!-- p.301 -->One thing with the mouth, and hide another beneath the silent breast.
