Next, let this move us unto chastity, that the grace of God, the Holy Spirit, and the inheritance of eternal life are lost by them that defile themselves. *"The whoremonger and the unclean person shall have no inheritance in the kingdom of God."* **Heb. 13**: *"Follow chastity, without which no man shall see God."* **Matt. 5**: *"Blessed are the pure in heart: for they shall see God."* **Eph. 4**: *"They are alienated from God, because of the blindness of their heart, who have given themselves over unto lasciviousness."*

Fourthly, because chastity is the ornament of all the virtues; and if it be not present, the other virtues also are disfigured, as is said in the tetrastich of **D. Philip**:

> As when from a statue the comely face is torn away,  
> The trunk hath no grace in the rest of the body:  
> <!-- p.222 -->So the rest of a man's manners shall lie despised, without honour,  
> Unless they be adorned with the praise of chastity.  

Fifthly, that we may avoid punishments in this life and in the life to come. **Heb. 13**: *"Whoremongers and adulterers God will punish,"* to wit, either mediately through the Magistrate in this life, or immediately in this life and in the life to come.

Sixthly, that we disfigure not the doctrine with scandals. As David by his lust gave occasion to the neighbouring Nations to speak evil of the doctrine concerning God, so at this day the Anabaptists and other filthy men, especially those in whose hands is some princely dignity, give occasion to other Nations, to France and Spain, to speak evil of the Christian doctrine, which they call Lutheran. And **Luther** writeth that there was at Wittenberg a Doctor, an Envoy of the French King, who affirmed that his King was of this opinion, that among the Lutherans there is no Magistrate, no marriage, and that divers minglings are practised without distinction. These scandals are great, both given and taken.

Let these things therefore move us unto chastity in thoughts, in speech and gestures, in apparel, and in the whole life, that we may be modest and studious of chastity, to the glory of God and to our own profit, Amen. ***ὅσια πράττειν καὶ ὅσια λέγειν δεῖ*** (one must do holy things and speak holy things). Thus far of the sixth Commandment.

# *Septimum Praeceptum* · The Seventh Commandment

### *Non furtum facies* · Thou Shalt Not Steal

This Commandment enjoineth labours and forbiddeth idleness. It willeth that every man labour, and that unless he labour he eat not; that he be content with his own lot and with the blessing of God, lest he must needs live with the loss of another, as the idle, usurers and others live. And the sum of this Commandment is contained <!-- p.223 --> in these words of the Jurists: *"The precepts of the law are these: To live honestly, to hurt no other, and to render to every man his own right."* Likewise the definition of justice which is extant in **Clement** belongeth hither: ***Δικαιοσύνη ἐστὶ κοινωνία θεοῦ μετ' ἰσότητος***, Justice is the communication of God with equality; that is, justice is such an equality as hath been ordained by the Law of God, that to every man his own right be left, that adultery be not committed, nor theft, etc. And the phrase ***κοινωνία θεοῦ*** seemeth to comprehend the first table of the Decalogue, in which God communicateth Himself to us, that is, whereby He shineth in us, is rightly acknowledged, and kindleth in us the true fear, love and trust of God. But the phrase ***μετ' ἰσότητος*** comprehendeth the second table, in which justice is equality, namely the love of our neighbour ordained by the Law of God. But these things by the way.

Now that we may say somewhat of this commandment suited to the capacity of the hearers, we will rehearse the chief virtues or good works which are contained in this Commandment. Let the first virtue, then, be the just and honest possession, and the good use, of a man's own goods and substance. And here at the outset let the Anabaptists be refuted, who deny that it is lawful for a Christian man to hold property, and seem to prove it by the examples of the Apostles:

> The Apostles and their hearers in Judaea brought their substance together into a common stock.  
> Therefore it is not lawful for Christians to have property.

Now it is well known that ***κακοζηλίας*** (perverse imitations) of examples are to be avoided. And the jest of **Luther** is most true:

| | | |
|:--|:--|:--|
| The Word of God | is imitated by | heretics. |
| The works of faith | are imitated by | hypocrites. |
| Ceremonies | are imitated by | idolaters. |
| War | is imitated by | the rash. |
| Kingship | is imitated by | fools. |
| Handicrafts | are imitated by | the dull, *die hümpler* (the bunglers). |
| The arts | are imitated by | asses. |

The Apostles and their hearers had a probable cause of their counsel. For their profession's sake they were being thrust out <!-- p.224 --> of their lands; therefore they chose rather to sell them beforehand, that being driven from their country they might have provision for the way. And this money was laid up with the college of the Apostles. Neither did the Apostles command this contribution; but the matter was free, and without superstition, and not convenient in all places. The Rule also is well known: *"Judgement is to be given not by examples, but by Laws."*

But whereas **D. Paul** saith, **1 Cor. 7**: *"Let them that use this world be as though they used it not,"* he by no means commandeth Christians to cast away their substance, but teacheth that the mind of a Christian man ought not to be taken up with earthly things, nor to rest in them. For so must we live, as though at every moment we were to depart out of this life; and therefore in all the affairs of this present life our hearts ought to be lifted up to the meditation of the heavenly life, according to the saying: *"Seek ye first the Kingdom of God, etc."* *"Take no thought."* And let these little verses be known:

> ***Ὡς τεθνηξόμενος τῶν σῶν κτεάνων ἀπόλαυε,***  
> ***Ὥστε βιωσόμενος φείδεο σῶν κτεάνων.***  
> ***Ἀλλὰ δ' ἀνὴρ σοφὸς ἐστὶν, ὃς ἄμφω ταῦτα νοήσας***  
> ***Φειδοῖ, καὶ δαπάνῃ μέτρον ἐφηρμόσατο.***  

> (As one that shall die, enjoy thy possessions,  
> And as one that shall live, be sparing of thy possessions.  
> But that man is wise who, having considered both these things,  
> Hath fitted a measure to sparing and to spending.)  

And let this be the general Rule concerning the use of the things of this present life.

Now that the possession of goods and the distinction of ownerships (*distinctio dominiorum*) hath been sanctioned and ordained of God, these do show:

The Divine Law.

The Law of nature.

The Testimonies of Scripture, or the word of God.

The Examples of the Saints.

The Divine Law forbiddeth thefts. Therefore the property of things is already ordained. Otherwise there were no need of this prohibition.

Next, the Law of nature seeth that there is need of the distinction of ownerships in this corruption of nature, seeing that men have not equal grounds of seeking and keeping goods, and that some are slothful, others prodigal, others greedy of power. Hither belongeth that saying: ***μετ' ἰσότητος*** (with equality). Likewise: ***Τὸ ἴσον πόλεμον οὐ ποιεῖ*** (that which is equal maketh no war).

<!-- p.225 -->

Afterward, the testimonies of Scripture are many, as: *"The blessing of the Lord, it maketh rich."* *"The rich and poor meet together: the Lord is the maker of them both."* *"Remain thou lord of thine own fountains."* *"Charge them that are rich, etc."* And the word of God approveth buyings and sellings, **1 Cor. 7**, **1 Thess. 4**. Lastly the Saints, as Abraham, Jacob, Jeremiah, buy fields; Abraham is rich, etc.

These things being premised, let these three questions now be considered:

**I.** What ways of getting riches are lawful.

**II.** How GOD willeth us to be affected toward riches.

**III.**[^P11-1] What use of riches is right and lawful.

These questions being known, it will be plain what ought to be held concerning the seventh Commandment, and concerning the virtues that belong to it.

### *De Quaestione Prima* · Of the First Question

**I.** As to what and how much concerneth the lawful ways of getting riches, the first care of all ought to be for the salvation of the soul, according to the Rule of Christ, **Matt. 7**: *"Seek ye first the Kingdom of God, and his righteousness."*

**II.** Next, because this life hath need of supports, we must labour, and substance is to be sought by labours, according to the ordinance of God, who promiseth a blessing to them that labour godly.

**III.** Afterward the fruit of our labour — which fruit cometh to us as we labour, out of the blessing of God — is to be taken as from the hand of God, without evil arts, without fraud and deceits, both in contracts and in the whole life. This elsewhere they call Commutative justice, by Arithmetical equality.

And this third Rule is to be diligently observed for these causes: **I.** That we may acknowledge that all good things are given of God. **Prov. 10**: *"The blessing of the Lord, it maketh rich."* **Hos. 2**: *"And she did not know <!-- p.226 --> this, that I gave her corn; therefore will I take away my corn again."* And in the former epistle to **Timothy, 6**: *"The living God giveth us richly all things to enjoy."* **II.** That our faith and invocation may be exercised in the asking and expecting of our household substance. **III.** That our Thankfulness toward God for all His benefits may daily be declared. *"O give thanks unto the Lord; for he is good: for his mercy endureth for ever."* *"Who giveth food, etc."* **IIII.** That the care of bodily things may admonish us to bestow a greater care upon spiritual things. **V.** That we may know that God willeth in this life to preserve the Church, and that the doctrine delivered by Himself be learned.

Hereby, therefore, are taken away: **[I.]** Unlawful care and carefulness. **II.** All kinds of thefts in the whole of life, private and public.

As touching carefulness (*solicitudo*), it is to be held that care is threefold: one profane or Heathen, another necessary and holy, a third mixed of these.

Profane or ungodly carefulness is that which ariseth from distrust and ignorance of the providence of God. And this is condemned by Christ, **Matt. 6**, with many arguments, from the greater to the less, from the less to the greater, and others. And it is sometimes called the care of the flesh, for two causes: because it proceedeth from the mind of the flesh without faith, and because it hath a carnal mark, namely the quiet and tranquillity of this present life.

Necessary carefulness is that which is diligence and labour in doing a lawful vocation. As Joshua, though he is compassed about on every side with enemies, yet keepeth constancy of mind and hopeth for good issues, because he knoweth that, being called, he is helped and defended of God. So Kings for their kingdoms, Parents for their households, Ministers for their hearers, Schoolmasters for their scholars, and every man in his own vocation, ought to have carefulness.

**1 Tim. 5**: *"If any provide not for his own, and specially for those of his own house, he hath denied the faith, and is worse than an infidel."* And Paul is careful for his Church, **Col. 2**, **1 Thess. 2**, **2 Cor. 11**. Now unto this necessary carefulness these five things are required:

<!-- p.227 -->

The Commandment of God.

Faith.

Invocation of God, and Thanksgiving.

The End, the glory of God.

Patience and Humility.

Without the commandment of God a godly mind ought to take nothing in hand. And without faith nothing can please God. And without the divine help nothing prospereth. And for success all ought to be thankful to God. And let the principal end of labours be the glory of God, according to the saying: ***ἀρχὴν ἁπάντων καὶ τέλος ποίει θεόν*** (make God the beginning and the end of all things). Likewise: Let God be α and ω, both as touching eternity, and as touching the spreading of His glory in our labours. Last of all, if our labour answer but little to our wishes, let us submit ourselves to the fatherly will of God in true fear and humility. **1 Pet. 5**: *"Cast all your care upon him; for he careth for you."* Likewise: *"Commit thy way unto God, and he shall bring it to pass."* Likewise: *"Cast thy care upon the Lord, and he shall sustain thee."*

Now this godly carefulness hath on either side opposite vices: Sloth and ***πολυπραγμοσύνην*** (busy meddling). Sardanapalus loseth his Kingdom through sloth. And of lazy servants **Cato** said: *"A slothful servant doth more hurt than a slothful thief."* Likewise: *"Men, by doing nothing, learn to do ill."* And it is rightly said: *"Idleness is Satan's pillow."* **Capitolinus** writeth that the Emperor Antoninus Pius[^P11-2] withdrew their salaries from those whom he had seen receiving them in idleness, saying that nothing is more cruel than that they should gnaw upon the Commonwealth who bring nothing into it by their own labour. And of the Emperor Honorius this sloth is reported, that he would set his name to letters which he had not read. His sister Placidia, desiring to amend this sloth of his, causeth letters to be drawn up by which she herself was betrothed to a most base slave, and causeth them to be subscribed by her brother's hand. Afterward she cometh to the Emperor her brother, casteth herself at his feet, and complaineth of her own unhappiness and of her brother's cruelty toward her. When the Emperor affirmed that her complaints were false, she brought forth the letters subscribed by the Emperor's hand, <!-- p.228 --> whereby he was admonished of his own negligence. There are many other examples in life, both of sloth and of ***πολυπραγμοσύνης*** (busy meddling). And either vice God detesteth, who willeth us to labour diligently and to do our own business. And let the Rule be held, **Ps. 36 [37]**: *"Be subject unto God, and pray unto him."* And Christ preacheth concerning labour: *"Sufficient unto the day is the evil thereof,"* that is, the labour of a man's own vocation. For in this short saying He forbiddeth Heathen carefulness, idleness, and ***πολυπραγμοσύνην***, of which it is elsewhere said: *"A great part of men perisheth by doing ill, a greater by doing nothing, the greatest by doing other men's business."*[^P11-3] Now from idleness let these causes dissuade us:

**I.** The Commandment of God, who enjoineth labour.

**II.** Our own reason, which persuadeth that we must labour.

**III.** Our vocation, which cannot be satisfied by us even with many labours.

**IIII.** That we may avoid and restrain many sins, of which idleness is the pillow and the tinder.

**V.** That we may have the supports of life, food, and the other things necessary to life, which idleness taketh away.

**VI.** That we may enjoy the blessing of God, which is far removed from the slothful and idle.

**VII.** That we may be a good example to others.

**VIII.** That we may have an honest place in the Commonwealth, and a good name, lest we be shut out and driven away, as the drones, a slothful herd, from the cribs and the hives.

**IX.** The examples of nature in the ants, in the bees, etc. For nature is not idle, and doth nothing in vain, as may be seen in metals, in herbs, and in all the works, or rather miracles, of nature.

**X.** That we may have a good conscience toward God and our neighbour, which cannot be in the idle.

**XI.** That we may provide for the health of our body, which without the exercise of labours and of motion doth, as it were, sink down and rot.

For the rest, mixed carefulness is that which in part appeareth godly, <!-- p.229 --> and in part is convicted of being profane: the former by reason of the office itself, which we rightly administer; the latter by reason of the doubt, inborn in us, concerning the providence and blessing of God; as when the father of a household doth indeed rightly and godly bring up his children, and yet is driven to impatience when he seeth that the labour and care answer not his wishes. Let the godly man therefore cast this care upon the Lord, and go on strenuously in his office, and not suffer a godly care to be defiled with Heathen distrust; let him rather go the more boldly against it, and wait for the blessing of God, whom he serveth. Let peace stand fast within. Toward men let him be meek and modest. Let him call upon God, and commit the success to Him, and so let him serve and labour. And let these things be said of carefulness. Now we must speak also of certain kinds of thefts.

**I.** Theft is when another man's thing is handled, or snatched away, against the owner's will, without just cause, whether by force, or by fraud, or under any fair-seeming pretext whatsoever. Now there are very many kinds of thefts, and in **2 Sam. 15** one species of theft is pointed out, where the seditious Absalom is said to have stolen the heart of the men of Israel; that is, his father not knowing it, he drew to himself, by what crafts he could, by a feigning of good will and by other means, the subjects of his father. Hence it is plain that the greatest thieves are they who estrange the minds of subjects from the Magistrate, and are seditious. For of sedition **Herodotus** rightly saith: *"By how much war is worse than peace, by so much is sedition worse than war."*[^P11-4] Likewise: they who estrange the minds of hearers from the ministers of the word, of scholars from their Schoolmasters, of servants from their masters, of children from their parents, of husbands from their spouses or of wives from their husbands. This kind of theft thou mayest rightly call devilish. For as the Devil turned away the minds of our first parents from God, so these, treading in their father's footsteps, estrange minds from those with whom they ought to be joined.

**II.** This theft, which is most of all devilish, is followed by Sacrilege, which is threefold: for either the wicked man stealeth something from God, or he stealeth the revenues of the Church, or otherwise he stealeth sacred things out of sacred places.

<!-- p.230 -->

To steal something from God is to take away from God His honour and glory, and to transfer it to creatures, as do the Heathen and idolaters, who feign something to be God which is not God, and ascribe divine worships to that thing, and bind God to things which He hath not set forth in His word. Likewise: they who choose worships without the commandment of God. Of these it hath been spoken above, in the first table. And in the same manner they steal His glory from the Son of God who deny that we are saved by His merit alone, and not saved by our own works, which is what we say: That we are justified by faith alone. For they ascribe to works that which is proper to the Son of God alone. Likewise: they who take away the one part or kind from the Supper, against the words of institution. Likewise, they who teach that Christ is not in the Supper by a bodily presence. For these endeavour to steal the body and blood of Christ out of the Supper, and to take away from the human nature in Christ the divine glory and majesty given unto it really and truly, without confusion of the natures. Likewise: they who bid us doubt whether we be in the grace of God, and have Remission of sins for Christ's sake. Likewise: they who say that Christ hath made satisfaction only for original sin, but that we ought to make satisfaction for actual sins by our own works and merits. Likewise: they who ascribe to free will the power of performing the commandments of God, and of obtaining grace and the Holy Spirit, etc. Of these things it is spoken more fully elsewhere. For hither belong almost all heresies and corruptions of doctrine. But to steal the revenues of the Church is when the contributions and revenues are transferred to other uses than to the preservation of the ministry and of studies. For the Church hath need either of contributions or of revenues for three causes: First, for the nourishing of the ministers of the Gospel, as Paul commandeth that ministers be nourished by the Church. Next, for the preserving of Schools, where charges are to be supplied to the Doctors and to poor Scholars. Thirdly, that other poor folk may be helped out of the public stock. And because the people contribute sparingly, it is the office of the Magistrate to appoint revenues and pensions for the Churches, even as Isaiah for this cause calleth Kings the nursing fathers and trusty ones of the Church, and Queens her nursing mothers. And these were in the beginning the causes <!-- p.231 --> of Ecclesiastical donations, as appeareth from **Augustine** and from the histories. And so God, **Exod. 30**, appointed half a shekel to be paid by all toward the sacrifice. And elsewhere He commandeth that every man in his own place help the ministry, and most severely threateneth those who help not the ministers of the temple, **Haggai 1**: *"Build my house, and ye shall please me."* Likewise: *"Mine house is waste, and every man hasteth to build up his own house; therefore have I forbidden the heaven to give dew, and the earth to be fruitful."* And **Mal. 3**: *"Bring ye all the tithes into my storehouse, and I will open you the windows of heaven, and pour out a blessing."* **Prov. 3**: *"Honour God with thy substance, and with the firstfruits of all thine increase: so shall thy barns be filled with plenty, and thy presses shall overflow with wine."* In these works God willeth faith to be exercised; and if it were present, we should be more liberal in this kind of duties, nor would that come to pass which **Jerome** saith: *"The panelled ceilings glitter with gold, the temples shine with marble: but of the ministers of Christ there is scarce any regard."*

### *Unde autem oritur tanta tenacitas ad iuvandum ministerium* · Whence Then Ariseth So Great Niggardliness in Helping the Ministry

First, it ariseth from contempt of the doctrine and of the ministers; next, from distrust toward God, from whom very few look for the preservation of their substance.

The doctrine is neglected in this security of men. Therefore the ministers also are neglected. For as the abstract is cared for, so also is the concrete. Hence those sayings: *"The Priests are idle, without labour; they are covetous; they are proud and ambitious. It is better to be a robber of churches than a priest of them"* (*sacrilegum quam sacrificum*), and worse things of that sort. But if indeed that is idleness which godly and faithful ministers bear, greater idleness, surely, is that ***τῶν σακκοφόρων*** (of the sack-bearers) and ***ἡμιόνων ταλαεργῶν*** (of the toiling mules). For the labour, or idleness, of faithful ministers consumeth the strength of body and of mind, as all godly ministers <!-- p.232 --> find by experience. And whereas covetousness is reproved in ministers, let it by all means be justly reproved in many. Neither can a man speak for every one's life; for each must altogether give account of his own life. But this for the most part is manifest, that when godly ministers die, there are left by them truly orphans and poor widows, and that there is scarce so much at hand as is often required for the ceremonies of burial and for satisfying the creditors. Is this, then, so great a covetousness, whereby they have scraped together great riches? And unless at times a godly Magistrate had regard to the orphans and widows, the seed of the pastors would in many places be driven to beg. But these things are to be commended unto God; for in so great a perverseness of the world they cannot be changed. And it is a most true saying: *"What Christ taketh not, the treasury snatcheth."* Neither is it a new thing that the posterity of godly ministers is neglected by most men, as the history of the Shunammite testifieth, **4 Kings 4 [2 Kings 4]**.

Now here there falleth in the question, to whom, namely, the ownership of Ecclesiastical things, contributions and revenues, pertaineth. I answer: It pertaineth not to the Pontiffs, not to the Princes, not to the people, but it pertaineth to the Church; that is, it is lawful neither for Bishops, nor for Princes, nor for the people, to transfer these revenues to other uses than to the preservation of the ministry and of studies. **Gen. 47**: Joseph buyeth the goods of all the Egyptians for his King Pharaoh, excepting the fields of the Priests, appointed by Pharaoh himself to this end, that out of them the Priests might sustain themselves and their households. And in **Exod. 30** God commandeth that the money gathered for the sacrifice be laid up to be kept in the tabernacle of the testimony, that it may be to the people for a memorial before the Lord, that the Lord may be made propitious to the souls of the people. And in **Lev. 25**, among the other privileges of the Priests this also is set down, that the goods dedicated to them be not sold; for their field is a perpetual possession. And in **Num. 5** the same is commanded. Hence the Jurists deliver the Rule: *"That which hath once been dedicated to God is not to be transferred any further to human uses."*[^P11-5] Neither is it lawful even for the Priests themselves to alienate and sell <!-- p.233 --> sacred things, save in three cases, as the Jurists speak: First, if captives are to be delivered from infidels and Heathen, as the Emperor Maurice ought in this manner to have delivered those that were taken. Next, if the needy and the poor must be succoured in time of famine, war, pestilence. Thirdly, if the debt and borrowed money which that Church hath contracted is so great that it cannot otherwise be repaid, etc.

Hence it may easily be judged that the thefts in this kind are manifold, because all who enjoy these goods, the ministry of the Gospel being neglected, and studies neglected, do sin. Here is a huge number of Bishops, Canons and Monks, who are of no use to the Church, and devour the Ecclesiastical substance, while in the mean time the pastors are hungry, or none are kept, and studies are neglected.

At this day, after the doctrine of the Law and the Gospel hath by the benefit of God been purged again, many have in this matter been too indulgent to the civil Magistrate and to the nobles, who have plundered the Ecclesiastical substance, and transferred it to their own pomps and prodigalities. So great is the abuse of the true doctrine. Nor can this evil any longer be in any way allayed otherwise than if the minds of godly Princes be instructed and bent by the divine word, that they defile not their hands with those goods dedicated to God, but with these help studies; which by means of these goods they can most easily do, without other charges.

Most men laugh at these things, especially the wealthy, the cheats, and the robbers of temples; but it befalleth them that together with the things unjustly gotten they lose also the things justly gotten, as is wont to come to pass by little and little. Now these plunderers do commonly object these things as their arguments:

**I.** The Ecclesiastical goods were at first bequeathed unto idolatry. Now, idolatry ceasing, they are to be distributed to them that ask.

**II.** The Priests and ministers can be nourished otherwise, and be given stipends.

**III.** Let something by all means be given to them, even out of these goods, provided that the dignity and the fruits be with us.

<!-- p.234 -->

**IIII.** If I snatch not, there are others who ask and obtain.

Such things, and more of the same kind, do the plunderers pretend. But to the first let this be said, that, idolatry ceasing, the goods are now to be transferred to the uses of the true Church; even as the Emperor Constantine, that he might punish the Donatist heretics, transferred their Ecclesiastical substance to the true Church. And although the Donatists complained of this matter, and disputed that no man's substance ought to be taken from him on account of a disagreement in doctrines, yet **Augustine** answered that this money belongeth to the Church, and therefore is rightly taken away by the higher magistrates from those who pertain not to the Church, and transferred to the true uses of the Church. But at this day there commonly cometh to pass, well nigh everywhere, that which **Luther** said: that those goods were at first appointed for asses and swine, that is, for unlearned and Epicurean monks, and are now again turned aside to asses and swine, namely to unlearned Epicureans, covetous men, ***ἀθέους*** (godless men), enemies of studies. To the second thing which they object, let it be said indeed that it is true that stipends can be procured otherwise; but why is there need of more charges, if by those things which have been appointed aforetime this matter can most easily be ordered, without troubles and difficulties to the Commonwealth? But in what manner stipends are procured is too well known. Surely, unless the remnants from the Papacy were left over, many ministers would be consumed with hunger. The third is too true, that a fly is given and a Camel taken away; that a louse is given and a horse taken away. But these men ought to have been mindful of the saying, **Prov. 20**: *"It is a snare to a man to do despite unto that which is holy, and afterward to seek for vows,"* which is wont to be said in the German Proverb: *"Saint Martin, they offer thee a farthing, and steal from thee a horse."* And of this sin **Malachi 1** greatly complaineth. And God, **Lev. 22**, forbiddeth that anything having a blemish be given unto God. He that will give, let him be cheerful in giving, and let him give that which is good and whole, or not give at all. To the fourth objection of these men no answer ought to be made. For every man ought to consult his own conscience, and not to follow the wickedness of others, as it is written of Jehoshaphat King of Judah, **2 Chron. 7**: <!-- p.235 --> *"Jehoshaphat walked in the commandments of the Lord, and not after the doings of Israel."*

**What then is to be done, when the mighty seize upon the money, the lands, and the goods of the Church?**

**I.** Our sins are to be acknowledged and bewailed, which have given cause for just punishments. This also is to be known, that goods ordained for idolatrous worships are plundered because God abhorreth and punisheth those things also; even as **Pindar** likewise speaketh wisely of buildings: *"Buildings reared against the will of God abide not."* And **4 Kings 10 [2 Kings 10]**: The temple of Baal is turned by Jehu into a draught-house or sewer, even as at this day out of many monasteries there have been made kennels of dogs. For that which the Lord blesseth keepeth His blessing for ever, and contrariwise, that which He curseth is accursed, **1 Chron. 18**. These punishments are to be acknowledged.

**II.** Bondage is to be endured, and the doctrine of Christian liberty is to be held fast, which is a liberty of grace, not a civil liberty: as Joseph is the bondservant of Potiphar, and yet is most free with Christian liberty. This also is to be known, that the rights due unto the Church are of two sorts: the principal or substantial, pertaining chiefly to the ministry, such as are true doctrine, the use of the Sacraments, and true worships; others are bodily or accidental, as revenues, buildings, money, which are not to be defended by unjust means or by sedition. But whereas Laurence denieth the money of the Church to the Emperor Decius, he doth it by the instinct of the Holy Spirit. For Decius demanded the money to this end, that he might destroy the Church. Laurence therefore chose rather to suffer death than to yield to the tyrant, the enemy of the Church.

**III.** The consolation is to be held fast, that God will nevertheless provide lodgings for the Church, even without those wasters and thieves of the goods of the Church.

**IIII.** It is to be known that God will certainly punish the violence of those men, who neither get those goods with a good conscience, nor possess them with the divine <!-- p.236 --> blessing, as many examples testify, which it were too long to rehearse.

Yet let the examples be observed and considered of Antiochus and of Heliodorus, who plundered the goods of the temple at Jerusalem, and were horribly punished. And of M. Crassus it is written that, when he had plundered the temple at Jerusalem, and among other things had broken in pieces a golden beam of the temple, of a hundred and fifty minae of gold, and had turned it into pay for his soldiers, he afterward fought a most unhappy war against the Parthians. For his son being lost,[^P11-6] and eleven legions, which had been the glory of the whole Roman soldiery, being destroyed with miserable slaughter, he himself, being taken by the enemy, lest he should come into the power of King Aristonicus, so aimed the rod wherewith he had been wont to guide his horse at the eye of one of the barbarians, that he struck it out; who, inflamed by the violence of the pain and of the disgrace, thrust Crassus through the side with a dagger. And when his body, amid the promiscuous heaps of carcases, had been cast forth to be torn by birds and wild beasts, and his head, cut off together with his right hand, had been carried to the King, it was the utmost mockery to the enemy; and, as to a man most covetous and who had deserved most ill of sacred things, molten gold was poured into his gaping mouth, that he whose mind had burned with an impious lust for sacred gold might have even his dead and bloodless body scorched with the gold itself, the barbarian saying: *"Thou hast thirsted for gold; drink gold."*

The Devil doth not lightly suffer his own shrines to be destroyed, or violence to be offered to them. For the examples are known. Brennus, King of the Gauls, when he had overcome the Macedonians in war, turned his mind to the spoiling of the temples of the Gods, scurrilously jesting that the Gods, being rich, ought to be bountiful unto men. But when he had hastened to Delphi to plunder the temple of Apollo, where there was a very great store of gold and silver, an earthquake suddenly arising, a great part of the mountain was broken off, which laid low well nigh the whole army of the Gauls. And Brennus himself, afflicted with many wounds, slew himself with his own dagger.

<!-- p.237 -->

The same befell Xerxes also, King of the Persians, when he had sent four thousand armed men to Delphi to lay waste the temple of Apollo. For the whole multitude was destroyed by rains and thunderbolts, and utterly consumed.

There is also the well-known proverb: *"He hath gold of Tolosa."* For when Q. Scipio the Consul had plundered the town of Tolosa in Italy, and much gold and silver had been found in the temples, whosoever in that plundering dared to touch the gold, he straightway, departing out of life with horrible pain and torment, paid the penalty of the sacrilege committed. The proverb therefore is used against him who is visited with great and fatal evils, and perisheth by a strange and pitiable end.

Now since such things have befallen the Heathen, what is to be concluded concerning the wrath of God, when He seeth the temples of Christians plundered, and the Ecclesiastical goods bestowed to fill up the covetousness and lust of swine and asses? Which thing, to speak briefly, we do assuredly, if we will be just valuers of things, find daily: that all sacrilegious persons universally, or plunderers and unjust possessors of Ecclesiastical goods, do destroy themselves with their own sword. For never hath any man been found, not only in our own age but also in that of the ancients, who at any time laid impious hands upon things dedicated to God, or trafficked in them, with whom things have for a long time gone prosperously and happily — if only he did not straightway pay the punishment deserved. And although the man himself hath sometimes flourished for a while, yet his seed hath been horribly punished, disfigured and blotted out. For sacred things bring no succour, no help, to the profane; but like a deadly poison and a most cruel pestilence, as by a certain death-bringing contagion and wrath of their own, they are wont to infect and stain whatsoever is nearest to them. Whence they are compared to the feathers of Eagles, which consume the other feathers that lie near them. And there is an Italian proverb: *"Accursed is the house into which a holy stone is carried."*

**Augustine** relateth that, when at Rome a most grievous pestilence of women and of cattle was raging, insomuch that, for the multitude of them that died, <!-- p.238 --> it was believed that the race of living creatures would now fail, and when, moreover, a winter was raging with so incredible a fierceness of cold that snows of a dreadful depth lasted in the forum for forty days, beyond what was wont, and the Tiber was bound with very much ice, the answer was given, forsooth, that the cause of the pestilence was this, that many private men held very many sacred houses in their occupation; and that when these had afterward been restored, then at length so many calamities, so many prodigies and deaths, ceased.

But I cease to make more words of these things, especially since we seem to make them in vain, and no man any longer heareth those who admonish well, even though punishments, publicly and privately, do vex both the Princes and Magistrates themselves, and the Nobles, the Counsellors, and other subjects, plunderers of the Ecclesiastical goods, and do exhort them to repentance and restitution. God Himself will reform all these things, with a judgement assuredly fierce and dreadful. But let these things be said briefly of the second kind of sacrilege, and sufficiently for him that understandeth.

The third kind of sacrilege, which is properly named sacrilege, when a sacred thing is carried off out of a sacred place, is a matter of private persons and things, and differeth not from other thefts, which are punished by hanging. Now it is committed either by those who are in office, or by other thieves, who are known to bear the name of thieves. By those who are in office: as when the keepers of the temples keep not the money faithfully, but privily pluck from it as much as they can, and transfer it either to their own or to other uses. Likewise: they who sell and buy prefectures, provostships, parishes, prebends, etc.

And these are named Simoniacs. Likewise: when the Stewards of scholars and of sick folk are unfaithful, and steal and withdraw from the food, the drink, the garments of the sick, and neither care for them as they ought, nor reach to them the things which they ought, and turn legacies left to pious causes to their own uses. These things suffice concerning sacrilege.

**III.** The third kind of theft are all ***πλεονεξίαι*** (covetous overreachings) and frauds in contracts, in buying, in selling, in measures, <!-- p.239 --> etc. And hither belongeth the doctrine of contracts, the foundations whereof are contained in these Rules: **[I.]** Natural law is truly divine law, according to which every man ought to render to his neighbour that which he would have done to himself by another. **II.** Moral certainty sufficeth in moral affairs. For the most exact reckoning is not to be sought, but the mean is to be taken ***ἐν πλάτει*** (in breadth), or broadly (*superficialiter*). And hither alludeth the verse of **Horace**:

> Virtue is the mean of vices, and drawn back from either side.  

And **Aristotle**, defining Virtue, saith that it is a habit consisting in a mean. And **Isocrates** saith prudently: *"Because it is hard to hit the mean, it is better to do too little than to exceed."* But these things are more general. **III.** In every contract charity, flowing from faith, ought to shine before, and nothing is to be done in contracts which fighteth against the charity of the Law. **IIII.** In contracts and exchanges of things the Arithmetical proportion, or ***τὸ ἴσον*** (the equal), is to be kept. **V.** The Commandment of Christ, **Luke 6**, is to be kept in the lending of things: *"Lend, hoping for nothing again"*; that is, although a like service can by no means be hoped for from those to whom the loan is given, yet ought it to be lent to them. Neither doth Christ forbid us to hope that the principal itself be repaid — otherwise it were no loan — but He forbiddeth that after the heathen manner we give only to those from whom we can hope for a recompense.

Every contract, therefore, that fighteth against these Rules is unlawful and forbidden.

### *Quid autem de usuris statuendum est?* · But What Is to Be Held Concerning Usury?

Seeing that usury is a gain exacted, by reason of the service of lending, over and above the principal of any thing whatsoever, it is easily evident that it is unjust to ask and to receive usury; and this is testified first by the divine Law, **Exod. 22**: *"Thou shalt not oppress my people <!-- p.240 --> with usury."* **Lev. 24**: *"Thou shalt not give thy money upon usury. Take thou no usury of thy brother, nor more than thou gavest."* **Ezek. 8**: *"The man, if he hath not given forth upon usury, neither hath taken any increase,[^P11-7] he is just, and shall surely live."* And **Ps. 14 [15]**: *"Lord, who shall abide in thy tabernacle? and who shall rest in thy holy hill? He that hath not put out his money to usury."* **Deut. 23**: *"Thou shalt not lend upon usury to thy brother."*

Let us embrace these divine testimonies, and know that usury doth truly displease God; neither let us seek excuses for manifest usuries against the word of God. Next let us consider also the reasons gathered by natural judgement:

> Civil society cannot endure, unless equality be kept in the communication of things.  
> In usury equality is not kept, since more is received than is given, the Arithmetical proportion being violated.  
> Therefore usuries destroy civil society.

Even so have great Nations been brought to want, and many have slain their own parents, whose heirs they knew they should be, as the Jurists prove in treating of the liberty under the Macedonian decree of the Senate (*Senatusconsultum Macedonianum*).[^P11-8] For there was a certain usurer, by name Macedo, who at times lent upon usury to young men, who, that they might be able to repay the debt at the appointed time, slew their own parents by poisonings or by other treacheries. This also is certain, that because of usuries many have been compelled to sell their children, from which thing changes of empires have often arisen. And for that cause the heathen often moderated usuries; as Solon, Lucullus, Cicero, Julius, Augustus and many others set a bound beforehand to usuries, that no more should be paid than the hundredth (*centesima*), that is, that usury which in the hundredth month after the money was borrowed, namely in the eighth year and the fourth month, equalleth the sum of the principal, by the paying of one florin in the hundred every month, which in the year made twelve florins in the hundred. And **Cicero** relateth that Brutus exacted four hundredths in Cyprus, and that certain Senators were starved to death. That usury made forty-eight in the hundred. And in the book of **Nehemiah, chap. 5**, the Jews are commanded to remit the hundredth.
