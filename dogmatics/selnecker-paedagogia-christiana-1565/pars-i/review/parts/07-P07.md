Even as **Homer** for this same cause nameth ***ἀριδάκρυας ἄνδρας*** (men quick to tears), especially in the feeling of the natural affections (*storgae*):[^P07-1] as when Archidamus, the enemy being overcome, was returning into his country, his father, beholding his son, whom the citizens and friends went forth to meet, began for joy to weep; even as it is written also of the Emperor Frederick, when his son Maximilian was created Emperor, that he began to weep abundantly, beholding the pomp and dignity, and wishing his son joy of the majesty. And good parents, especially mothers, whose affections by reason of the weakness and tenderness of their nature are more easily moved, sweeter, and also more vehement — they feel, I say, motions of this kind <!-- p.142 --> very often. And hither pertaineth Anteros, whom the ancients set over the mutual conjunction of minds, as it is said in the little verses:

> This one is sung as one, yet that one  
> May be called both twin and one,  
> And, what can scarce be reckoned, in him  
> Twice one maketh not two, but one.  
> This, to wit, is the one force of Anteros.  
> This is the mutual force of mutual love.  

But I return to the *storgae*:

How sweet is this saying, **Sirach 4**: *"Be as a father unto the fatherless, and as the master of the house unto their mother. So shalt thou in turn be a tender son of the most High, and He shall embrace thee more sweetly than thy mother."*

It is worthy of consideration also, that God hath placed the breasts in the bosom in mankind, that He might distinguish men and beasts. For the beasts only nourish their offspring, afterward they neglect it, and forgetfulness followeth both of the parents and of the offspring: but men pour over ***στοργὴν*** (natural affection) into their offspring, and the remembrance is kept, and there ariseth a mutual love. Parents owe also instruction and wisdom to their offspring.

Now let us speak of the duty of parents, which consisteth in these three things: I. In ***νουθεσίᾳ κυρίου*** (the admonition of the Lord), that is, that they instruct their children in the doctrine of God. II. ***παιδείᾳ*** (nurture), that is, in discipline, or the honest governing of manners, and in the cultivation of good arts. III. In the nourishing and defending of their children, whereunto they are driven by the natural ***στοργαῖς*** (affections).

Here let the ***ἀστοργία*** (want of natural affection) and the indulgence of parents be censured. ***Ἀστοργία***, as: such as is in the ravens, which forsake their unfledged young, whom God meanwhile wonderfully feedeth with little worms bred of dung, and of the flesh of carcases heaped together into the nests, until, being grown, they are able to fly forth to get their living, **Ps. 146 [147]**: *"Who giveth to the beast his food, and to the young ravens which call upon Him."* Of ***ἀστοργίας*** there are many examples in the Tragedies, in the histories, and in the whole of life, as: of Absalom toward his father. Of Herod toward his sons. Of the Turks toward their sons. Medea slayeth <!-- p.143 --> her own children conceived of Jason, when he had married another. Hercules slayeth his wife and sons. A mother among the Xanthians hangeth herself from the roof, and hath her son hanging from her neck, and holdeth a torch, wherewith she setteth the house on fire. Selim, the Turkish Emperor, slayeth his father and his brother. Nero most cruelly slayeth his wife, his sister, his mother and others. And it is rightly said:

> No house at all is without Tragic examples.  

And the greater the families are, the more Tragic are the calamities.

The indulgence of parents is altogether to be reproved. For this is punished by God with the ungodliness of the children, and with other calamities, as is manifest in Eli, **1 Kings 2 [1 Sam. 2]**. And there is a common verse:

> The fond indulgence of fathers maketh slothful sons.  

And **Sirach, chap. 7 and 30**, earnestly warneth that parents be not indulgent to their children, saying: *"Hast thou children? instruct them, and bow down their neck from their tender years."* *"He that loveth his son keepeth him under the rod, that he may afterward have joy of him."* *"He that is indulgent to his son bewaileth his stripes, and is affrighted as often as he weepeth."* This doctrine is necessary at this time, in which parents think it enough if they leave great riches to their children, and have no regard of true instruction, and indulge their children by winking at most vices — drunkenness, blasphemies, whoredom, pride, revelling into the midst of the night. What blessing can there be here? Christ, being twelve years old, is brought by His parents into the temple a journey of nineteen miles, and yet He Himself had no need of instruction. So let godly parents also do, and bring their children into the temple to hear the word of God, wherein they meet with no offence, as they do in almost all other places. For there is no place in the whole world where there are not offences, whereby thou mayest either be made sad or be led astray. But in the assembly of the godly, coming together that they may hear the word of God, thy God speaketh with thee, and teacheth thee concerning life and death. <!-- p.144 --> Hither bring thy children with thee, and, being returned home, inquire what they have marked. For this is the true and best wisdom, which bringeth forth fortune and welfare to the children, and comfort and gladness to the parents, saith Solomon.

Furthermore, the duties of children are comprised in this saying of **Sirach 3**: *"Honour thy Father and Mother, in word, in deed, and in patience."*

Now the duties of children may be thus reckoned up. I. Love and Reverence. II. Obedience. III. Gratitude. IIII. Patience, or ***ἐπιείκεια*** (forbearance).

Of love it is wont commonly to be said: *"Love descendeth, it ascendeth not."* For parents embrace their children with a greater ***στοργῇ*** (natural affection) than children their parents. And the history is known, that in Flanders, at Ghent, a father and a son were prisoners, and were condemned by the magistrate for certain crimes. The father was a decrepit old man. The son a tall man, and high-spirited. And when the entreaties of many made intercession for them, it was concluded by a certain peculiar law, that the one should become the executioner of the other, and that either the father should behead the son, or the son the father, and that to him who should have beheaded the other life should be granted. This being reported to them both, it was agreed between them that the son should take upon himself the charge of beheading his father, and should himself remain longer among the living; and so it was done. And therefore, for a memorial of the deed, a statue was set up, containing upon the bridge the figure of that sturdy son beheading his decrepit father. And this saying was added: *"Love descendeth, it ascendeth not."* Now although the Magistrate dealt cruelly in passing such a sentence, yet the history, seeing it came to pass, is worthy of remembrance for this saying: *"Love descendeth, it ascendeth not."* In which saying let us know that there is an image of the vast love of God toward us, and of our faint love toward God in return. Love descendeth from God; it doth not in turn ascend unto God such as it ought to be. Let us consider this, and mark the sentence of **Augustine**: *"Love the love of Him, who for love of thy love descended into the womb of the virgin, and there coupled His love with thy love, by humbling Himself, <!-- p.145 --> by exalting thee, by joining the light of His eternity to the clay of thy mortality."*

To the love of parents belongeth reverence, or honour, which, as is wont commonly and truly to be said, is not in him that is honoured, but in him that honoureth. For greater is the glory, both with God and with men, of sons who honour their parents, than of the parents themselves who are honoured by their sons. And most worthy of remembrance are the verses of **Euripides**, which the young ought often to think upon:

> ***Ὅστις δὲ τοὺς τεκόντας ἐν βίῳ σέβει,***  
> ***Ὁ δ' ἐστὶ καὶ ζῶν καὶ θανὼν θεῷ φίλος,***  
> ***Οὐκ ἐστὶ παισὶ τούτου κάλλιον γέρας***  
> ***Ἢ πατρὸς ἐσθλοῦ κ' ἀγαθοῦ πεφυκέναι,***  
> ***Καὶ τοῖς τεκοῦσιν ἀξίαν τιμὴν νέμειν.***  
> ***Ἐρᾶτε μητρὸς παῖδες, ὡς οὐκ ἔστ' ἔρως***  
> ***Τοιοῦτος ἄλλος, οἷος ἡδίον ἐρᾶν.***  

> (Whoso in life doth reverence them that begat him,  
> He is, both living and dead, dear unto God.  
> There is for children no fairer honour than this,  
> To be sprung of a father noble and good,  
> And to render to their parents the honour that is due.  
> Love your mother, ye children, for there is no love  
> Of such a kind beside, that is sweeter to love.)  

Sweet also are the verses of **Orpheus**, which **Stobaeus** citeth:

> ***Ζεὺς δ' ἐφορᾷ γονέων ὁπόσοι τίουσι θέμιστας,***  
> ***Ἠδ' ὅσοι οὐκ ἀλέγουσιν ἀναιδέα θυμὸν ἔχοντες,***  
> ***Καὶ τοῖς μὲν πρόφρων τε καὶ ἤπιος ἐσθλὰ δίδωσι,***  
> ***Τοῖς δὲ κακὰ φρονέων νεμεσίζεται ἐν μενίδεοσί.*** [?]  
> ***Δειναὶ γὰρ κατὰ γαῖαν ἐριννύες εἰσὶ τοκήων.***  

> (Zeus looketh upon as many as honour the rights of parents,  
> And upon as many as regard them not, having a shameless heart;  
> And to the one, forward and gentle, he giveth good things,  
> But with the other, minding evil against them, he is wroth in his anger.  
> For dreadful upon the earth are the Furies of parents.)  

Of this last verse there are many examples, as of Ham, cursed by his father for his shamelessness. And what befell in our own age at Freiberg in Meissen is known, where a son, when he had been chidden by a father prone to cursing with these words: *"Stand, and standing so remain, as long as thou shalt live,"* stood, and standing so remained many years, nor could he ever be moved from the place, even unto the last breath of life. And he died in the year 1552. This history, seeing it is true, and many yet live who know that it so befell, warneth parents that they do not lightly curse their children, and warneth children that they despise not the curses of their parents, which seldom pass away without hurt, as **Sirach** often testifieth: *"The curse of the mother overthroweth the houses of the children."*

<!-- p.146 -->

We know a godly, learned and renowned man, who used this imprecation against a stubborn son: *"Because thou hast troubled Israel, God trouble thee."* But there are now such times, as those whereof Paul taught **in the first to the Romans**: that in the last times there should come despisers of parents, etc. But godly children, living in the fear of God, know what they ought to do. They love and reverence their parents, and set before themselves the example of Christ Himself, who was obedient unto His parents; they rejoice also, if at any time they can be a delight and an ornament to their parents, as Epaminondas said that he took this as the chief fruit of his labours, that he had his parents for beholders of his glory.

### *Obedientia* · Obedience

Out of love and reverence is born a willing obedience, which is rendered from the heart, in all duties which are not against the commandment and word of God. For so Paul saith **to the Coloss.**: *"Children, obey your parents in all things: for this is well pleasing in the Lord."* But if parents will require anything against God, they are not to be obeyed: as when Chrysanthus denieth obedience to his father, who would have him renounce the Christian faith, and who to that end prepareth a splendid banquet, five most beautiful maidens being brought in, that by these allurements he may the more easily persuade his son to fall away. Rightly saith **Jerome** upon that place, **Matt. 10**, *"He that loveth father or mother more than me is not worthy of me"*: *"If the necessity shall come,"* saith he, *"that the love of parents or of children be set against the love of God, and both cannot be kept, hatred toward one's own, piety toward God, is to be kept."* For in this place the Rule holdeth: We must obey God rather than men.

### *Gratitudo* · Gratitude

<!-- p.147 -->

Rightly said **Aristotle**, in book 9 of the *Ethics*: To God, to Parents and to Teachers an equivalent price cannot be repaid. Yet must it be done, as far as it can be done, both in word and in deed, and we ought to be mindful of the saying: *"Evil shall not depart from the house of the Unthankful."*

Well known is the word ***ἀντιπελαργεῖν***, that is, to render to parents the thanks that are due for one's nurture, or ***θρεπτήρια***, or ***θρέπτρα*** (the returns made for rearing), or, as Paul speaketh: ***ἀμοιβὰς ἀποδιδόναι τοῖς προγόνοις*** (to requite their parents). And the word is taken from the Storks, which in Greek are called ***πέλαργοι***, because they feed their parents when worn out with age, and bear them upon their own shoulders, and therefore in Hebrew they are named *Chasidah*, as it were the godly, and merciful toward their parents. And among the ancients the Stork was the emblem of piety. And the Germans name it *Storch*, from the natural ***στοργαῖς*** (affections).

The daughter of Cimon fed with her breasts her father, who was to be put to death by hunger. And at Rome a certain woman lately delivered of a child, lowly and of no rank, came to her mother, who was shut up in prison, and fed her with her breasts, though she had always been searched by the doorkeeper, whether she were carrying in any food. This being found out, her life was granted to the mother, and both were maintained with a perpetual provision of food, and that place was consecrated to Piety. **Pliny**, book 7, chap. 36. This came to pass in the year of the world 3816, before Christ 147.

In the eighty-first Olympiad, that is, in the year of the world 3509, before Christ 454, they say that Etna flowed with fire, at which time Philonomus and Callias, men of Catana, took up their fathers and carried them out through the midst of the flame, despising their other goods.[^P07-2] Therefore help was bestowed on them from heaven. For as they ran, the fire was parted asunder on that side by which they were going in. These things **Stobaeus** relateth out of **Aelian**. **Pausanias** also, and **Lycurgus** against Leocrates, and **Virgil** in the *Aetna*, rehearse the same history of two men of Catana, Amphinomus and Astapus:[^P07-3]

> To them their only riches were mother and father.  
> And rightly is piety the safest virtue for man.  
> The flames blushed to touch the godly youths,  
> <!-- p.148 -->And wheresoever they bear their steps, give way.  

**Homer** writeth of Simoisius, that his life was shortened and cut off, because he did not render to his parents ***θρέπτρα***, or the duties owed for his nurture, ***ἰλιαδ. δ*** & ***ρ.*** (*Iliad* 4 and 17):

> ***οὐδὲ τοκεῦσι***  
> ***Θρέπτρα φίλοις ἀπέδωκε, μινυνθάδιος δέ οἱ αἰών.***  

> (nor to his dear parents  
> Did he repay the price of his rearing, and short was his span of life.)  

And among the ancient Laws this also is extant: ***ἐάν τις μὴ τρέφει γονεῖς, ἄτιμος ἔστω*** (If any man nourish not his parents, let him be without honour).

It is told of a certain son, to whom his father had delivered over his goods, and who nevertheless was held of no account by the son. Once, when the father had asked that a coat might be bought for him by his son, the son gave him an ell of cloth, that he might have his torn coat mended. Now the son also had a little son of three years, who came to his father weeping, and saying: *"Father, give me also an ell of cloth."* The father gave it. The little son went up, and hid the cloth by the roof of the house. The father, following privily, when he saw what his little son was doing, asked of him why he hid the cloth. To whom the little son: *"I will keep the cloth,"* saith he, *"until thou also grow old, that I may have cloth to give thee, as thou hast now given to my grandfather."* The father, being thus admonished, came to himself. And this is that which **Isocrates** saith: *"Show thyself such toward thy parents, as thou wouldest wish thy children to show themselves toward thee."* And **Sirach 3**: *"Whoso honoureth his father shall have joy of his own children."* And there is known out of the *Ethics* of **Aristotle** the history of the son dragging his father by the hair as far as the stairs, to whom the father said: *"Cease, O son, for thus far I also dragged my father, thy grandfather, by the hair."*

> For with what honour each man reverenceth his parents,  
> So shall the parent receive worthy rewards from his children.  

### *Patientia* · Patience

***Ἐπιείκεια*** (forbearance), bearing with the infirmities and blemishes of parents. **Sirach 3**: *"My son, care for thy father in his old age, and grieve him not as long as he liveth. And take it in good part, if he be become <!-- p.149 --> a child, and despise him not, although thou be wiser."*

T. Manlius threateneth death to the Tribune who had appointed a day of trial for his father, a man most harsh toward his son Manlius, warning him to change his purpose. This was praiseworthy, that the harshness of his father turned the mind of Manlius no whit from filial piety.

A certain young man of Eretria heard Zeno so long, until he reached man's estate. Afterward, being returned to Eretria, when his father asked what wisdom, pray, he had learned in so great a space of time, he answered that he would show it; and not long after he performed it. For when his father was angry, and at length laid stripes upon him, he, quiet and enduring, said that he had learned this, namely, to bear easily the anger of his parent, and not to take it ill, nor to be angry. How much more ought children furnished with the Christian faith to bear patiently the blemishes of their parents, in words and in deed? And they that answer again are of the worst disposition. More rightly said Pittacus to a son going to law with his father in the court: *"If thou shalt speak things more unjust than thy father, thou shalt be condemned; if things more just, even for that thou art worthy to be condemned."* And let these things now have been said of the duties of children; to which I judge that there ought to be added the most sweet name which is extant in **Gen. 19**, *Abimael*, that is, my father from God. For so ought all children to hold, that their parents were given them from God, even though of slender lot and condition.

IIII. Hither pertaineth ***Φιλαδελφία***, brotherly love, that there be concord among brethren, concerning which **Ps. 133** preacheth.

The proposition of the Psalm is: Concord in the Church, in the commonwealth and in the Household is a good thing. This proposition it adorneth with two similitudes, whereof the former is taken from the quiet estate of the Church, the latter from the quiet estate of the commonwealth, and of the Household which is contained therein.

In the former similitude it setteth down not any oil whatsoever, but the holy oil, wherewith the High Priests were anointed by divine commandment, which dropped down from the crown of the High Priest's head upon the beard; then upon the rest of the body, even unto the lower hem of the garment. And this oil was only in Judaea, and not in other <!-- p.150 --> Countries, which is a marvel to tell. With oil of old the Priests were consecrated, and the Kings, bearing the type of the Son of God, the true King and Priest. And the ointment was made of choice myrrh, cassia, cinnamon and reeds (*calami*), which grow fragrant also in Arabia, India and Syria: which, being bruised together, were mingled with the oil of Olives, and so a most sweet ointment was made, wherewith Moses, sprinkling the Priests themselves and the tabernacle, cleansed them, **Exod. 30**, and **Josephus**, book 3 of the *Antiquities*.

Now first of all myrrh was used. For this preserveth them that are subject to the cross and to death, through the blood and death of the Son of God. Myrrh is not without thorns, and it preserveth the bodies of the dead uncorrupted. So the doctrine of the Gospel setteth forth the kingdom of the cross in this life, in which nevertheless there is no rottenness, seeing the powers of death are broken. The stem also of the myrrh is clean, and twisted, from whose bark, when it is cut, there floweth down a tear. This is the most pure blood of Christ, twisted, and cut, that is, shed for us.

Next, Cassia was used. This is good for giving clearness to the eyes, and, being drunk, it helpeth against the bites of vipers. So the doctrine of the Gospel first removeth the darkness, and bringeth back the light to men, that men may be able to judge what is white, what black, what true, what false, and that they may have a sure way wherein they must walk, and that they may be delivered from all dread and fear, which is wont for the most part to befall in the darkness; next, it driveth out the poison in two ways, by resisting the sins which are yet in the flesh, and by overcoming all corruptions and heresies which can ever arise.

Afterward cinnamon was used, which groweth in the plains among the thickest brambles and briers, like unto a dry thing, and the greener it is, the less doth its odour smell sweet. This is the estate of the Church in this life. It is born and increased amid the very waves of the sea, and amid the ragings of the Devils and of the world, and its appearance is mean and abject, whether it be green or not; and <!-- p.151 --> the more plentiful it is, the more it is despised, and the less cared for, as at this time. And this is a marvellous thing, to be considered of all: The more the Church is pressed, the more it groweth; and the more it groweth, the more it is pressed, despised and neglected. So the more the gem which they call the Pearl is shaken by the waves of the sea, the more it increaseth, and becometh the purer.

Last of all the reeds were used, which are most fragrant, and send forth a wondrous sweetness of breath. These are the fruits of faith, and good works in the regenerate, Concord, Temperance, Chastity, etc.

All these, being bruised, that is, confirmed by exercises, are mingled with the oil of olives, that is, they bring forth peace and tranquillity. For *"being justified by faith, we have peace with God."* And oil elsewhere denoteth the preaching of the Gospel, **Luke 10**. But as reeds, when they are broken, become cleavable into many parts: so the Church, being once broken and weakened by contentions and brawlings, by ambition, and by the like vices, is rent into many parts, and there spring up in it from time to time more contentions and heresies, even about things not necessary, which our age evidently proveth. But these things ***ἀλληγορικῶς*** (allegorically).

Now in the Psalm mention is made first of the High Priest. For agreement in the Church is required first of all. From the top of the head the Balsam floweth upon the beard, afterward upon the whole body; that is, from Christ the High Priest floweth the Balsam, that is, the Doctrine and the Holy Spirit, upon those who have cleaved more nearly to the mouth of Christ, as upon the Apostles, and others whom Christ called and taught immediately, without the ministry of man. From these, as they teach, the Balsam floweth upon the remaining members of Christ even unto the hem, that is, upon the universal Church, which shall endure even unto the end of the world. Such degrees are rehearsed in **Hebr. 2**: *"Salvation, which at the first began to be spoken by the Lord, was confirmed unto us by them that heard Him."*

<!-- p.152 -->

**Augustine** transferreth the definition of Friendship which is extant in **Cicero**, that Friendship is an agreement in things divine and human, etc., to concord in the Church. For Julian the Apostate is no friend of Basil, although he write unto him: ***φίλος φίλῳ καὶ ἀδελφὸς ἀδελφῷ*** (friend to friend, and brother to brother). And how dissension in religion troubleth all friendship and ***φιλαδελφίαν*** (brotherly love), this our age showeth. And a memorable example is the history of Diaz the Spaniard. The godly Diaz is slain at Neuburg in the Palatinate, by the treachery and devices of his brother Alphonsus, for the confession of the true doctrine, in the year of Christ 1546. And this our age affordeth many other examples in many places, whereof Christ Himself saith: *"I came not to send peace, but a sword."* The latter similitude pertaineth to commonwealths and Households, concerning which let the noble sentence of **Sallust** be marked: *"By concord small things grow, by discord the greatest fall to ruin."* And the history is known of Scilurus the father, who, being about to admonish his sons, of whom there were eighty, unto mutual concord, a little before his death held out a bundle of javelins, and bade each one break it in pieces. But when they all, being wearied, had effected nothing, he himself, drawing out the javelins one by one, brake them all easily, and so taught them that, united and of one mind, they should continue strong, but, being at discord, they should be weak. Known are the histories of the Theban brothers, Eteocles and Polynices, the sons of Oedipus, king of Thebes.

**Xenophon** also, the historian, and a Commander most skilled in the art of war, describeth a whole war of his own time waged between two brothers, Artaxerxes Mnemon, surnamed the Great, and Cyrus the younger, the sons of Darius Nothus.

And known are the histories of the Macedonian Princes, fighting among themselves for the kingdom after the death of Alexander the Great. Likewise: of those three grandsons of Charles the Great, Lothair, Lewis the German, and Charles the Bald, fighting among themselves for the Empire. But who could rehearse the examples, whose <!-- p.153 --> numberless number experience also showeth? And **Plutarch** writeth, that among tyrants it is as customary to slay brothers who are partners of the kingdom, as it is customary with the Geometers to assume ***αἰτήματα*** (postulates) as things granted.

But seeing it is known that all discord ariseth from the Devil, let us be intent, let us watch, and let us pray, that we may be one in God. And surely, if ever there was need of this prayer, there is need of it now in our age, both in the Church, and in the commonwealth, and in the affairs of the family. God rule us mercifully, and confirm among us, for the glory of His Name, the desire of tranquillity and of peace ***τῆς κουροτρόφου*** (the nurse of the young), as **Hesiod** nameth peace, and repress the ragings of the Devils, and scatter the seeds of contentions, of which the Devil hath long since endeavoured, and continually endeavoureth, to sow an infinite number. Let the significations of the words also be marked: *Brethren* in this place are they named, who are bound one to another either by nature, as lawful brothers and brothers-german; or by law, as the Church, and one's country, or the Commonwealth; or by use, as colleagues, companions and neighbours. Of these it is said in **Sirach 25**: *"Three things are by far the fairest, which please God and men: the unity of brethren, the mutual love of neighbours, a man and a wife that agree well together."* And **Prov. 16**: *"God will abhor the author of dissension among brethren."*

*Balsam* signifieth the lord of ointments, by reason of the excellency wherein it excelled in Judaea alone. Howbeit in the Hebrew there is not *Balsam*, but precious ointment, *Schemen hatof*.

*Hermon* is a mountain beyond Jordan, **Josh. 12**, from which the neighbouring places are called *Hermonim*, **Josh. 13**. And that beyond Jordan the places are most rich in pastures, is evident from **Num. 32**. The dew therefore which falleth there is said to be the best. And the same dew, not in number but in kind, that is, equally fruitful, falleth upon the hill country of Judaea, or upon the mountains of Zion. And the whole land is written to flow with milk and honey. Unto this dew is compared brotherly kindness, of which we ought rightly to sing in the Church: <!-- p.154 --> *"Drop down, ye heavens, from above"* (*Rorate coeli desuper*). But of these things thus far.

Now we must speak also of the duty of the Magistrate and of subjects.

Now whatsoever can be said of the duty of fathers, the same is to be understood also of the duty of the Magistrate.

For Princes, Cities and Magistrates are nothing else but Fathers of their country, as it is said in **Isa. 49**: *"Kings shall be thy nursing fathers, and their Queens thy nursing mothers."* And **Xenophon** saith excellently: ***ἀγαθὸς ἄρχων ἢ βασιλεὺς οὐδὲν διαφέρει ἀγαθοῦ πατρός*** (a good ruler or king differeth nothing from a good father). And in **Homer** a good Prince is named ***ἤπιος πατὴρ*** (a gentle father). And **Aristotle** saith: Like is the relation of a King to his subjects, of a shepherd to his sheep, of a father to his sons.

By this name, therefore, two very great vices are taken away: I. Tyranny. II. Too much indulgence.

Tyranny is, when Princes reign by violence, and make away with whom they list, and do what pleaseth themselves, by right or by wrong, and consider not that they bear rule over men, and ought to be fathers, not wolves.

Wisely said Agathon: A Prince must remember three things: I. That he beareth rule over men. II. That he ruleth according to the Laws. III. That he shall not bear rule for ever.

Now what the end of tyrants is, the examples show of Pharaoh, of Absalom, of Jezebel, of Joash, of Herod the Great, of Herod Antipas, etc. And in the Heathen histories there is known the example of Astyages, King of the Medes, who commanded his grandson Cyrus to be exposed, and set before Harpagus his own little son, that he might eat him. He was afterward vanquished by Cyrus, and deprived of his kingdom. Likewise: Cambyses the son of Cyrus, King of the Persians, being reproved by Prexaspes for drunkenness, slew the son of Prexaspes with a dart that he let fly. He killed also his brother Smerdis, and his own wife, who had before been his brother's wife, when she bewailed the perfidy of brothers at the spectacle of a fight between a lion and dogs. Likewise: Tarquin the Proud, the seventh King of the Romans, slew his father-in-law Servius <!-- p.155 --> Tullius, the sixth King of the Romans. And his wife, the daughter of Tullius, drove her chariot over the dead body of her slain father. Cambyses slew himself with a sword. Tarquin with his sons and his wife was cast out of the city. Of Theodoric of Verona we read that, when he had unjustly slain two Roman consuls, Symmachus and Boethius, it happened on a time that there was set before the King at supper the boiled head of a great fish. At the sight of this the King, being terrified, began to tremble, thinking it to be the head of Symmachus, and he rose up, and betook himself to bed, and crying out so long, he bewailed in his bed the murder of the Consuls, until he gave up the ghost. This is the conscience and the misery of tyrants. And many other examples are to be marked in the histories, of Nero, of Valens, of Caligula, etc.

And it is truly said: *"Neither doth the man that is a tyrant go to his grave bewailed upon the earth."* Likewise:

> To the son-in-law of Ceres without slaughter and blood few  
> Kings go down, and few tyrants by a dry death.  

And the same saith **Ps. 55**: *"Bloody men shall not live out half their days."*

Indulgence also is to be reproved, which is, to wink at manifest crimes, and not to use the sword against the stubborn. It is true indeed that ***ἐπιείκειαν*** (equity) and lenity ought to be used, but according to the nature of the circumstances, and of the deed itself. Neither are intercessions and entreaties for the guilty to be lightly admitted, if the crime be manifest and certain. And the divine right, or commandment, and the ordinance of God, is always to be preferred before the intercessions of others. Therefore **Publianus**[^P07-4] saith: *"He hurteth the good, whosoever hath spared the evil."* And: *"He inviteth fault, who hath hushed up a sin."* And: *"Wickedness that prospereth is the calamity of the best men."* And **Cicero** saith: *"Let the Magistrate restrain the disobedient and hurtful citizen by fine, by bonds, by stripes."* But of these things we must treat more largely in the fifth Commandment.

Now we have said that the duty of a good father consisteth in ***νουθεσίᾳ κυρίου*** (the admonition of the Lord), as Paul calleth it, and in ***παιδείᾳ*** (nurture), and in nourishing.

<!-- p.156 -->

Such therefore let the good Magistrate also be. First, let him take care that the true doctrine concerning God sound in his Countries, that is, let him be *Thideal*, **Gen. 14**, which word signifieth one that acknowledgeth God, according to the verse:

> The greatest virtue of a Prince is to know God.  

So did Constantine I, Theodosius, and other godly Princes.

Next, let him take care of discipline, which is done in three ways: I. If he be the keeper of the Decalogue, as touching outward obedience, and so be as it were the Vicar of God, sounding forth the voice of the Moral Law, and ruling his subjects according to it. II. Let him be the keeper of the positive Laws, and of his own, which are determinations of the Decalogue, and are as it were helps for keeping the Law of nature, or the Decalogue. And hither pertaineth the saying of **Aristotle**: ***ὁ ἄρχων φύλαξ τῶν νόμων*** (the ruler is the guardian of the laws). III. Let him be also the executor of the Decalogue and of his own Laws, and let him exercise judgements, and punish the stubborn with bodily punishments, **Rom. 13**: *"He beareth not the sword in vain, but is the minister of God."*

Afterward, let him be the nourisher and defender of his own, of their goods and of their bodies, against unjust violence, and let him favour his own with a fatherly mind: which also is done in two ways: I. If he be peaceable, a lover and keeper of peace, and defend his own against robberies, and stand for the Law, and for the Flock, as Alphonsus showeth in his Device (*Symbolum*), having a Pelican drawing forth blood from her breast with her beak, to nourish her young. II. If he be ***γεώργιος*** (a husbandman), that is, if he provide for the sustenance of his subjects, that they be not flayed with exactions too great, even as Tiberius said: *"It is the part of a good shepherd to shear the flock, not to flay it."* And in Daniel, the Prince is a tree, under which the subjects are fed. The Magistrate hath indeed the right of exacting tributes: but there are three ends, which **Cicero** also reciteth in the oration for the Manilian Law: that they be the ornaments of peace, the aids of war, and the sinews of the Commonwealth. But seeing it often cometh to pass that, for the avoiding of greater dangers, and for the driving away of the enemies of the country, and for other extraordinary and unwonted burdens of this kind, the <!-- p.157 --> Magistrate hath need of greater tributes, it is lawful to ask extraordinary tributes also; which ought not to be the instruments of private lust or riot, as is done everywhere at this day.

From these things it is also in some sort evident, how far the Magistrate hath right over the goods of his subjects: not as touching the seizing of them, but as touching the defending of them, as the history showeth of the ungodly Ahab, seizing by force the vineyard of Naboth. And this is to speak what is right (*aequum*), although flatterers for the most part get the horse (*equum*), as is said in that history of the Emperor Henry of Luxemburg.

Now let us mark the name of Father. And there is a royal name in Genesis, *Abimelech*, that is, my father the King.

Valerius Messala, when by commandment of the Roman people he had saluted Augustus Caesar as Father of his country, Augustus was filled with so great joy at this title, that he said that in his whole life nothing could be more pleasing to him, and that he desired of God that he might keep this notable name even unto his death.

Moreover, the Duties of subjects toward the Magistrate are: I. To honour the Magistrate, that is, to look up to him, and to reverence him as the minister and ambassador of God, and to judge and speak of him candidly, and to take his errors, if they be not enormous, in the better part, and not with the common people to speak evil of the Magistrate, according to the commandment of God: *"Thou shalt not curse the ruler of thy people."* II. To obey his edicts in all things which are not against the word of God, and against the Law of nature; to give tributes, the ordinary payments, or, as Paul nameth it, ***φόρον*** (tribute), and custom, or ***τέλος***. III. To pray for the Magistrate, as Paul commandeth, to make *"supplications, prayers, intercessions, and giving of thanks for Kings, and for all that are in authority; that we may lead a quiet and peaceable life in all godliness and honesty,"* **1 Tim. 2** [?]. And especially in this our age Princes and commonwealths have need of the prayers and supplications of the godly. IIII. Not to break into the Magistrate's part, but to refer the cognizance of it to the Magistrate, if anything seem to pertain to the welfare of the Commonwealth.

<!-- p.158 -->

**Here there falleth in the Question concerning the bound (*meta*) of obedience: Whether the Magistrate is to be obeyed, when he commandeth anything against the word of God, and against the Law of nature.**

The answer is at hand: By no means is he to be obeyed. And the Rule is to be held: *"We ought to obey God rather than men."* Likewise: *"Render unto Caesar the things which are Caesar's, and unto God the things that are God's."* And memorable is the answer of Socrates, which is extant in **Plato**: ***ἐγὼ ὑμᾶς, ὦ ἄνδρες ἀθηναῖοι ἀσπάζομαι μὲν καὶ φίλω, πείσομαι δὲ θεῷ μᾶλλον ἢ ὑμῖν*** (I, O men of Athens, embrace you indeed and love you, but I will obey God rather than you). And Antigone in **Sophocles** saith: *"We must rather obey the Gods, with whom we shall have to abide the longer, than men, with whom we have to live but a very short time."* And the history of **Dan. 3**, teacheth that ungodly commandments are not to be obeyed. And in the *Phoenissae* of **Euripides** it is rightly said: *"Is it not always fitting to execute the commands of Princes? No, not if they be ungodly, unjust, and ill commanded."* And in the *Iphigenia*: *"We will obey the sons of Atreus when they command honest things; but if they shall command things dishonest, we will not obey."*

And seeing we read these things in the Heathen, we ought by all means to imitate them, and to accuse ourselves, if we are Christians, and are destitute of that mind zealous for piety.

But as touching immoderate pillagings, burdens are indeed to be patiently borne by subjects, if there be not added some atrocious wrong, which cannot be borne without sin, and in which defence against the Magistrate is granted; but nevertheless the Magistrate must look to what purpose he ought to use the sweat of his subjects, lest he consume the blood and sweat of his subjects upon pleasures and prodigalities, and upon superfluous buildings, as is wont everywhere to be done.

## *Officia Iudicum* · The Duties of Judges

I. Whether it be lawful for a Christian man to go to law in the court.

II. Wherefore Civil judgements were ordained.

III. What are the duties and the virtues of Judges.

<!-- p.159 -->

### *De Primo* · Of the First

The Magistrate is the ordinance of God, as Paul testifieth, **Rom. 13**. Therefore judgements also are approved by God. For without these the Magistrate is unprofitable. It is profitable to hold these things against the Anabaptistical and Antinomian ragings.

**Objection.**

> Scripture commandeth that we forgive, as often as any man shall have sinned against us.
> Therefore no man is to be accused for a wrong done.

**I answer.** A difference must be made between the thing itself (*res ipsa*) and the accident (*accidens*). The thing itself is to accuse, which of itself is not faulty. For the Gospel doth not abolish the civil ordinance. And Paul granteth unto Christians arbiters and judgements. But the accident is the affection that cleaveth thereto, the desire of revenge, hatred, obstinacy. These affections are forbidden in Scripture, that we should quiet and restrain them in ourselves, which is wont to be done out of the very fountain of charity in the godly, who know that the matter of accusation is one thing, and the abuse another. But the secure and the stubborn are ruled more by the accident than by the thing itself, as many examples everywhere in the courts do show. Now it is necessary to know that the thing itself is one thing, and the abuse another, for these four causes:

I. That we may know that judgements were not instituted by God in vain.

II. That we may know how much is lawful for us, lest we undertake anything with an evil conscience.

III. That we may be admonished not to defile by our own fault the remedy permitted us by God.

IIII. That the boldness of the wicked may be repressed with a pure and sincere zeal.

<!-- p.160 -->

### *De Secundo* · Of the Second

The final causes of civil judgements are four. Because:

- **I.** God willeth the just to be discerned from the unjust, that we may know what manner of God He Himself is: just, and a lover of virtue.
- **II.** That we may be put in mind of the judgement of God, which He declareth, because:
    - **I.** He hath sown in the minds of men the knowledge of good and evil, and the conscience of things honestly and ill done.
    - **II.** Atrocious offences are wonderfully punished with atrocious punishments in this life.
- **III.** That pleading in Court may be an exercise of discipline, and of many virtues in the Judge and in them that go to law: of truth, of obedience, of concord, etc.
- **IIII.** That the public peace may be preserved, controversies being composed, namely, by justice and the laws.

### *De Tertio* · Of the Third

First, let there be in the Judge the Fear of God, which is the Rule of all other virtues, and the beginning of wisdom.

Next, let there be diligence in the cognizance of the cause, and of all the circumstances of each party, not of one only, **Deut. 17**: *"When thou hearest, thou shalt enquire diligently."* **Phocylides**:

> ***μήτε δίκην δικάσεις, πρὶν ἀμφοῖν μῦθον ἀκούσῃς.***  
> Give thou no judgement, if both have not spoken.  

And by the Attic law the Judges sware ***ὁμοίως ἀμφοῖν ἀκροᾶσθαι***, that they would hear both parties alike. Hence **Plutarch** recordeth of Alexander that, as often as he heard an accuser, <!-- p.161 --> he stopped the one ear with his hand, signifying that he would reserve it whole for the accused. And **Seneca** saith: *"He that hath decreed aught, the other side being unheard, though he have decreed what is just, is himself not just."*
