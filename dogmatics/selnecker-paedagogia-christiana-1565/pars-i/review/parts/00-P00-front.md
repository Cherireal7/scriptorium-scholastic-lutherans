# Preface

*Praefatio in Paedagogiam Christianam*

<!-- p.3 -->

To the most illustrious and renowned Prince and Lord, the Lord GEORG FRIEDRICH, Margrave of Brandenburg, right noble Duke of Stettin and Pomerania, etc., and Burgrave of Nuremberg, his most gracious Lord: Grace and peace from our Lord JESUS CHRIST.

Whilst I am persuaded, renowned and most illustrious Prince, to suffer these lucubrations of mine to be sent forth abroad, there come into my mind the manifold kinds of men that will judge of this my labour. For first I fear lest that ***κακοηθὲς*** (ill habit, the itch of writing), at this day too common, be branded upon me ***παρὰ ταχυβούλων*** (by men hasty in their judgment); with which itch men being infected do think and meditate this and that, and ***ἀπο ταυτομάτου*** (offhand, as it cometh of itself) do write it, utter it, and at length publish it, labouring sometimes more with the disease of ambition, or of other affections, than with a zeal to advance and enlarge true doctrine and to serve the Church and the Schools. Hence it cometh that our age is truly called a scribbling age, and such in very deed as is painted in the verse:

> We write our poems everywhere, unlearned and learned alike.  

But if I had purposed to handle some argument of philosophizing concerning human matters, tongues and arts, which they call ***τοὺς ἔξωθεν λόγους, καὶ παιδείαν κατ' ἀνθρωπισμόν*** (the learning that is from without, and instruction after the manner of men), I would indeed confess <!-- p.4 --> that I am troubled, moved, and in some sort corrupted by that liberal ambition which is the ambition of well-nigh all Doctors and Writers. But because neither my study, nor my calling and office, nor yet either my person or my doctrine looketh, so to speak, unto human glorying and dignity; but hath a Divine matter, whereof the very form is neither of my wit nor of my strength, neither is it approved of all, nay rather of very few, in this not only injury of the times, but rather perfidy and ungodliness: I easily hope that I shall be delivered from the suspicion of that plague wherein ambition reigneth, whether it be honest and liberal, as they call it, or base. And this also I add: that it is indeed most true that there is extant a sufficient and abundant store of books concerning all things, and that many also are not only superfluous, but unprofitable and pernicious; yet concerning the Divine truth I hold that books enough can by no means be composed or spread abroad. For the divine Word is a fountain never drawn dry, and a wisdom infinite and eternal, and it can never in this life be fully handled, or at the least sufficiently understood, by our books, sermons, lectures and studies. And if all men, as many as this world ever bringeth forth, should bestow their labours upon the exposition of the Word of God, yet could they never either conceive or portray, much less deliver into our hands, a perfect knowledge of so much as one Article. And truly the greatest part of those things which we know is the least part of those which we know not. But of these things we treat more largely elsewhere, in their place. But if there be any who will not be content with this our answer, let them be so, and let them go on according to their own judgment. For it mattereth not much <!-- p.5 --> what every man judgeth, so that we think, speak and write the things that are true. And whereas of other, Philosophical books that is true which is wont to be said: *"A book that is to live must have a genius"*: much more is that true in the books which are composed concerning the Word and truth of God. And we know that hurtful books perish by little and little, and endure not long. Wherefore we commit this whole business unto God. ***Ἐὰν γὰρ ᾖ ἐξ ἀνθρώπων ἡ βουλὴ, ἢ τὸ ἔργον τοῦτο, καταλυθήσεται: εἰ δὲ ἐκ θεοῦ ἐστιν, οὐδεὶς δύναται καταλῦσαι αὐτό*** (*"For if this counsel or this work be of men, it will come to nought: but if it be of God, no man can overthrow it"*). Unto the Son of God also this our little prayer is, as is meet, ever directed:

> Without thee, O Christ our God, labour vanisheth into the air,  
> And the work sweateth in vain beneath its wretched burden.  
> Thou makest labours easy, thou thyself makest them happy:  
> So be thou present, O best Christ, with my studies.  
> While I live, grant good success and prosperous labours;  
> For if these profit nothing, what can life be?  
> And because neither strength of mind nor of body is at hand,  
> Be thou my Physician, according to thy wonted goodness.  
> Yet as in all things else, so here also thy righteous will  
> Be done: and rule me, O Christ, at thy beck.  

Next, another kind of men withstandeth us, which hath place in our own mind also, namely, that nothing is brought forth, said or written by us which hath not been before so unfolded, and so founded, builded and polished, that it needeth no more the props and endeavours of others, much less the increase and, as it were, the accession of our stammering. There are extant, say they, so many books of men excelling in the grace and gifts of God, who surely have passed over nothing that is profitable, necessary and wholesome to be known. What and how I should answer these men, I well-nigh have not. These two things only come <!-- p.6 --> to our aid. First, that if in those arts which after the Philosophical manner they name the humaner arts that saying of **Plato** is true: ***δὶς καὶ τρὶς τόγε καλῶς ἔχον ἐπανύω πολεῖν τῷ λόγῳ δεῖ*** (that which is well must be gone over again in discourse twice and thrice): much more is it certain that this is true in Divine things. For that can never be enough beaten in, nor anywhere enough repeated, which all men ought to know for the assurance of their salvation: and that the same things be set forth concerning the same matters, such as are good and true, as **Socrates** said, is godly and right. Secondly, that none of the Doctors, whether of the Church or of the Schools, either could or ought ever to have committed anything to writing, if he had been minded to write down only such things as were sprung of his own wit, seeing nothing can be said which hath not also been said and set forth before. For we have in the Church the haven and anchor of our Christian faith, the Prophetical and Apostolical books themselves. Out of these the Doctors of the Church receive whatsoever things pertain to doctrine and salvation, and these succeed one another in order, and are bound together among themselves as by a certain chain of words and sentences. And would to God there were many in this our age who would faithfully, diligently and constantly, both by voice and by writings, every man according to his gift and talent, whether great or mean, which he hath received of God, hand on unto posterity those things which they have received from their elders as it were from hand to hand (I mean the sound doctrine founded in the Prophetical and Apostolical writings); and that without new and strange devices, without a mind and desire of wrangling and brawling, and finally without the pride and overmuch ambition wherewith many of the Divines at this day are swollen. Thanks were to be rendered unto the true and merciful God, for that he himself hath mercifully paved, ordered and shewed us the way, <!-- p.7 --> raising up Doctors of the Church who have made the true doctrine clear by their writings, so that there is no need of searching out some new way wherein we ought to walk, whether in believing, or in speaking, or in living. And, to pass over for this time other Doctors of the Church of other times, God of his bounteous liberality hath kindled for us in these dregs and corners of the world two true lights of the Church, by whom he hath restored unto us anew the sound and true doctrine of his own essence and will, and hath lifted it up on high when it had been thrust out of doors by the Popish superstitions and idolatries. For how much those two men, the Magnificent **Luther** and the most humane **Philip**, have performed, the purer Church of the Son of God knoweth, and godly posterity will acknowledge, and the whole offspring of all men unto the last day. There is now extant, by the benefit of God, the divine Scripture exactly rendered both according to the letter and according to the spirit, and a true, learned, plain, large and methodical ***ἐξήγησις*** (exposition) of the same Scripture is set before the sight of all, so that nothing can befall, be thought, be asked, be said or written, which is not found wholly and clearly expounded in the writings of those two men, ***τῶν θεοσεβεστάτων, καὶ φιλαληθεστάτων*** (most godly, and most loving of the truth). Out of the writings and instruction of these men, therefore, this our labour also, such as it is, is builded up. And would to God that at the beginning, when I began to write these things, I had used greater diligence, that I might not only attain unto the true meaning of sound doctrine, but might also retain in all points the words wherein they uttered it. And this had been faithfully done by me, if I had ever had any thought at all of bringing this labour forth to light. But it shall be done, God helping, in other works hereafter, especially seeing I have taken in hand an exposition of the Psalms and of the new Testament, <!-- p.8 --> and have in some part finished it. Now these things I say for this cause, that it may be known upon whom I rest and in whose footsteps I tread. I beget no new things, I bring forth no things of yesterday; but that which **Paul** chargeth his Timothy, that he keep that which is committed to his trust, and avoid novelties absurd, unprofitable and full of labyrinths, which lead us away from faith and salvation, the same do I, according to my strength, God helping, endeavour to do; and I pray God that he would mercifully preserve me in this my simple affection and endeavour unto the last breath of my life, whatsoever others may judge of this my purpose.

The third kind of men that standeth in my way is of those who, taking account of age, do hold that solid and true doctrine concerning Divine things is to be committed unto old men, long since confirmed by experience and use of things, and that it is not to be granted that young men should take upon them to give sentence of those matters whereof their elders are sometimes afraid to speak: ***ἡ γὰρ σοφία ἐκ πολλῆς ἐμπειρίας τῷ μακρῷ χρόνῳ γίνεται*** (*"for wisdom cometh of much experience, in length of time"*), saith **Nazianzen**; ***οἵδε παῖδες ἐκ τρόχων πεπαυμένοι στείχουσι, μητρὸς οὐδὲν ἐννοούμενοι κακῶν. Νέα γὰρ φροντὶς οὐκ ἀλγεῖν φιλεῖ*** (*"Here come the children, having ceased from their running, and thinking nothing of their mother's ills. For a young mind loveth not to grieve"*), as it is in **Euripides**. Now this objection of many men is true and good. And would to God that the speech and authority of the aged held its own place at this day in Churches and Schools. For if we will judge truly, old age addeth much authority to the exposition of the greatest matters. And it most of all becometh the aged themselves to deliver unto the younger the doctrine of godliness, of righteousness, and of the other virtues. But as touching myself, who am daily occupied in the consideration of mine own infirmity, I will not for the present use the answer of **Isocrates**, which in the *Archidamus* he returned to them that objected the same thing against him; <!-- p.9 --> but this only I say, that I bring forth no new and youthful thoughts, speculations and disputations, but do faithfully set forth those things which first he himself, who is the Ancient of days, delivered, and which from him our forefathers, the Prophets and Apostles, and from these the other Doctors of the Church of God received, and commended unto us all, of what age soever we be. And seeing I expound the true judgments, faith and doctrine of godly old men, and know myself to be in the number of those who hearken unto them that teach and admonish aright (albeit the first kind of men, who of themselves are wise in all things, according to **Hesiod**, I willingly and deservedly yield unto others), no man shall any more be able rightly to expostulate with me concerning my age. For whether a man be a child, or a young man, or an old man, so he profess and hold fast the true doctrine of the Son of God, it is all one. For age doth not establish doctrine, but doctrine pertaineth unto every age. And that in the heavenly doctrine we all are, and are called, infants and sucklings, is manifest. And all men of all ages are to be admonished that they bestow their studies upon the learning and retaining of the true and simple doctrine, lest, which is to be feared, ***δοῦλοι ἀτόπων, καὶ ὑπερόπται τῶν ἀληθινῶν καὶ εἰωθότων*** (the slaves of things strange, and despisers of things true and accustomed), as **Thucydides** speaketh, do at some time, and that ever and anon, wax strong; especially seeing our times are such as are described in the little verses:

> Nothing is so blasphemous but it can find its patrons,  
> And render the author of it honourable.  

And surely it is to be feared lest we slide back by little and little into the former barbarism and ungodliness, seeing our age bringeth forth ***Θεολόγους μὲν γέροντας, καὶ πρεσβυτέρους ὀλιγίστους,*** <!-- p.10 --> ***φιλοδόξους δὲ νεοφύτους καὶ φιλοχρημάτους παμπληθεῖς*** (of Divines that are old men, and of elders, very few; but of novices that love glory and love money, a very great multitude).

But I cease ***δύσφημα γράφειν*** (to write things of ill omen), and I judge that answer hath been made enough and more than enough unto the chief objections. For I neither will nor can put away all the bitings of venomous censors. And a fortune common to me with many others must be patiently borne of me, insomuch that I often think with myself: seeing **Luther**, **Philip**, **Brenz**, **Schnepf** (to say nothing now of others), men who deserved most excellently of the Church, could not even after they were turned to ashes, neither yet can, escape the slanders not of adversaries only, but also of their own, who would forsooth be so accounted; much less can I, and such as I am, who with our voice and writings do but stammer in comparison of the labours which those men performed, be ever free from slanders, whether open or secret. But what then? Let the truth be and abide with you, CHRIST helping. Let the judgments and deliberations of others be what they will, whether just or unjust. Let this be our sure rule, which is reported to have been that of Doctor **Wenceslaus Linck**, of godly memory: *"If thy doctrine, which thou professest, cannot be blamed out of the Word of God, and thy life cannot be reproved as defiled with enormous vices, be of good cheer, and fear not even the very Devils, but go on in thy calling, fear God, and serve the Church of God."* Let these men and those now judge, let them fret and be wroth, let them slander, and let them slay us; of all this ***οὐδὲ γρῦ*** (not so much as a jot) is to be made, so that we war a good warfare, holding faith and a good conscience, that we may one day be able to render unto the Son of GOD an account of our doctrine, life, calling and faith. But let these things also suffice concerning these matters for this time, <!-- p.11 --> which elsewhere peradventure, when need shall be, we shall set forth in more words.

Now somewhat must be said of this my Book, whereunto I have prefixed the title of the Christian Pedagogy, or *Paedia*. I began to write it in the year 61, not with this mind, that I should at any time bring it forth to light; but, seeing that according to my calling the Christian Catechesis was to be set before the hearers by way of sermon in the Court of the most Illustrious Prince and Lord, the Lord Augustus, Duke of Saxony, and Elector, etc., my most Gracious Lord, that I might have in readiness certain heads wherein the hearers should be instructed. And that this was my purpose, and none other, God knoweth, ***ὁ καρδιογνώστης*** (the knower of hearts). Hence also the kind of speech is somewhat more lowly and humble than that it can safely undergo the hazard of publication; albeit it is neither obscure nor altogether mean, but such as may be used in an ***αὐτοσχεδιάσματι*** (a thing made offhand), and, so to speak, in an extemporary writing. But when at times I shewed unto learned and good men the things which I had hastily poured out, and lent them to such as asked, these began to put the spur to me, and, rehearsing I know not what profits to the Church, to exhort me that I would finish the labour begun.

In the name therefore, and with invocation, of the Son of GOD I went forward; and amid my occupations, which seldom suffer me to have leisure for letters and to enjoy a liberal ease, in hours as it were stolen and sought out I did as much as I could, God helping; and although I was never in strength of body while I was writing, as is known unto God and to my friends, yet I performed what I could, commending the success unto God. But I truly both can and will glory in God ***ἀναντιῤῥήτως*** (without gainsaying), <!-- p.12 --> that I have written things true and necessary, leaving aside ***παρέργοις*** (things beside the purpose), especially in this wretched and troubled state of the Church. I defend no errors, I beget no new opinions, but I follow the Lamp of my feet, the Word of God, and with **Ambrose** I say: *"All new things, which Christ hath not taught, we rightly condemn, because unto the faithful Christ is the way."* I pray also the Son of God, our Lord Jesus Christ, that he would sanctify and preserve me and us all in his truth, that is, in his Word, especially seeing all things in the Church and elsewhere do threaten manifold corruptions, ruin and destruction. And surely I had rather not only be driven hither and thither as an exile and a wanderer, and bear about the mark either of faithless Cain or of Orestes, and undergo, as **Euripides** nameth them, ***φόνια πειρατήρια*** (trials of blood), but also be taken away out of the world by any torments whatsoever, as a ***κάθαρμα*** (an offscouring), than be accused and convicted of manifest error against conscience in the propagation of the heavenly doctrine. ***ἴστωρ ὁ θεὸς τούτων*** (God is witness of these things). For how great a matter it is, that we shall one day, every man in his place, all and each of us, before the throne of the Trinity and of the Divine Majesty, in the sight of all Angels and men, render unto the Son of God an account of our doctrine, faith and constancy: this, not secure minds, not worldly, not Epicurean, not wavering and uncertain, but godly minds do consider. With this mind therefore, of setting forth the true doctrine, and, Christ helping, of propagating it, I have hitherto lived in my calling, having swallowed down many adversities, wherein the Son of God hath exercised me, unequal to them and insufficient, and hath sustained me even unto this day. And so I have at length brought this Pedagogical book unto an end, although by reason of a state of health too contrary unto me, <!-- p.13 --> and of ***καχεξίαν*** (an ill habit of body), I was oftentimes constrained to cast aside and to suspend the labour, insomuch that I was fain also to dictate some things unto others, as unto amanuenses, and to transcribe some things from elsewhere. And so far as was required for these lucubrations, I have obeyed the old precept: ***ὥσπερ τὴν μέλιτταν ὁρῶ ἐφ' ἅπαντα μὲν τὰ βλαστήματα καθιζάνουσαν, ἑκάστου δὲ τὰ χρήσιμα λαμβάνουσαν: οὕτω χρὴ καὶ τοὺς παιδείας ὀρεγομένους, μηδενὸς μὲν ἀπείρως ἔχειν, πανταχόθεν δὲ τὰ χρήσιμα συλλέγειν*** (as I see the bee settling upon all the blossoms, but taking from each that which is profitable: so ought they also that reach after learning to be unacquainted with nothing, but to gather from every side the things that are profitable). And, to add this also, as touching the ***ἐξήγησιν*** (exposition) of the Lord's Prayer, I was minded to insert it into this Work, being written by me not lately, but while I yet abode at Wittenberg. And the doctrine of the Sacraments I hope is clearly and truly set forth. And whereas the heresy ***τῶν μυστηριομάχων*** (of them that fight against the mysteries), born not of the word of Christ but of the brain of man (as all the godly must needs confess), hath at this day everywhere so many and so great patrons, that scarce any man is accounted learned and godly, or a lover of concord and peace, unless he be mad together with these men, and be wise above Christ, and take away from the Son of God and of man his omnipotence and truth, and be in the whole of religion ***ἄπλευρος*** (without ribs, that is, without any firmness)[^P00-1] and uncertain: I began to draw up a short and true exposition of the sound doctrine concerning the Lord's Supper, which I trust will be welcome unto the godly. For it leaneth not upon human favour, or authority, or learning, but hath its foundation in these words: *"Thus saith the Lord."* I had moreover inserted an exposition of the Creeds, and of the whole History, both of the Nativity and of the Passion of Christ, gathered out of the Evangelists, and of the fifty-third chapter of Isaiah; but because the book was otherwise growing to a full bigness, I took it out again, and left well-nigh only the half part of this Pedagogy, <!-- p.14 --> meaning to reserve the rest unto another and more convenient time, that is, unto the books of the Institutions, and unto the explication of the Sunday Gospels: lest, if this book were too long, that should befall me which **Herodotus** reporteth to have befallen certain Ambassadors, unto whom, when they rehearsed things at too great length and dwelt long upon their oration, ***οἱ ἄρχοντες*** (the rulers) had answered: ***τὰ μὲν πρῶτα λεχθέντα ἐπιλελῆσθαι, τὰ δὲ ὕστατα οὐ συνιέναι*** (that the things first spoken they had forgotten, and the last they understood not).

Thus then these lucubrations of mine do now go forth abroad, to undergo manifold judgments, which yet are free unto every man, so that candour and godliness be used therein. I know that I am by nature fearful, and not fit for great matters. Yet here I profess myself to be a servant and soldier of JESUS CHRIST, whose strength is made perfect even in the weak; and I hope, if GOD shall grant me life, that I shall go forward in this my course begun, howsoever the lot may fall. And truly I detest all those Ecebolians[^P00-2] who ***πᾶν προσποιητὸν ἔχουσιν*** (have all things feigned), and who bend and bend back again even their faith and doctrine unto every wind, yea even when matters are quiet. But the judgments of good men I flee not, but do desire and seek them, God being witness. Let the worldly now murmur, let the Philosophers wrangle, and let the pettifoggers come together, and the scoffers bring in their sentence beforehand; let the wise snarl[^P00-3] and burst, let them rail; and let the nice-nosed cast away our labour at the first sight, and let the rest by all means condemn the same: we must bear the injury and iniquity of the times, so that we approve our service unto Christ.

We know, and in the short space of our age have often found by experience, that the kinds of men who judge of other men's <!-- p.15 --> endeavours and labours are about four. For first there are certain wits of right judgment, which are wont to judge of things rightly considered, and do praise the things that are good, and such things as are said or done less properly and fitly they amend by their gentleness and ***ἐπιεικείᾳ*** (equity), and do mildly interpret unto the better part all things that are not altogether absurd and enormous. In this kind are for the most part good men, who seek the glory of God and make great account of Christian peace, and are soundly learned; and in the praise of so great humanity D. **Philip**, of godly memory, did excel.

Secondly there are certain wits of hasty and hurried judgment, which, the matter being scarce heard, much less considered, but with a running tongue, do take unto themselves the judgment and right of determining all things, and lightly call this good and that evil, whereas the matter standeth far otherwise, and their own judgments do shew that they have not so much as touched and tasted with the tip of their lips the things whereof they judge. In this kind are the half-learned and the rude at this day, of whom it is said:

> ***Εἰ πᾶσιν οὐκ ἔξεστι διαρτίζειν καλῶς,***  
> ***Θέλομεν ὅμως πάντες κρίνειν πάντων πέρι.***  
> (Though it is not given unto all to frame a thing well,  
> Yet will we all be judging of all things.)  

**Plutarch** relateth that, when Aristides was being banished by the *ostracon* used among the Athenians, that is, by voting-shells given man by man in the assembly, certain of the common people, men altogether rustical, held out their shell unto Aristides, who by chance met them (of whom nevertheless they knew not whether he were Aristides), and bade him that he also should condemn Aristides. And when Aristides marvelled at the zeal of the multitude that was stirred up against him, he asked whether they had suffered any evil at all <!-- p.16 --> from him whom they had determined to send into banishment. Unto whom they said that no evil had been done them, neither was the man known unto them; but that they took it grievously that in all places they heard this same man called just and good, and that therefore they also added their voting-shell. Such, forsooth, are the judgments of the rude and of the common sort, of whom it is said: ***λαῷ μὴ πίστευε, πολύτροπός ἐστιν ὅμιλος*** (trust not the people; the multitude is of many turnings). And there is an old rule: ***ὄχλος σὺν ἀμαθείᾳ μέγιστον κακόν*** (a multitude joined with ignorance is the greatest evil).

Next there are those who openly slander even things well said and well done, and neither admit nor approve anything save that which they themselves do, and ever and anon watch for occasions of carping at this and that. These men in the Schools they call Lucianic wits, and, from the Scourge of Homer (*Homeromastix*), Zoiluses.

Last of all there are certain wits that resemble Thersites in their manners, hypocritical, proud, and silently envious, not openly slanderous, but such as by reason of some perverse affection do lightly and by stealth find fault with most things, and accuse the good endeavours of good men under the name either of ambition and pride, or of covetousness, or of other blemishes, or else neglect them as petty things, make light of them behind the back, dispraise them, and by what means soever they can keep them under. And this kind hath place at this day even among acquaintance, ***καὶ συλλειτούργους*** (and fellow-ministers), everywhere in Churches and in Schools, ***οἱ περὶ τοῦ πλησίον αὐτοῦ λαλοῦσιν εἰρωνικὰ καὶ αὐλικὰ, ἐν δὲ τῇ καρδίᾳ ἔχουσι τὴν ἔχθραν, εἴτ' οὖν κακίαν*** (who speak concerning their neighbour things dissembling and courtly, but in their heart have enmity, or rather mischief), according to the Prophet.

But how pestilent and contagious an evil in Churches and Commonwealths is this study of sophistry, stuffed full of ambitious contentions and secret hatreds, sad experience doth testify at this day, with the utmost loss of all things. And most true are the verses:

<!-- p.17 -->

> ***ὅπου σοφισμάτων γέμουσ' οἱ ἐν τέλει,***  
> ***Ἔχθρας τ' ἰδίας ποιοῦσι πλείονος πέρι,***  
> ***Ἢ τὴν τεκοῦσαν πατρίδα, ἢ δὲ τὸν θεὸν,***  
> ***Ταύτην νόμιζε τὴν πόλιν χρόνῳ ποτὲ***  
> ***Ἐξ οὐρίων δραμοῦσαν εἰς βυθὸν πεσεῖν.***  
> (Where they that are in authority are full of sophistries,  
> And make more account of their private enmities  
> Than of the country that bare them, or of God,  
> Reckon thou that that city, in time, at the last,  
> Though it hath run before fair winds, shall fall into the deep.)  

These then are the kinds of men who at this day do judge of the affairs and studies of others. But, lest we be too long in rehearsing these things, we heartily desire to undergo the judgments of good men; and good men, lovers of the simple truth, we ask and beseech again and again that they would pardon a godly boldness, which we hope shall profit the Church of Christ. Unto the rest we answer, that this our labour is thrust upon no man; and therefore they who shall not be content therewith may let it alone and cast it away, or may themselves write and publish better things, if only they shall be able: albeit the greatest part of them, that I may use the words of the Comic poet, doth verily bring it to pass by understanding, that it understandeth well-nigh nothing. We confess and acknowledge our own slenderness, and we are oftentimes dry and short in great matters, insomuch that we scarce persuade ourselves that this our labour, as touching the form and the working of it, can be less approved of any man, even of the envious, than of our own selves: yet nevertheless we serve the Church of Christ in this our stammering, and, Christ helping, we shall serve it after this manner, whereof a draught is here exhibited by us, so long as the Son of GOD shall grant us the breath of life. And seeing the thread of our life is slender enough, and too weak, and without doubt not long, we shall bestow whatsoever there is of it upon the propagation of the true doctrine, lest, that short thread being broken <!-- p.18 --> off, we appear before the judgment seat of Christ altogether naked, delicate and idle; but that we may be seen to have laid out some labour upon the vineyard of Christ, according to the gift which he himself hath given us. That the Son of God would increase and preserve this mind in me, and would stir up all that are studious of godliness unto an earnest study of the true doctrine, I pray fervently with my whole heart. For if ever there was need of watchful and cheerful study in learning the fountains of sound religion, surely in these dregs of the world there is need. For Satan will not rest, seeing the day of judgment is at the doors, which the godly look for, and daily with groaning long after.

Now I have dedicated this Christian Pedagogy unto Your most illustrious Highness, O best of Princes, both because it is the custom in the Church that such writings as seek the glory of God be offered for many causes unto the great estates; and because I handled the chief part of this Pedagogy by way of sermon in the Court of the renowned Elector Augustus, Your Highness's uncle, for whose name's sake I have no cause to doubt that this Work will be welcome unto Your Highness; and also because the knowledge and propagation of the true doctrine pertaineth unto the August office of Your Highness, as it is said in the verse:

> The greatest virtue of a Prince is to know God.  

Lastly, seeing the benefits of Your most illustrious Highness toward me are great and many, I thought that a declaration of a thankful mind would not be unwelcome unto Your Highness; which that Your Highness would look upon and receive with a gracious countenance and mind, I humbly ask.

And seeing this *Paedia* is now the fifth time put forth abroad, <!-- p.19 --> I was minded to revise it for the last time, and, being revised, to consecrate and offer it anew unto Your Highness. And those who deem this *Paedia* worthy the reading, and the studious, I admonish that they join unto this *Paedia* my Institutions and Catechetical books, wherein I have expounded certain things more largely.

I pray the Son of God, our Lord Jesus Christ, that he would rule and defend Your Highness, and bestow upon you a calm and quiet government, and preserve unto himself in these Regions a company of them that teach and that learn the true doctrine of the essence and will of God. AMEN. At Leipzig, in the month of August, 1576.[^P00-4]

Your most illustrious Highness's

most devoted servant,

NICOLAUS SELNECCER.

# *In Paedagogiam Christianam Georgius Fabricius ad Lectorem* · Georg Fabricius to the Reader, upon the Christian Pedagogy

> Thus did great Nathan teach great Solomon,  
> While he fashioned the tender heart of his King.  
> These precepts Jacob also gave unto that son for whom  
> His hard brethren made ready bonds, but God a sceptre.  
> Jehoiada the priest sang things not diverse from these,  
> Under whose teaching the safety of the king stood sure.  
> Such things doth Selneccer now deliver in these books,  
> While with a divine art he instructeth godly minds.  
> Learn, O child, the things that must be learned in the first years,  
> And the things that profit at every season, learn, O old man;  
> And lest a pathless way draw thee asunder into divers sects,  
> Cherish these doctrines with a mind not unmindful.  

## *Κλήμεντος Ἀλεξανδ. Παιδαγ. α.* · Clement of Alexandria, *The Instructor*, Book I

***Παιδαγωγία ἐστὶ θεοσέβεια, μάθησις οὖσα θεοῦ θεραπείας, καὶ παίδευσις εἰς ἐπίγνωσιν ἀληθείας, ἀγωγή τε ὀρθὴ, ἀνάγουσα εἰς οὐρανόν.***

**Of Clement of Alexandria, Book I.**

Pedagogy is divine religion, which is the doctrine of the divine worship, and an instruction unto the attaining of the acknowledgment of the truth, and a right training and manner of leading the life, which bringeth unto heaven, etc.
