**II.** To think well of others upon probable reason, **1 Cor. 13**: *"Charity believeth all things,"* that is, it thinketh well of men, it is candid, not suspicious, so far forth as there be no proofs to the contrary. For every suspicious man is malignant and a deceiver. **III.** To bend doubtful sayings and doings to the better part, without calumny and detraction.

**Taciturnity** (*Taciturnitas*) is the moderate use of the tongue, that we speak neither more nor less than is needful, when there is need. Either we must speak when it is necessary, or of those things which we know.

> In all else it hath hurt no man to have held his peace; it hurteth to have spoken.  

Today talkativeness doth almost everywhere bear away the palm. We judge of all things, even of those which are above us, and which we have never so much as touched with the tip of our lips.

Of loquacity many things have been said: Empty vessels sound most, full vessels sound little. **Euripides**: ***ἀχαλίνων στομάτων τέλος ἡ δυστυχία*** (the end of unbridled mouths is misfortune). And of Tantalus: ***ἀκόλαστον ἔσχε γλῶσσαν, αἰσχίστην νόσον*** (he had an unchastened tongue, a most shameful disease). It is better to offend by too much ***ἐχεμυθίᾳ*** (reserve of speech) and taciturnity than by loquacity.

**Courtesy** (*Comitas*) is the declaring of goodwill toward others in speaking, hearing and answering. As **Augustus** was courteous; **Alexander Severus** debarred no man from speech with him, and visited his friends when they were sick, even those of lower degree; **Trajan**, when his friends blamed him that he made himself too common toward all men, answered: *"I am such an Emperor toward private men, as I, being a private man, would have wished an Emperor to be toward me."*[^P15-1] And this virtue is in Princes a notable one, but exceeding rare.

**Urbanity** (*Urbanitas*), ***εὐτραπελία***, is the ornament of truth, without scurrility and without dulness, in sayings wittily spoken, seasonable and honest, whereby others are admonished, soothed, or even, in due place, provoked. As when **Alexander the Great** was minded to sack Lampsacus, and **Anaximenes** his Master, willing to make intercession <!-- p.302 --> for the city, before he began to speak to Alexander, received this answer: *"By Jupiter, I will not do that which thou art about to ask."* Then Anaximenes: *"I ask that thou destroy Lampsacus."* Thus he withdrew from destruction a town renowned for its ancient nobility, and appointed to ruin.

### *Quae res hodie potissimum omnem veritatem, pacem, et iustitiam evertunt?* · What Things Do Chiefly at This Day Overthrow All Truth, Peace and Justice?

**I.** The licence of sinning, as well in the Magistrate as in the common people.

**II.** Dissembling (*Dissimulatio*), whereby they who ought to punish wickedness are too indulgent and wink at it, both of the Magistrate and of the Teachers of the word of God. For in our sins we either cover or hide the wickedness, or we seek pretexts, pleading for our own affections, or we resist faithful admonitions, even as the adders stop their ears to the voice of the charmers, or we run altogether boldly and bareheaded to evils of every kind.

**III.** Patronage and Defence. Nothing can at this day be feigned, or wickedly said and committed, for which defenders and patrons are not found, if only there be money at hand wherewith the hands may be greased, or some favour, etc.

The Eternal God, the Father of our Lord Jesus Christ, be graciously present with us, and sanctify us by His truth, that we may be one in Him.

# *Nonum et Decimum Praeceptum* · The Ninth and Tenth Commandment

We shall join together the Ninth and the Tenth Commandment. And we have admonished above that there is to be no anxious disputing concerning the order of the Commandments, in the distinguishing whereof neither the ancient nor <!-- p.303 --> the recent Divines agree. Some make four Commandments of the first Table and six of the second, as **Nazianzen** and **Ambrose** do. Others otherwise. We retain the received judgement of the Churches, seeing that this matter hath in it nothing inconvenient, and seeing that it is evident from **Deuteronomy, chap. 4 and 10**, that these Commandments are ten in number, as Moses saith: *"I declared unto you the covenant of God with you, which He commanded you in His ten words, which He wrote upon two Tables, etc."*

Concerning the reason of the order also it hath been said above, in the beginning of the fifth Commandment. The First Commandment treateth of the acknowledgment (*agnitio*) of God; the Second, of the profession; the Third, of the preserving of that acknowledgment and profession. Then, in the second Table, the Fourth establisheth the political order and human society; the Fifth fenceth our bodies and life; the Sixth fenceth marriage and chastity; the Seventh fenceth our poor cottages and our substance; the Eighth fenceth judgements and contracts; the Ninth and Tenth are the interpretation of all the foregoing Commandments, and testify that in the Decalogue not only outward offences are forbidden, but inward vices also. This order is most apt. For to the establishing of a wholesome and honest life there is required, first of all, true doctrine and religion concerning God; next, a peaceable commonwealth or society. And these two are comprehended in the first four commandments. But that both of these may be retained, there must needs be at hand a certain fence, whereby our persons, our chastity and honesty, our goods and substance, and our fame or honest name may be guarded; for without these neither religion nor commonwealth can stand. These things are contained in the fifth, sixth, seventh and eighth Commandment. Now are added the Ninth and Tenth, as the end and sum of those that go before; of which the former pertaineth to the genus, the tenth to the species. For the Ninth, under the name of *house*, willeth that we hurt not the household and possession of our neighbour; the Tenth, that no man upon any occasion, under an honest show, entice unto himself the things of another. Some refer the Ninth to that which containeth, <!-- p.304 --> namely to the house; but the tenth to the things contained in the house and possession. Now these two Commandments make the difference between human Laws and divine: whereof the former forbid deeds and words only, according to the Rule: *"No man suffereth punishment for a thought"*; but the latter restrain the inward ***ἀταξίαν*** (disorder), or the inordinate concupiscence of all the powers of man.

In the foregoing Commandments also concupiscence is indeed forbidden; but that concupiscence which is the effect of this concupiscence which is forbidden in the ninth and tenth Commandment. For in the other Commandments voluntary concupiscence, that is, inward and outward action, counsels and deliberations, are forbidden; but here the fountain and source of all those things is stopped up. There the active, here the passive concupiscence is interdicted, and all the powers in the whole nature are comprehended, as it is said in **Deut. 6**: *"Thou shalt love the Lord thy God"* (this is the proposition, laid down after the manner of Logic, which the ***μερισμός***, or distribution, now followeth) *"with all thy soul"* (that is, with the natural actions, meat, drink, sleep, etc., that they may look unto God, and may arise out of a true burning of love toward God), *"and with all thine heart"* (that is, let all counsels be governed out of that burning of love toward God), *"and with all thy might,"* that is, with all the powers of the whole nature.

In this place let the saying of **Rom. 8** be observed: ***φρόνημα σαρκός***, the affection of the flesh, that is, all the motions and affections in the will and heart, the notions, inclinations, appetites, are enmity against God. The whole nature is an enemy to God, and God is angry with it. This is a thing dreadful to be thought and spoken. Likewise **Jerem. 17**: The heart, that is, the whole lump, the whole ***πλάσμα*** (frame) of the knowledge that knoweth and desireth, is perverse, turned away from God, despising the divine promises and threatenings, and abusing all the works of God; and it is wretched, without light and consolation, full of doubtings and sorrows, and at the last rushing headlong into everlasting sadness.

<!-- p.305 -->

But let the appetites that are in nature as it was created — as the appetite of meat, drink, marriage, ***στοργή*** (natural affection), etc. — be distinguished from inordinate concupiscence. For Samson sinneth not when he desireth Delilah to wife, etc.

It pleaseth me in this place, for the sake of Students, to repeat some few things, fetched somewhat higher, and yet more briefly.

I. Of the perfection and dignity of man before the fall.

II. Of the fall, and the causes thereof.

III. Of the effects of the fall of the first man, and of original sin.

IIII. Of evil concupiscence.

V. Of the will and free will after the fall.

VI. Of the works of unbelievers.

### *De Primo* · Of the First

What manner of perfection man had, this one thing showeth: that man was created after the image and likeness of God; which, namely, was nothing else than a conformity, perfect in all ways and in every point, with the rule of righteousness revealed unto us in the Law of God; so that, to wit, in the whole mind, the whole will, the whole heart, the whole conscience, and in all the members of the whole body and the powers of the soul, there were strengths most entire, and directed only unto the acknowledgment, love and celebration of God, and unto the love and profit of the neighbour, according to the voice of the divine Law: *"Thou shalt love the Lord thy God with all thy heart, with all thy soul, with all thy mind, and with all thy strength, and thy neighbour as thyself."* Here was no darkness, no ***ἀταξία*** (disorder), no rebellion, no strife of reason and will, no corruption at all, nor stubbornness, were it but in the least particle; but all things were most perfect and most holy. First, all the senses, both inward and outward, were most effectual, and by nature most ready to perform every duty without trouble. Secondly, there was no hindrance, <!-- p.306 --> no hurt, no stay, whereby both soul and body and all the senses should the less retain their perfection. For man was placed by God in the garden of Eden, which word, by an allusion of the letters to ***ἡδονὴν*** (pleasure), denoteth both pleasure and all things sweet, and necessary and profitable to human nature; and man sustained himself by the tree of life, so that nothing was wanting to him for living happily, blessedly and sweetly, without any sin. Thirdly, there flourished in man a most full and most absolute knowledge, as well of God as of himself, and of all creatures, substances and accidents; man knew all the specific differences, virtues and powers of all things, and gave unto all names most agreeable to the properties of the several things. Fourthly, man had liberty and an upright choice (*arbitrium rectum*), whereby he could have persevered in good, and would have desired only the things profitable and agreeable to his nature, as well in the natural as in the sensitive, and also in the voluntary appetite, and would have shunned all things contrary and hurtful. Fifthly, he not only had all natural things entire and perfect, but was also endowed by God with supernatural good things, that is, with the gifts of the Holy Spirit; whom if he had obeyed, he had not fallen headlong into so great a sea of evils. Sixthly, original righteousness ruled, adorned, fenced, led and preserved the whole man, mind, will, heart, conscience, body, members, life and all actions, inward and outward. Seventhly, all things which man thought, purposed, went about, said or did, were holy, true, sincere, right, sweet and profitable, acceptable to God, and wholesome to the man himself and to his neighbour; and all things turned to the glory of God and the profit of the neighbour.

### *De Secundo* · Of the Second

How foully, shamefully, lightly and horribly man sinned against the commandment of God, and was cast down from the highest top of his original majesty, the Mosaic history testifieth, **Genes. 3**, of which we must speak in its own place. Now the causes <!-- p.307 --> of that fall are these: First, the Devil himself, a liar and a murderer from the beginning, through whose envy death entered into the world; and who, having craftily set upon the woman by the serpent, whose body he entered, did under a plausible show deprave the commandment of God and treacherously maim it, and charged God with lying and with envy. Secondly, man himself, who of his own free will, without any compulsion ***καὶ ὑπὲρ μόρον*** (and beyond any fated necessity), gave assent to the suggestions, flatteries and lies of the Devil, the Commandment of God being blotted out and neglected.

### *De Tertio* · Of the Third

Upon the fall of our first parents followed original sin, and whatsoever evil flowed out of it, as well in the soul as in the body, and in the whole life, from that fall even unto the end of the world: such as are the depravation of the whole nature, the loss of the divine light in the mind, horrible darkness, the turning away of the will from God, and the enmity of the flesh against God, in the heart the stubbornness of the affections, and in all the powers a dreadful ***ἀταξία*** (disorder) and corruption, chiefly as touching things divine and spiritual. Likewise: the weakness of the senses and of all the powers of the mind, diseases, torments, miseries, misfortunes, penalties, punishments, death. Likewise: unhappy actions, human purposes and counsels without success, reproach and dishonour, and whatsoever evil besides, either in nature or without it, can be thought or spoken. Neither is there any man who (the Son of God alone excepted, ***τῷ θεανθρώπῳ***, the God-man) can exempt himself therefrom, whether they be Patriarchs, Prophets, or the mother of the Lord herself, or Apostles, or others. All are concluded under sin, as David saith: *"I was shapen in iniquity, and in sins"* ***ἐκήσισε***[?] *"did my mother warm me,"* to wit, with that contagion which she received from her very birth. And Paul testifieth that we are by nature the children of wrath; whose *Jezer*,[^P15-2] namely, as Moses speaketh, or ***φρόνημα***, as Paul interpreteth it, that is, the whole lump, both substantial and <!-- p.308 --> accidental, is depraved and most miserably defiled with the poison of the Devil, so that God can no more find in us anything so entire and pure as He Himself had created it; but as often as He looketh upon us, He pronounceth us a ***βδέλυγμα*** (abomination) and as it were a rotten carcase, and a lump most worthy to be cast away and abhorred; seeing He beholdeth that our understanding lacketh the first light and the true acknowledgment of God, and that the will is not directed unto God, but turned away from Him, and that the heart swarmeth with impure affections; and that even the purer affections, which haply seem to remain unto us — such as are the love of God the creator, though unknown, ***στοργαὶ*** (natural affections) and the like — are manifoldly defiled, and, as often as they ought to look unto God, do sin in defect, and, as often as they are directed unto the creatures, do in excess overleap the bounds beyond measure; and, to speak briefly, that the whole nature, all the notions, the soul, the mind, the will, the heart, the members, the life and actions, and all things which are in man, are infected with the poison of the Devil, or original sin, out of which afterward arise manifold other errors, and sins without number. For not the bare accidents only in man, the qualities and ornaments, are depraved; but sin hath so invaded and possessed the nature or substance of man, that it may rightly be called an essential sin, or one that is of the essence of man: not that sin is the substance or essence of man (for to say that is contumelious against God, the Creator and Preserver of all substances, and is of itself false), but because with that leaven and deep poison of the Devil, as with a leprosy and a hidden wasting, the whole nature of man in soul and body is infected, and is propagated from man to man, *"from the sole of the foot even unto the crown of the head,"* as **Bernard** speaketh.

Original sin, therefore, is the corruption of our whole nature, whereby we are all constituted sinners and guilty of damnation, both as touching the soul, and as touching the body, and as touching all the powers and strengths, from our birth, and thereafter also are ever prone to sinning, neither do we at any time satisfy the Law of God.

<!-- p.309 -->

In the Schools, for teaching's sake, we use in the place of the genus the words *defect*, *inclination*, *impotency* and *action* — not of a light defect or disease, but of one too impotent unto good, and potent unto evil. These words are afterward more largely unfolded, when the subjects are taken up in order: the mind, the will, and the heart. The mind is vitiated with horrible darkness and ignorance, or blindness, and with the want of the wisdom of God, and without God it can do nothing else but err in things divine and spiritual, and is plainly blind and foolish, unless it be enlightened anew and taught by God. The will is turned away from God, and fighteth against the will of God, unless it be directed by God through the word and the Holy Spirit. The heart is full of an infinite confusion of appetites, as of a hellish beast, which with most flagitious violence hurrieth man away unto evil.

That these things are true, most evident testimonies do witness, as:

**Genes. 8**: *"The imagination of man's heart is evil from his youth."*

**Psalm 51**: *"In sin did my mother conceive me."*

**Psalm 14**, **Roman. 3**: *"There is none that doeth good, no, not one."*

**Job 14**: *"Who can bring a clean thing out of an unclean? not one."*

**Proverb. 20**: *"Who can say, My heart is clean, I am pure from sin?"*

**Rom. 5**: *"By the offence of one the evil was propagated upon all men to condemnation."*

**Rom. 8**: *"The* ***φρόνημα*** *of the flesh is enmity against God, and is not subject to the Law of God, neither indeed can be."*

**Ephes. 2**: *"We were by nature the children of wrath,"* that is, inasmuch as we are born, we are guilty of eternal damnation.

**1 Corinth. 2**: *"The natural man receiveth not the things of the Spirit of God,"* that is, man, so far forth as he liveth the natural life, and enjoyeth natural reason and sense, without the Holy Spirit, is so far wholly wrapped in thickest darkness, and neither acknowledgeth nor feareth God, nor understandeth the word of God, nor believeth in God.

<!-- p.310 -->

Now although these and the like things, whereby the whole man is accused, and is by the thunderbolt of the divine curse laid prostrate, seem exceeding hard, and bitter and violent to human reason — even as the **Pelagians**, and the Scholastic doctors of old, and at this day the Monks and the Papists, take it ill that this evil, which is never enough amplified, should be amplified — yet let us believe God accusing us,[^P15-4] and looking into us, who and what manner of men we are, and exactly considering our nature with far other eyes and another judgement than we wretched men in this blindness are wont; as it is written, **Psalm 90**: *"Thou settest our secret thing in the light of Thy countenance."* Let us therefore willingly acknowledge our own infirmity, and in our whole life bear ourselves humbly, and cast ourselves down before God, and of our own accord and freely confess, without any delay or going about, that we have deserved eternal damnation; and if we receive any good, or obtain the mercy of God, let us know that this befalleth us by the free and spontaneous goodness of God alone, and let us render unto God His glory, and give Him thanks for the benefits which He largely bestoweth upon us, defiled as we are, and guilty of wrath and eternal damnation.

But one thing remaineth, whereof Students are to be admonished, lest we dote with the **Manichees**. Nature, of itself created by God, and even now sustained and preserved, is one thing; the sin that dwelleth in, or cleaveth to, nature is another. God created nature; sin He created not. Wherefore if we consider the nature of man such as it was before the fall, then is the highest perfection, integrity and dignity to be ascribed to it, as we said above. But if we consider it also after the fall, we rightly say that it is vitiated, and that sin dwelleth in it, and holdeth it as its own proper house and seat, and that it is totally defiled and infected with sin: yet not of itself (*per se*) — for otherwise the vice cleaving to it would be imputed to God the Creator Himself — but by accident (*per accidens*), that is, sin coming upon it from elsewhere,[^P15-3] and infecting human nature. Nature, mind and will, or essence, substance, soul, heart, <!-- p.311 --> body, the strengths also and powers, formation, life, sense, and whatsoever things are good, are all of God, and are present with man as that which is his own. But viciousness (*vitiositas*) is present with man as an accident, and as a thing coming from elsewhere; that is — to use the words of **Augustine** (*Of Nature and Grace*, chap. 3) — *"the vice which darkeneth and weakeneth the natural good things was contracted from original sin."* For it flowed from Adam, who corrupted the good works of God. And this vice, brought into nature, is the work of the Devil and of man, and is in all who draw their origin from Adam — in them indeed through nature propagated, but not derived from nature itself as created by God. For this cause this very sin is named a *natural vice*, that it may be shown that by propagation alone it is inborn in all, not gotten by imitation, or by habit, or by progress of time; and that it cannot be laid aside by our own powers, seeing that all things which are of man, as well in the soul as in the body, are vitiated, and the several strengths and powers of the mind, the senses, the organs of the senses, and the operations, are not only made weaker than they were before the fall, and by progress of time are little by little lessened and decreased (even as also in this old age of the world men, in comparison of their forefathers, are daily rendered, both in substance and in strength, weaker, smaller, more subject to diseases, more unwise and more unhappy), but also of their own accord tend unto evil. For how grievously is the vegetative power defiled, and daily perverted, by meat, drink, sleep, watching, rest, motion, the faculty and instruments of generation, and the like, which man abuseth? The outward senses, how intent are they upon things forbidden and depraved? The eyes upon the beholding of vanity, as David speaketh; the hearing upon filthy things, detractions and lies; the taste upon dainties superfluous and immoderate; the smelling and touching upon filthy enticements? So that **Augustine** hath said, not without weighty cause, that death entereth into our soul by the five senses, as by windows. The inward senses, how prone are they to consider, <!-- p.312 --> gather and retain things dishonest and hurtful? The natural appetite, how intent is it beyond measure upon gratifying the body and the belly? The sensitive appetite — and first indeed that which is wrought by contact, whence delight and pain arise — how often is it immoderate, how unseasonable, how hurtful, oftentimes also the token, and as it were the forerunner, of some certain evil? Next, that which is said to be wrought without contact, how shamefully is it often corrupted by accident, and infected by reason of the malice and infirmity that perpetually cleaveth to human nature, sometimes also by reason of other causes and circumstances, and sometimes is by the power of the Devil hurried away to the destruction of nature? Furthermore the voluntary appetite, as touching the understanding, in how many ways is it darkened, and into how many and how great errors doth it most often rush headlong? As touching the will, how grievously and perilously doth it slip and err in choosing, how hatefully and perversely doth it fight against the understanding? These and other things show the heinousness of the original evil, and yet withal admonish us that nature created by God is one thing, and the vice wherewith nature is polluted and corrupted is another.

### *De Quarto, videlicet de mala concupiscentia* · Of the Fourth, Namely of Evil Concupiscence

We do not sever evil concupiscence from the signification of original sin; but we name evil concupiscence the ***ἀταξίαν*** (disorder) in all the powers of man, which is ***ἀνομία*** (lawlessness), that is, it fighteth with the Law of God, and of its own nature maketh man guilty of the wrath of God and of punishments, unless there be remission through and for the sake of the Son. Neither indeed is there understood only the inordinate appetite of meat, of drink, of lusts, of ambitions, or of other men's goods and possessions; but Paul, **Roman. 6, 7**, **Philip. 4** and elsewhere, under the name of Concupiscence comprehendeth the greatest evils: darkness and doubtings concerning God in the mind, the turning away of the will and heart from God, the variously wandering <!-- p.313 --> flames of the affections in the heart, and in a word that whole evil which is called enmity against God.

This ***ἀταξία*** of all the powers, strengths and affections is not a good thing, nor a thing indifferent (*adiaphora*), but of its own nature it is ***ἀνομία***, that is, it fighteth with the Law of God, and therefore draweth the thunderbolt and curse of the Law unto all, and upon all, who have and feel that ***ἀταξίαν*** and ***ἀνομίαν***, no man at all excepted.

All men therefore after the first fall, as many as ever have been, are, and shall be unto the end of the world, born of the conjunction of male and female, are sick of this deadly disease, as of a leprosy, which hath infected soul and body, and all the powers and strengths thereof, and therefore are by nature the children of wrath, as was said above.

But God, having mercy upon us, and seeing that we are all subject unto death, as the **Son of Sirach** speaketh, poured out upon us the fountain of His most plenteous mercy, and gave His only begotten Son for us, that He should be our Redeemer, Justifier, Mediator and Saviour: which thing also the Son — from His heart, and out of an immeasurable and unspeakable love toward us, wretched and defiled, and in soul and body deadly wounded, and destitute of all counsel and help, and lying prostrate upon the ground — as that true Samaritan, **Luke 10**, whom Jacob in the field of Samaria saw leaning upon the ladder, **Genes. 48**, hath performed and accomplished; and for the assurance of this thing, before He ascended, ***ὁ θεάνθρωπος*** (the God-man), into the heavens, He instituted and left the ***σφραγῖδα τῆς δικαιοσύνης*** (seal of righteousness), namely Baptism, in which original concupiscence is remitted, weakened, diminished and kept under, that it reign not henceforth in us, and that it be not imputed unto us, and that it condemn us not.

For although even after Baptism there remaineth in the regenerate evil concupiscence, or ***ἀταξία*** and ***ἀνομία***, which of its own nature displeaseth God, as Paul saith, **Rom. 7**: *"That which dwelleth in me, that is, in my flesh, is not good."* Likewise: *"O wretched man that I am! who shall deliver me from the body of this death?"* — yet <!-- p.314 --> the godly know that saying of Paul: *"I thank God through Jesus Christ,"* that *"the Law of the Spirit of life in Christ Jesus hath made me free from the Law of sin and death"*: to wit, not so that the Law of sin is not present — because the same Paul saith: *"I see another Law in my members, warring against the Law of my mind, and bringing me into captivity to the Law of sin which is in my members."* Likewise: *"With the flesh I serve the Law of sin"* — but so that it is both wholly remitted for Christ's sake, and withal is mortified, and continually, even unto the end of life, crucified, destroyed and put off, as it is written to the **Ephes. 4**. For the remission is perfect. But the mortification of sin, and the renewing of man, is begun, and daily increaseth from day to day, as Paul testifieth, **2 Corinth. 4**. And of the perfect remission, and of the begun mortification, is that most sweet saying to be understood: *"There is now no condemnation to them which are in Christ Jesus, who walk not after the flesh, but through the Spirit do mortify the deeds of the flesh."*

Now whereas the Ecclesiastical Writers, **Augustine** in the first place, do sometimes say that concupiscence after Baptism in the regenerate is not sin, they by no means intend that there is nothing in the regenerate which is unclean, and which God can hate; but they simply understand sin reigning, mortal, or condemning, even as James also speaketh: *"When lust hath conceived, it bringeth forth sin"* — to wit, reigning and ***φονικόν*** (deadly) — *"and sin, when it is finished, bringeth forth death."* Neither indeed is evil concupiscence in the regenerate only a punishment and a cause of sin, as the Scholastics have disputed; but over and above, and in very deed, as Paul expressly speaketh, it is an evil fighting with the Law of God, which, because it is in us, would by its own nature and efficacy drag us down to eternal death, were it not, for Christ's sake, not imputed.

Of the objections of the Papists we shall speak elsewhere in their own place. Now I will only set down the words of **Augustine**, from book 4, chap. 3, *Against Julian*: *"We deny not that the help of God is of so great avail, if He will, that at this day we should be able to have <!-- p.315 --> no evil concupiscences, against which we should fight, though it were most invincibly. But why it is not so done, who hath known the mind of the Lord? Yet I know not a little, when I know that, whatsoever that cause be, there is neither iniquity in the righteous God, nor infirmity in the Almighty. There is therefore something in His hidden and deep counsel, why, so long as we live in this mortal flesh, there is in us that against which our mind should fight; there is also that for which we should say: Forgive us our debts."* Thus far Augustine.

### *De Quinto* · Of the Fifth

I will add only a brief admonition concerning free will (*liberum arbitrium*), which the Greeks have named by a very specious word, ***αὐτεξούσιον***. The Latins have rendered it *liberum arbitrium*. A general definition thereof may be delivered in this manner: Free will is the judgement of the understanding, or reason, following which the will doth, of its own accord and without any compulsion, desire or reject those things which are set before it as to be desired or to be shunned. Now the Ecclesiastical Writers and Doctors have used the name of Free will in divers manners. Some, when they name free will, understand the very substance of the mind and will in man. Some understand the ***δύναμιν***, the power and faculty of the mind and will, whereby man is distinguished from those things which, without thought of mind and without choice of will, either act naturally or are violently driven. Some understand that liberty, such as it is, which the will yet hath in those things which are subject to the senses or to reason. Some, as **Augustine** in the first place, when they speak of the free will of the unregenerate man, understand contingency in vicious actions. Some understand free will of spiritual motions and actions, or of divine things and of the works which pertain unto God. Now of these we must discourse distinctly, that it may be manifest without ambiguity of what signification, and in what respect, <!-- p.316 --> free will is to be understood, and that a right judgement may be made.

Now it is to be known that the dispute here is not of that entire and perfect free will, which and such as was before the fall of our first parents, and which and such as shall be after glorification in all eternity (for in that respect and signification free will is a thing of a bare title only, and is nothing either Theologically or Philosophically, as **Luther** speaketh); but the question is only what and of what manner free will is after the fall, and how far it extendeth itself.

### *De libero arbitrio, quod substantiam significat* · Of Free Will, as It Signifieth the Substance

It is a most certain truth that, if free will be taken for the substance of the mind and will, although this be by original sin miserably depraved and defiled, yet there is free will in man, because the substance of the mind and will is not utterly extinguished. To deny this is the same as if one should affirm that man is a living creature without a soul, brute and *alogon* (void of reason); which would be truly worthy of the name of madness and of a lie. Man hath the substance of mind, will and heart, whether he be regenerate or not regenerate, that is, before and after conversion. And although nature is corrupted not in some part only, but in all its parts and powers, yet the substance itself is one thing, and the corruption or vice which hath infected nature is another: of which matter **Augustine** discourseth, *Of Nature and Grace*, chap. 3, where among other things he saith: *"All the good things which nature hath in formation, life, senses, mind and will, it hath from the most high God its Creator; but the vice was contracted from original sin."* Although also in the conversion of man it is necessary that of the old man there be made a new man (as Paul speaketh, **Rom. 6**, **Ephes. 2 and 4**, **Coloss. 3**), or a new creature (**Galat. 6**), yet the substance of the mind and will remaineth. For we are not to approve <!-- p.317 --> the dotage of the **Manichees**, who feigned that the substance was taken away by sin, and that by grace it is made again new and another. And rightly doth **Augustine**, *Of the City of God*, book 14, chap. 11, say that conversion is wrought, not by the substance of the soul being withdrawn, or any part thereof taken away, but by that which had been vitiated and depraved being healed and amended. And *Against Julian*, book 4, chap. 3, he saith: *"The grace of God through our Lord Jesus Christ is not bestowed upon stones, or stocks, and cattle."* Likewise: *"By grace the human will is not taken away, but of evil is changed into good, and when it hath been made good, is helped."* And famous is the sentence of **Prosper**: *"Free will, naturally implanted in man, remaineth in nature, but changed in quality and condition through Christ the Mediator, who turneth away the will itself from that which it perversely willed, and turneth it to will that which should be good for it."*

### *De Secundo, id est, potentia et facultate mentis et voluntatis* · Of the Second, That Is, the Power and Faculty of the Mind and Will

It is certain that there is a great difference between creatures void of reason, and acting only naturally or Physically, and creatures that are rational, and act voluntarily. And a threefold difference is rightly wont to be shown to youth. The first is in the subject: for the effects of natural causes arise from the matter and form of the thing, or from the nature itself, the temperament and the qualities which properly accompany the substantial form; as fire heateth, because heating is of the nature of fire, which is so created by God that it hath the property of heating. But the effects of a voluntary cause arise from a power that is rational, thinking, and free in those things which it hath subject unto itself. The second difference consisteth in the manner of acting (*modus agendi*), to speak by a name that is usual, and long since received for teaching's sake. For things that act naturally, <!-- p.318 --> when they are entire, and have an object or subject upon which they may act, agreeable and ***σύμμετρον*** (proportioned) to their strengths, or yielding and not withstanding the action, and are not hindered from heaven, cannot but act, and do act necessarily, by a Physical necessity; as fire cannot but burn the stubble cast into it. And this manner of acting remaineth in all natural things always, unless it be interrupted by God Himself, who is the Creator, Preserver, Ruler and Lord of nature: as the three Israelites are kept safe and unharmed in the Babylonian furnace, and are hurt in no part of body, or of form, or so much as of their garments. The Sun standeth still, and goeth back. The Red sea and Jordan give way to the people of Israel. And the whole creation is in its own nature so framed that it serveth the commandments of God, as it is written in the book of **Wisdom, chapter the nineteenth**, and **Sirach 40**. For God is a most free agent, and doeth all things whatsoever He will, **Psal. 115**, and not only is nothing impossible unto Him, but nothing so much as hard. But when God Himself changeth not that manner of acting in the creatures, then it abideth in its own order and in its own action always one and the same, by reason of its nature and power, or qualities. But on the contrary, things that act voluntarily do, in those things which they have subject unto themselves, act freely, that is, they are able to act or not to act, and have not one only manner of acting, because God Himself hath endowed the rational creatures with liberty. And experience itself teacheth us that all men whatsoever do from time to time will something, and again, of the things which they will, do accomplish some indeed, whether they be good or also evil, and others do not accomplish. The third difference lieth in the constancy of the action. Nature acteth in one and the same manner, ***κατὰ ἐπιτηδειότητα τῆς ὕλης*** (according to the fitness of the matter). Fire always heateth and drieth. The Sun consumeth moisture, and therefore hardeneth clay and melteth wax. But voluntary causes act not in some one certain and uniform manner; as it is wont to be said of the wills and desires of men, that nothing is more changeable and more inconstant than they. Likewise: ***ζῶον εὐμετάβλητον ὁ φίλος*** (a friend is a creature easily changed); whereunto pertaineth also that saying:

> So long as thou art fortunate, thou shalt number many friends;  
> <!-- p.319 -->If the times be cloudy, thou shalt be alone.  

Now a man dealeth gently, and anon cruelly, as Alexander the Great. *"And he showeth forth divers motions even in a single hour."* And as the affections, so also the actions are often changed in a moment, and more swiftly than one would think. These things therefore, although a man may say that they are taken out of Philosophy, yet, the order of God being considered, do in very deed show that there is a great difference between things that act naturally and things that act voluntarily, and that man, created after the image and likeness of God, is to be far distinguished from the creatures destitute of reason and will: of which matter **Gregory of Nyssa** discourseth most learnedly and excellently in the book *Of the Making of Man*, to which we refer the studious. And the dignity and excellency of man, and his prerogative above all the rest of creation, is diligently to be considered; which indeed is seen from these things. First: That man was created the last of all, the other things being created, and was set as King and Lord over all things. Secondly: That to the framing of man alone God cometh with a peculiar consideration and deliberation. Thirdly: That man was created after the image of God, and hath a mind, *"a particle of the divine breath,"* shining notions, and speech, and a will that is free in governing the power of moving from place to place, and in acting or suspending the action, and hath the righteousness of the flesh, as Paul calleth it, even after the fall, in these ruins, and almost carcases, of his former and entire liberty. Fourthly: That by the voice of God Himself man was inaugurated Emperor over all the lower creatures, whose service, labour, nimbleness and plenty he may enjoy for his necessary uses in his whole life; for which cause man himself is furnished by nature with no weapons and coverings, that not so much his nakedness and weakness, as his power over the living creatures, may be shown. Fifthly: That the figure of man is upright, and his whole frame is full of dignity and of excellent majesty in soul, body, and all the members and the very smallest particles; whence also he hath been called ***μικρόκοσμος*** (a little world). And the verses are well known:

> And whereas the other living creatures, bending downward, look upon the earth,  
> <!-- p.320 -->To man He gave a countenance lifted up, and bade him behold  
> The heaven, and raise his face upright unto the stars.  

Sixthly: That upon man alone is grace bestowed through the Son of God, who out of an immeasurable and unspeakable love toward mankind was Himself also made man and a sacrifice, that He might bring man back unto life, salvation, gladness and everlasting integrity. Hence it is commonly wont to be said by the Ecclesiastical Doctors, **Justin**, **Augustine**, **Bernard**, **Ambrose**, **Irenaeus** and others, that man alone is capable of divine grace and of the divine gifts — in respect, namely, of the other creatures, upon which that grace is not bestowed. For it is not a stock, nor a stone, nor an ox, nor an ass that is converted, redeemed, saved; but man, created after the image of God, and endued with mind, understanding and will, and to be reformed anew after the same image through Christ. Seventhly: That unto man is given through the word the Holy Spirit, governing, ruling, teaching, leading, defending and preserving them that believe, kindling and confirming in their minds and hearts true faith and true consolation, and holding them back, that they rush not into wickedness and into errors, but that they may always be and remain the temples, dwellings, habitations and instruments of the Holy Spirit, and that their bodies also may become members of Christ, weapons of righteousness, a living sacrifice, and temples of the indwelling Holy Spirit, **1 Corinth. 6**, **Rom. 6 and 12**. Eighthly: That all things turn unto good to the man that believeth: adversities, chastisements, wrestlings and fightings, infirmities, **2 Corinth. 12**, temptations, and finally death itself. Ninthly: That the lump of human flesh alone is after death, by the benefit and working of God, capable of resurrection, and of new qualities. Tenthly: That man hath an immortal soul, which in the Resurrection shall take again its own body, and thereafter the whole man with his soul and body shall live for ever, and shall return to the first life and to the heavenly country, to glorify God unto all eternity.

These things it hath seemed good to add concerning the chief differences between man and the other <!-- p.321 --> created things; unto which the Studious will be able to add more.
