It is read of **Aristides**, that he once accused a certain man at Athens. But when the Judges, having heard Aristides, were minded to condemn the accused unheard, Aristides would not suffer the Judges to do this, and besought them that they would in like manner first hear the defendant also. So God Himself judged not Adam, save when He had first heard him. And the Son of God shall at the last day hear both the ungodly and the guiltless. When **Ptolemy** was once playing at dice, there came one who read out the names of men condemned to certain punishments; and when he had declared the crimes of each, he would have it determined by the King with what punishments each should be visited. Straightway Berenice, the King's wife, snatched the schedule out of that Secretary's hands, saying: *"No man ought to judge of the life and death of others while he playeth at dice and is not in the best wise prepared for true deliberations. Dice are one thing, the life of a man another."* Moved by this speech, the King ceased from his game, nor did he ever afterward hear capital causes at play or amid any other merriment.

**Demosthenes**, when he was once pleading a capital cause before Judges who gave no heed to the matter and were talking diversely among themselves, besought the Judges to hearken a little while, for he would recite a pretty story that had lately befallen. They, pricking up their ears, desired to hear the story, which was of the Ass's shadow. *"There was,"* saith he, *"a certain young man, who hired an Ass of another, to carry certain burdens, or household stuff, to Megara. Now when it was noon, and the Sun with his heat scorched them both, the possessor or master of the Ass would gladly have betaken himself into some shade, and the young man likewise; but since there was no shade at hand, the young man sat him down under the Ass. The master, seeing the young man's craftiness, would not grant him this, and said that the Ass had been hired of him, but not the shadow. The young man answered that he had hired the Ass, <!-- p.162 --> to use him so long, until the time of the hiring should be run out. So a strife arose between the two, insomuch that they fell to fisticuffs with one another, and at length brought the matter before the Judges."* Having recited thus much, Demosthenes went away. But the Judges, being eager to learn the end and issue of the suit, caused Demosthenes to be called back, that he might carry the story through to the end. Demosthenes answered: *"Ye men, Judges, of the shadow of an Ass ye are willing to hear, and the cause of this good man, great and perilous, ye neglect. Take cognizance: what is the law?"*

**Philip**, King of Macedon, when he had once in judgement sentenced Machetes, for certain offences, to pay a sum of money which was of great price and number, and had done this being drowsy, Machetes said to him and to the Assessors: *"I complain with good cause of this your sentence, and I appeal from it."* The King, chafing, asked of him to whom he would appeal. Machetes answered him: *"To thee, O King, when thou shalt be awakened, and shalt have inquired more diligently into my cause."* The King rose up from his throne and conferred with Machetes; and yet, since he would not change the sentence, he himself gave that sum of money out of his own Chamber, and remitted to Machetes the payment.

Next, let there be in the Judge ***εὐνομία*** (good order of law), or righteousness examining and pronouncing sentences according to the rule of the Laws. For that is just which agreeth with the Laws: ***δίκαιον τὸ νόμιμον*** (the lawful is the just). There is an example in the Tutor of **Cyrus**. When Cyrus, being a youth, had acquitted a somewhat big boy accused before him, because he had snatched from another, a lesser boy, a longer coat, and had delivered to him his own shorter coat, as better fitting the measure of that boy's body, the master reproveth the judgement of Cyrus, and giveth Cyrus stripes, and admonisheth him that those things are just, not which seem to himself convenient, but which are established by the Laws. Hither pertaineth the elegant picture which is extant in **Pindar**, in the Olympians, Ode 13. The Mother is Themis, the Goddess of honesty. The Daughters are three:

I. Eunomia, or the establishing of honest Laws.

II. ***Δίκη***, righteousness, or vengeance without respect of persons.

III. ***εἰρήνη***, peace and concord.

<!-- p.163 -->

Fourthly, let there be in the Judge equality, shunning ***προσωποληψίαν*** (respect of persons), which rendereth equal things to the unequal, and unequal things to the equal, according to the saying:

> The censure giveth pardon to the ravens, it vexeth the doves.  

And:

> The hornets get away, the flies fall back into the nets.  

Lastly, let ***δωροφαγία*** (the devouring of gifts) be far from the Judge, which selleth faith and religion for a price; as **Hesiod** often nameth ***δωροφάγους***, gift-devourers, those unjust judges who are corrupted with presents and largesses, to pervert words and sentences. And let the history be known of the punishment of Sisamnes, the ***δωροφάγου*** (gift-devouring) Judge, whose skin **Cambyses** commanded to be flayed off and to be spread upon the judgement seat, and appointed his son Judge, that, admonished by his father's hide, he might judge justly. Let these things suffice concerning the duties of Judges, which are great and perilous and laborious. For although a man himself commit nothing enormous, yet must he often wink at many evils, which afterward afflict the conscience. Therefore **Demosthenes**, being driven into exile, said: If two ways were set before him, the one to the highest magistracies, the other to death, he would enter upon the latter. And **Aeschines** wrote that he rejoiced that he was delivered from the Commonwealth as from a mad dog. And one Similis in the Court of **Hadrian**, when he had long been set over public affairs, being released lived seven years quietly on his own land, and dying commanded this Epitaph to be inscribed for him: *"Here lieth SIMILIS, whose age was of many years, yet he lived but seven years only."*

Howbeit, the callings of government are not to be shunned on account of their labours and difficulties, but the Rule is to be kept in view: *"War a good warfare, holding faith, and a good conscience."* And above all let that sentence be known, **Eccles. 4**: *"In judging be merciful to the fatherless, as a Father, and thou shalt be as an obedient Son of the most High, and He shall have mercy on thee more than thy mother."* But let sentences out of sacred and profane letters concerning the duties of judges be sought in the common places, and in the *Polyanthea*, where many notable ones are recited, and in **Stobaeus**, of magistrates, and what manner of man a Prince ought to be; where among <!-- p.164 --> others a very notable sentence is recited out of **Plutarch** concerning the Thebans, who paint the images of judges without hands, and the eyes of the chief judge winking, or ***μύοντας*** (closed), because Justice may neither be taken with gifts nor bent by the countenance of men.

## *De Dominis et Servis* · Of Masters and Servants

**Ephes. 6**: *"Ye Masters, do the same things unto your servants, forbearing threatening: knowing that your own Master also is in heaven; and there is no respect of persons with God."* **Colos. 4**: *"Masters, give unto your servants that which is just and equal."*

These sayings of Paul teach three things: **I.** That it is lawful for a Christian man to use the labours of servants. **II.** How masters ought to be affected toward their servants, that they despise them not as abject bondslaves, but, as being fellow-servants of God, handle and cherish them gently and lovingly. **III.** That they render ***τὰ αὐτὰ*** (the same things), or that which is just, and equality, according to the saying: *"Thou shalt not muzzle the ox when he treadeth out the corn."* Likewise: *"The labourer is worthy of his hire."*

Of servants it is said, **Ephes. 6**: *"Servants, be obedient to them that are your masters according to the flesh, with fear and trembling, etc."* **Colos. 3**: *"Obey in all things your masters according to the flesh; not with eyeservice, etc."*

Let there be therefore in servants: **I.** The Fear of God. **II.** Singleness of heart, without naughtiness and servile ***πανουργία*** (craftiness), without rebellion and stubbornness and an answering-again boldness. **III.** Obedience toward their masters. **IIII.** The mark and rule of obedience, that they obey their masters as they would Christ; that is, that they look always to the commandment of God, and do nothing that fighteth against the commandment of God, who is the supreme Master, whom all must needs reverence. **V.** Consolation in their calling. For Paul magnificently adorneth the obedience of servants, when he saith that it is rendered unto God. The services therefore of men-servants and maid-servants are a worship most acceptable to God.

## *De Promissione addita quarto Praecepto* · Of the Promise Added to the Fourth Commandment

*"That thy days may be long upon the land which the Lord thy God giveth thee."*

**Objection.**

Yet godly children often sink under untimely death before their parents. And there is an old Rule: *"Whom God loveth, dieth young."*

**Answer.**

First, the kind of the promise is to be observed, which is, that it may be well with the obedient, either in this life, or in the eternal life which God will give to the godly, to wit, the promised land, flowing with milk and honey.

Next, the Rule is to be observed: Whatsoever of bodily and earthly goods God promiseth us is to be taken under this condition, so far as it is expedient for the good of our soul and for our salvation. Hence it cometh that sometimes the more a man is loved of God, the sooner he is snatched out of this life, especially in this our age, lest he either see the evils to come, or be seduced, or be long tormented.

Often with great anguish of mind we see suddenly taken out of this life the young plants of the Church, infants, boys, striplings, young men of the best disposition and hope, and others whose labour, in our judgement, would have profited the Church, the Commonwealth and the Schools, and who by natural reason, as we at least determine, might have remained longer alive. Concerning these let us hold fast the consolation: *"Precious is the death of the Saints."* *"Blessed are they which die in the Lord."* *"Though the righteous be prevented with death, yet shall he be in rest."* And that in **Ps. 31**, how sweet and full of consolation it is: *Beiodecho ithothoi*, *"In thy hand <!-- p.166 --> are*" ***καιροὶ***, that is, *"my fates, times, occasions, lots, circumstances, events, accidents, and my life"*? Let us hold fast also the causes why sometimes our children, and others, are called away in the flower of their age. The **first** is, that they may be the earlier translated out of this vale of miseries to eternal life and gladness, and may not long stick fast in hope, but come quickly to the thing itself, according to that: *"Suffer the little children to come unto me, for of such is the Kingdom of God."* For let it be lawful for the godly to take this saying in this wise also. The **second** cause is, that they may be snatched beforehand from offences and seductions, lest they be perverted by wickedness, as it is written in the book of Wisdom: *"The righteous is taken away, lest he be corrupted."* And for this cause **Luther** said: It were to be wished that with our whole seed we might die peaceably and godly, lest we either see the offences and evils that are to come in the Church and in the whole of life, or be perverted by them. The **third** cause is, that, being delivered from all evil, they may escape all the calamities which are about to arise, as it is written **Isa. 56**: *"The righteous departeth, no man considereth it."*

> We know not what the fates shall shortly be.  

**Epiphanius** writeth that no sons died before their fathers in the age of the first Patriarchs, down to Haran the son of Terah. For Haran died before his father Terah in the year of the world 1984. And if any would object concerning Abel, it is nothing; for he departed not by his own and natural death. Haran therefore was the first who died before his father, and that for this cause, because his father Terah was the author ***τῆς ἀνδριαντοπλασίας***, that is, of images fashioned after the form of God, which were afterward worshipped, and gave a beginning to idolatries. Whence the Scripture saith, as it were marvelling: *"And Haran died in the sight of Terah his father in the land of his nativity."* Thus far Epiphanius. But he erreth, seeing that Shem saw many die before himself, as is said in its place.

Here falleth in the question concerning the term of each man's life. The measure of the term is determined not from Stoical necessity, but from the plain word of God, which sendeth us to the providence of God, grounded upon the divine promises and threatenings. <!-- p.167 --> For God, according to His own promises and threats, either shorteneth or prolongeth the term of every man's life. Often the godly are relieved by the prayers of the godly, as James saith in the fifth chapter concerning the sick. And there are examples, **Deut. 9**, when Moses maketh intercession for the people, that it be not blotted out; and in the history of Jonah concerning the Ninevites; and concerning Hezekiah the king, obtaining a longer space of life, **Isa. 38**; and concerning David rearing an altar, that the slaughter might cease, **3 Kings 24**. So the days of the ungodly are shortened, and they themselves shorten them by surfeiting, rage, whoredoms, and other wickednesses. **Ps. 54 [55]**: *"Bloody men shall not live out half their days."* And there is an old Rule, which, rightly understood, is not absurd, expressed in these verses of ours:

> Thou canst shorten thy days, never lengthen them:  
> To shorten is thine, but to prolong is the Thunderer's.  

But that which is written in the book of Job, the fourteenth chapter (*"The number of the months of man is with thee: thou hast appointed his bounds, which shall not be able to be passed"*), hath no difficulty in it. For the measure of the term is determined not from Stoical necessity, but from the clear word of God, as hath now been said. For if an inevitable term of life were fatally determined, surely the fault also would be inevitable for which many are hurried away to punishment, and so God would be called the author of sin; which is both ungodly, blasphemous and false, and to be abhorred, and horrible to be spoken and heard, and hindereth all the exercises of godliness, and hurteth manners. But let us fear God, and, when we think of future events, let us look to the promises and threatenings of God, and let us pray Him that He would rule us by His Holy Spirit, that we may do those things which are acceptable to Himself and wholesome to the common society of men, and that He would deal with us according to His mercy and goodness.

# *Quintum Praeceptum* · The Fifth Commandment

### *Non occides* · Thou Shalt Not Kill

The order of the Commandments is diligently to be considered. The first table preacheth of the worships to be rendered unto God. The second table speaketh of the services to be rendered unto our neighbour, and beginneth from obedience, because in human society there must needs be governors, lest there be ***ἀναρχία*** (want of government), in which ***ἀκούει οὐδεὶς οὐδὲν οὐδενός*** (no man hearkeneth to any man in anything). And it beginneth from obedience to parents, and not from obedience to the higher magistrates. For out of parents the other estates are sprung, and this order was the first of all. Therefore **Hierocles** saith rightly: *"Households have brought forth cities."*

Next, the Fifth Commandment taketh its beginning from our mutual Conversation one with another, and speaketh of the person of our neighbour, that this be not hurt and killed.

The Sixth preacheth of the Person that is nearest akin to our neighbour, that this be not treated with dishonour, nor defiled and disgraced.

After it hath been spoken of the Person, it must now be spoken of the things which the Person possesseth. Therefore the seventh Commandment forbiddeth our neighbour's goods to be taken away.

Next, since regard must be had not only to body and goods, but also to fame and an honest name, the eighth Commandment speaketh of preserving the honest fame of our neighbour, that our neighbour be not loaded with lies, slanders, envy and grudges.

> Body.  
> Goods.  
> Fame.  

This order is most apt. Lastly, the two Commandments contain the sum of the whole Decalogue; and the Ninth speaketh in general <!-- p.169 --> of not coveting any of the goods and possessions of our neighbour, but the tenth reckoneth up in particular the chief things; and so both commandments show that inward obedience is required, not outward only. Now we must treat of the fifth Commandment, in which these things are chiefly to be considered:

I. Of public vengeance (*vindicta publica*), or of the office of the Magistrate in inflicting penalties and punishments.

II. Of ***ἐπιεικείᾳ*** (equity, or mildness), which the Magistrate ought to use, and when it is to be used.

III. With what mind the punishments of the condemned are to be beheld.

IIII. Of private vengeance (*vindicta privata*), and of the punishments of manslayers.

### *De Primo* · On the First Point

Public vengeance is that which is exercised by reason of public office by a public person, following the prescript of the Laws, to a good end, without hatred conceived and grown old against the person upon whom the punishment is laid. This vengeance is not only lawful, but is also commanded, and hath the promise of rewards. Paul, **Rom. 13**, saith that the sword is given to the magistrate, that he may be for honour to good works and for terror to evil, and may be a revenger unto wrath. And elsewhere it is said: *"Thou shalt not spare him, neither shalt thou pity, etc."* And worthy of memory is the saying of **Aeschines**: ***οὐδὲν ἐστὶν ὄφελος πόλεως, ἥτις μὴ νεῦρα ἐπὶ τοὺς ἀδικοῦντας ἔχει*** (there is no profit in a city which hath not sinews against them that do wrong). **Deut. 17**: *"Thou shalt put the evil away from thee."* **Levit. 24**: Moses stoneth the blasphemer. **Num. 15**: Likewise him that had gathered sticks on the Sabbath. **Joshua 7**: Joshua stoneth Achan, who against the commandment of God had taken a garment. **1 Kings 2**: David chargeth Solomon that he slay Joab, Shimei and others after his decease. And Paul, **Rom. 13**, saith most clearly: *"The Magistrate beareth not the sword in vain, but is the minister of God, and a revenger for the punishment of evil men."* For since men degenerate into such perverseness that they cannot be bent by words and admonitions, it is necessary for the common peace and tranquillity, <!-- p.170 --> public and private, that they be drawn away from evil and from wickednesses by force and power. As, if a thief cease not from thieving, he is to be hanged and taken out of the way. The manslayer is to be punished. And in this office the Magistrate ought not to be slothful and sluggish, lest God take the sword away from him, and Himself become the avenger; which is a grievous and hard thing. Neither are intercessions and entreaties always to be admitted, but regard is to be had to the ordinance of God, without ***προσωποληψίᾳ***, respect of persons, of dignity, of estate, and of goods; although it often cometh to pass at this day, with great peril of evils to come, that intercessions for wicked men are more admitted than the words, Laws and commandments of God Himself.

### *De Secundo* · On the Second Point

A Judge ought to have regard to three things: to the Law, to the guilty person, to the people. For first, heed must be taken, lest either by unseasonable indulgence and lenity the authority of the Law be diminished and made light of, or lest by overmuch rigour the will of the Law be corrupted, according to the saying: *"Extreme right is extreme wrong."* As, if he who, constrained by extreme poverty, stealeth bread or a moderate sum of money, should be adjudged to be hanged just as he who hath wantonly seized other men's goods, because the Law commandeth thieves to be hanged: this is strict right, or extreme right, ***καὶ ἀκριβοδίκαιον*** (and exact justice to the letter), which is not always free from chicanery, as examples show. **Paches**, the Athenian general, when he was besieging Colophon, because he knew how much lay in the Captain who was set over the city, calleth him forth to a parley, and promiseth that he will send him back again into the city alive and unharmed. The Captain, suspecting no evil, cometh to the parley. When therefore the city was now without its Captain, Paches assaulteth it, and, having gotten possession of it, bringeth the Captain back, and there slayeth him.

**Cochlaeus** was wont to interpret the decree, *"Let a Priest who taketh a wife be suspended"* — from the gallows, forsooth; whereas the decree understandeth suspension, a vacating of public office.

<!-- p.171 -->

Next, as touching the guilty person, it must be seen what manner of man he is, and what manner of offence his is. From this the penalty ought to be estimated; which may best be gathered from his life aforetime.

Thirdly, two things are to be guarded against in respect of the people: first, lest an example of lenity beget a patronage for sin; next, lest the guilty be spared with damage and peril to the Commonwealth. Let this therefore be the Rule: When the will of the Law admitteth ***ἐπιείκειαν*** (equity), and the guilty person can be amended without a harder penalty, and the people is not made worse by the example, the magistrate may use a more clement sentence, and spare the suppliant offender in hope of amendment.

Now that we may briefly comprehend these two heads, of public Vengeance and of ***ἐπιεικείᾳ*** (equity), let these causes be set before every magistrate, by which he may be moved to punish and take away the wicked:

I. The commandment of God, calling the magistrate to this office, **Rom. 13**.

II. Lest for his own indulgence and lenity the Magistrate himself also be punished, as Saul, Eli, and others. **Judg. 20**: The children of Benjamin would not have those ravishers punished who had defiled the wife of the Levite, and therefore they are vanquished in war and shut out by the children of Israel. **1 Samuel 15**: Saul spareth King Agag, whom according to the commandment of God he ought to have slain, and therefore he offendeth God, and is rejected of Him, that he should not be King. And Jeremiah saith, **48**: *"Cursed be he that keepeth back his sword from shedding blood."*

III. That by these penalties a bridle may be cast upon the evil and the wicked. **Deut. 9**: *"Put away the evil from thee, that others may hear, and fear, and no more do those evils in thee."*

IIII. Lest the Magistrate become a partaker with the wicked, and himself also be punished with them, according to the saying: *"He that doeth and he that consenteth are worthy of the same punishment,"* **Rom. 9**.

V. That the good may be defended, and kept safe from evil men. But how the Magistrate ought rightly to bear himself in these things, lest he either do too much or be too indulgent, these Rules are to be observed:

<!-- p.172 -->

I. He must live in the Fear of God, which is the beginning of wisdom. As Daniel straightway acknowledgeth the innocency of Susanna and the wickedness of the judges, because he feared God, who suggested to Daniel the true judgement. So Solomon is set between the two women a true and upright Judge, etc.

II. Let not the Magistrate be too swift to judge and to punish. **Sirach 5**: *"Be not hasty; if thou understandest the matter, instruct thy neighbour."* And **Sirach 10**: *"Condemn no man before thou hast examined the cause; understand first, and then rebuke."* **Genesis 38**: Judah rashly condemneth Tamar, who nevertheless was more righteous than himself. So the master of Joseph, upon the information of his wife, too rashly condemneth Joseph to the prison. And the verses of **Ovid** are known:

> A Prince is slow to punishments, swift to rewards,  
> And he grieveth, as often as he is constrained to be fierce.  

III. The penalty is to be executed at a seasonable time. **Sirach 22**: *"Discipline and instruction is to be exercised in due season."*

IIII. There is to be no respect of persons. **Deut. 1**: *"Ye shall regard no person in judgement, but ye shall hear the small as the great, and ye shall fear no person; for this office is God's."*

V. Let not the penalty be greater than the offence. **Cicero**: *"Heed must also be taken, that the penalty be not greater than the fault."* Let those Princes remember this rule, who at this day, on account of any wild beasts whatsoever, even without consideration of the circumstances, put wretched men to death, while justice and equity cry out against it.

VI. It must be seen to, that the penalty be not appointed out of hatred, envy and anger. **Sirach 10**: *"Be not over-sharp in avenging, nor use thy power to punish every fault, and to refresh thy mind when thou punishest."* Hence **Cicero** also saith: *"Let them that are set over the Commonwealth be like unto the Laws, which are led to punish not by wrath but by equity."*

VII. Sometimes even a just penalty is to be deferred. As David deferreth the penalty and punishment of the Captain Joab, who had slain two <!-- p.173 --> Captains, and committeth the execution of the penalty to Solomon his son; without doubt for this cause, because David could not, without greater damage and peril of sedition, put the Captain Joab to death in his own lifetime. And hither pertaineth the proverb: *"He that cannot dissemble, or wink, cannot reign"* — namely, when there is peril of some greater damage. As, if a Knight hath taken away the goods of a certain citizen, and the Magistrate, being minded to apprehend the Knight, levieth an army, and fleeceth his subjects: here the Lord doth greater damage than the Knight. And many such things are done in our age in many places.

### *De Tertio* · On the Third Point

I. The punishments of the condemned are dreadful spectacles, and are instituted not for the sake of delight — which in a most cruel matter neither can nor ought to be any, unless a man be plainly mad — but to terrify the wicked, and to admonish the thoughtless, that they may even now abhor with their whole mind the very thought of such deeds, and may be moved by these examples to be wary in their whole life, and furnished with faith and a good conscience.

II. Justice is to be praised and highly esteemed, according to the saying: *"Let justice be done, though the world perish."* And that is to be thought upon:

> ***Ὡς ἀπόλοιτο καὶ ἄλλος, ὅστις τοιαῦτά γε ῥέζοι.***  
> So may every man perish, who doeth such things.  

Likewise:

> ***Εἴκε πάθοι τάκ' ἔρεξε δίκη τ' ἰθεῖα γένοιτο.***  
> This is a just penalty to evil men for the crime committed,  
> If they themselves suffer the same things which they have done.  

And let us give thanks to God, that He preserveth this justice and discipline in mankind.

III. Let this be thought upon: As we grieve with and pity the condemned, although they pay a most just penalty, as it is said:

<!-- p.174 -->

> ***Ἐστὶ δίκη, νεμεσίς τε κακῶν, τρύζει τε καὶ οἰκτός.***  
> The rule of justice is at hand, and pity is at hand:  

so God hath pity on us, although before His eyes we are nothing but guilt. And let us know that God hath willed this compassion to be joined in our minds to the consideration of justice, that we might be admonished of the great love and unspeakable mercy of God, wherewith He receiveth us, fallen and entangled in eternal punishments, for the Son's sake.

IIII. There is to be considered both the weakness and the wickedness of human nature, which, being destitute of divine help, is most easily driven by the Devil into every kind of shameful deed and crime, ***σφῇσιν ἀτασθαλίῃσιν ὑπὲρ μόρον*** (by their own presumptuous follies, beyond what is fated). Rightly said **Menander**: ***Ἡμεῖς χωρὶς τῶν ἀναγκαίων κακῶν αὐτὸ παρ' αὐτῶν ἕτερα προσπορίζομεν***: *"We, besides these evils which are necessary, do of ourselves continually add more of them."*

And **Homer**:

> ***Οὐ μὲν γὰρ τὶ ποτ' ἐστὶν ὀϊζυρώτερον ἀνδρὸς***  
> ***Πάντων, ὅσσα τὲ γαῖαν ἐπιπνείει τε καὶ ἕρπει.***  
> Whatsoever on earth breatheth and creepeth and surviveth,  
> Nothing liveth more miserable than sickly mortals.  

V. The divine help is to be implored, lest we also, either slipping of our own accord by the impulse of corrupt nature, or circumvented by the snares of Satan, be cast down with a like fall. And let the saying be always in view: *"Let him that standeth take heed lest he fall."* Likewise: *"That may befall any man, which befalleth any one."* For either unawares, although we have used the utmost diligence, or by the cruelty of tyrants, or by the ravages of war, or by other ways with which life daily swarmeth, we may be made subject to the same spectacles; which hath befallen many good and holy men. Our life is exposed to all the darts of fortune. We must therefore pray, that God would either mercifully turn such things away from us, or at the least render our mind, fortified with true godliness and faith, constant and undaunted. And let us always think upon that most sweet sentence:

> Without thy divine power,[^P08-1]  
> There is nothing in man,  
> Nothing is harmless.  

VI. We must pray that God would have mercy upon that calamitous man, <!-- p.175 --> forgive him his sins, and confirm faith and true consolation in his mind.

These things are chiefly to be considered in the punishments of the condemned, who are assuredly the image of God. But they who run together as it were to the shambles, and consider not those things, these are either foolish and light, or ungodly; and since they are delighted with another's destruction and with so dreadful a spectacle, they are no more humane than the hangman himself. Now let us conclude these things with our own verses:

> ***Χριστὲ θεοῦ πατρὸς ἀϊδίου μεγαλώνυμε υἱὲ,***  
> ***Ἡμετέρας ψυχὰς τῇ σέο σῶζε χερὶ.***  
> ***Μαυλίδι χαλκείῃ εἰ τέμνει αὐχένας ἡμῶν.***  
> ***Ἄνδραγχος, λαῷ κύριε ἐλθὲ παρών:***  

(O Christ, Son of great name of God the eternal Father, save our souls with thine own hand. If the executioner cutteth our necks with the brazen knife, come, O Lord, and be present with thy people.)

> He that hath stedfastly learned to commit himself to Christ,  
> Him no rage of death can hurt.  
> We live in the Lord, and are, and move in  
> Him;  
> Death is to us gain, thou, gracious Christ, our salvation.  

### *De Quarto* · On the Fourth Point

**Out of the fifth chapter of Matthew: Ye have heard that it was said by them of old time, THOU SHALT NOT KILL.**

Christ expoundeth the fifth commandment, and withal refuteth the Pharisaical errors concerning the Law, showing that by the Law of God there is required perfect obedience, inward and outward, that so the wrath of God against sin, and our uncleanness, may be acknowledged, and all may understand that there is need of Christ the Mediator.

Let the words be considered: ***ἔνοχος ἔσται τῇ κρίσει***, he shall be held to the judgement; let him be condemned; or, he shall be in danger of the judgement. ***ἔνοχος*** with the Dative is *liable*, with the Genitive it is *partaker*, as in the Prologue of the Son of Sirach: ***τούτων ἔνοχοι γενόμενοι***, having been made partakers of these things.

<!-- p.176 -->

*Rhaca* seemeth to be derived either from *Rak*, spittle, or saliva, as it were, one worthy to be spat upon, fit to be spit at; or from *Rik*, empty, vain, idle, profitable to no man, ***ἐτώσιον ἄχθος ἀρούρης*** (a fruitless burden of the earth), a windbag void of brain, a buffoon, a man without shame and modesty, ***χέτλιος πτῶμα*** (a wretched carcase), a dead body, which can be turned to no use. Either is reproachful and enormous, hurting the fame of our neighbour. Therefore it is also worthy to be brought before the ***συνέδριον***, that is, before the higher Senate, or the College of the seventy elders instituted in **Num. 11**. Others understand *Raka* to be only an outward gesture in the countenance, whereby we signify the flames of anger and hatred kindled in the heart. Of *Gehenna* it hath been spoken above, which is so called as it were the valley of Hinnom, or *Ge-Ben-Hinnom*, the valley of the son of Hinnom. For it was a most pleasant place near Jerusalem, between the east and the south, planted with trees and watered with springs. Thither both dead bodies and other uncleannesses were cast forth, and were burned with fire, by the appointment of King Josiah, so that a most pleasant place was defiled with exceeding stench. In this valley there was horrible idolatry, the worship of the idol Moloch, as we have said above.

Now it appeareth that in the time of Christ the manner of speaking was received, and that hell was then scarce otherwise called than *gehenna*. Now let us consider the words of Christ and the meaning of them.

Christ opposeth these words not to the commandment of Moses, but to the common figment of the Scribes, who thought that, if only they refrained from outward slaughters, the fifth Commandment was already satisfied, and that nothing further need be laboured for. But Christ urgeth greater things. For the words of the Commandment are not, *"Let not thy hand kill"*, but, *"THOU shalt not kill"*; that is, thou, so far as thou art a man, in soul and body, thy heart, thy mouth, thy tongue, all thy powers, senses, and whatsoever thou hast joined with the body, whereby slaughter taketh its beginning, is cherished, nourished, accomplished and executed. And the word, *Thou shalt kill*, is not to be taken so strictly and narrowly as to signify only the slaughters <!-- p.177 --> wherein bodies are made away with and spoiled of life, but whatsoever is done besides, from which afterward, so far as in thee lieth, slaughter may follow; as, when thou hatest any man, and wouldest not have him relieved with the least succour, as the rich man is toward Lazarus. Such a one might perish through thee, as **Ambrose** saith: *"If thou hast not fed him, thou hast killed him."* And **1 John 3**: *"Whosoever hateth his brother is a murderer: and ye know that no murderer hath eternal life abiding in him."*

Now Christ showeth that sin is committed against the fifth Commandment in four ways. First, in the heart, when this is stirred with anger and indignation against a neighbour. Next, when such a heart bewrayeth itself outwardly, and by gesture putteth forth an angry sign, as when we pass by with averted countenance, neither saluting nor answering. Afterward, when anger cometh forth out of the heart into the mouth,[^P08-2] when men are evil spoken of, and their good fame is detracted from. The fourth is the worst, when all things run together: an angry heart, an ill-boding motion, an angry tongue, an angry hand, when violence is undertaken, slaughters are committed, or at the least a cruel will is declared. And although these things have differences of heinousness, as Christ Himself declareth this by the difference of the penalties (whereby the Stoics are refuted, who say that all sins are equal), yet all alike, small and great, are sins against this Commandment. And he that hath offended in the least is a murderer before God. And Christ alludeth to earthly judgements for this cause, that He may testify that God will be the judge even of hidden anger, to punish it. And because he goeth further who bewrayeth his indignation by bitter speech, He saith that he shall be arraigned before the whole heavenly assembly, to bear a heavier punishment. But those who shall have broken forth even to revilings, them He adjudgeth to the fire of Gehenna, signifying that hatred, and whatsoever is contrary to charity, sufficeth for the guilt of eternal death, although no violence be added thereto.

In the first member Christ forbiddeth anger, that is, both wrathfulness itself, and the desire of vengeance, and envy or spite, ***φιλονεικίαν*** or contentiousness, and <!-- p.178 --> commandeth patience, meekness, clemency, ***φιλησυχίαν***, or the study of concord, friendship, faithfulness, candour.

In the second member He forbiddeth pride and insolency in gesture, gait and speech, grimness, frowardness, and courtly lightness, which promiseth all things and performeth nothing; and commandeth humanity, affability, courtesy, beneficence and goodness.

In the third member He forbiddeth slanders, backbitings, tale-bearings, evil-speaking, scurrility and doltishness, and commandeth truth, civility and concord. Of these, both the virtues and the vices, it must be spoken elsewhere. But of wrathfulness let somewhat be said in this place.

Anger is a work of the flesh, which whoso doeth cannot come into the kingdom of God, **Galat. 5**. Now it is to be considered:

> Of Anger:  
> Whence it ariseth.  
> Whether it be always evil.  
> How it is to be restrained.  

I. If the nature of man had remained whole, there would be no need of these Questions. For nothing would be known of anger. Now, in this depravation of nature, it is too well known how great a burden anger is, so that **Menander** hath rightly said:

> ***Ὅσος τὸ κατέχειν ἐστὶ τὴν ὀργὴν πόνος.***  

*"O how great is the labour of restraining anger."* Now anger ariseth in us for the most part out of impatience; as, when either we or others are offended, or something is done which displeaseth us, then there is felt in us a certain motion of the heart, which is both sorrowful and desirous of vengeance, so that the heart both turneth away from that offence and withal endeavoureth to drive it off, and therefore would as it were break forth out of its own camp, and sendeth out the blood and the spirits like soldiers to repel the object. Hence the actions of the members are troubled, and the brain is hurt by reason of the motion of the blood and the confusion of the spirits. It is profitable to consider these things.

II. But sometimes anger ariseth out of righteousness, and sometimes out of godly <!-- p.179 --> zeal. And then it hath the name not of a vice, but of a virtue, as it is said in the verses of **Sosiphanes**:

> ***Νῦν σοὶ πρὸς ὄψιν θυμὸς ἡβάτω γέρον,***  
> ***Νυνὶ δεῖ ὀργὴν ἡνίκ' ἔνδικον λαβεῖν.***  
> Now, O old man, let wrath grow green,  
> For it becometh to be angry for righteousness' sake.  

And thus God is angry out of righteousness, when He punisheth men for their wickednesses, disobedience and stubbornness, either with temporal or with eternal penalties, as when He first punished Adam and Eve transgressing the commandment, etc. And thus David also speaketh: *"I hated them with perfect hatred"*, namely, for the glory of God, and the love of righteousness against ungodliness and filthiness. As Phinehas is angry, and therefore for his zeal the Priesthood is promised to him. And Jehu is praised, who saith: *"See my zeal for the Lord."* And elsewhere it is said:

> ***Ζῆλος ἐμὲ ζαθέοιο τεοῦ καταδαίνυτο οἴκου.***  

*"The zeal of thine house hath eaten me up"*; I burned with a great vehemency to defend thine house. With this affection Elijah and Elisha slew the ungodly, and the Prophets curse the ungodly. Peter slayeth Ananias. Christ casteth out them that sold and bought. And in heroical men there is a certain like vehemency to defend righteousness, to punish filthiness, to guard the Commonwealth. This affection the Philosophers have called *Nemesis*, because it chiefly pertaineth to governors, who ought to be shepherds and distributors of righteousness, from ***νέμω*** (I distribute). But heed must be taken, lest under the pretext of such zeal anger become unrighteous, as often cometh to pass at this day; as when we are angry in private causes, and hold others in hatred, being inflamed with the desire of vengeance, not principally for the glory of God, or the love of righteousness, or the hatred of filthiness, but because our own commodities are snatched from us and our pleasures are disturbed. Hence the boys say:

> Often wickedness troubleth heaven under the veil of zeal.  

as at this day examples everywhere in the Church do testify.

Hither pertaineth also the mention of wars. War ought to be a defence lawful and blameless, that without wrong, in peace, <!-- p.180 --> the Magistrate and the subjects may be able to live, as **Cassiodorus** saith most rightly: *"Then only is it profitable to run together to arms, when righteousness can find no place with the adversary."* But when war is undertaken out of anger, impatience, the desire of vengeance, the desire of getting more goods, or *"for the sake of Empire and riches"*, as **Sallust** speaketh, success is seldom found, as infinite examples testify. But of wars let it be spoken elsewhere. God be with us, that there be no need to say much of wars.

III. But how anger is to be restrained, although reason seemeth to give counsel, yet the word of God showeth best what is to be done. The Heathen teach that reason is to be consulted, as **Seneca** saith out of **Aristotle**: *"Anger is to be used, not as a Captain, but as a soldier."* And worthy of memory are the words of **Seneca** with which he concludeth his books *Of Anger*, where among other things he saith thus: *"Nothing will more avail against anger than the thought of mortality. Let each man say to himself, as to another: What doth it profit to proclaim[^P08-3] our angers, as though we were born for eternity, and to scatter abroad a most short life? What doth it profit to bestow upon another's grief and torment the days which we may spend upon honest pleasure? These things admit of no waste, nor have we time to lose. Why do we rush into the fight? Why do we fetch strifes upon ourselves? Why, forgetful of our weakness, do we take up huge hatreds, and, being brittle, rise up to break others? Presently those enmities, which we bear with implacable mind, a fever or some other ill of the body shall forbid to be borne. Presently death coming between shall part the fiercest pair. Why do we make tumult, and seditiously trouble our life? Fate standeth over our head, and reckoneth up the days as they perish, and cometh nearer and nearer. That time which thou appointest for another's death is perchance near to thine own. Why dost thou not rather gather up thy short life, and make it peaceable both to thyself and to others? Why art thou angry with others? Bear a little. Behold, death cometh, to make us equal. We are wont to see, among the morning spectacles of the arena, the fight of a bull and a bear tied together, whom, when the one hath harried the other, their own dispatcher awaiteth. <!-- p.181 --> We do the same. We provoke one that is bound together with us, while an end equally near hangeth over the vanquished and the victor. Let us rather pass in quietness and peace the little that remaineth. Let not our dead body lie hateful to any man. Whether thou thinkest of the extreme punishments of him whom thou hatest, or of lighter ones, how little a time is it, wherein either he may be tormented with his penalty, or thou mayest reap an evil joy from another's case? Even now we are spitting out our very breath, while we draw it. While we are among men, let us practise humanity; let us be a fear to no man, a peril to no man; let us despise losses, wrongs, revilings, carpings, and bear with a great mind our short discomforts. While we look back, as they say, and turn ourselves about, immortality will be at hand."* And **Suidas** saith:

> ***μωρία ἐστὶ χαρίζεσθαι τῇ ὀργῇ τῷ ἀχαριστοτάτῳ πάθει.***  

*"It is madness to gratify anger, a most thankless affection."* And the prudence of **Heraclitus** and **Democritus** is greatly celebrated, of whom the one wept instead of being angry, the other laughed instead of being angry. Now let there move us to restrain anger: **I.** The commandment of God: *"Be ye angry, and sin not."* *"Let not the sun go down upon your wrath."* *"Let all indignation and anger be put away from you."* **II.** The penalties, bodily and eternal. For the powers of body and mind are weakened by overmuch wrathfulness; thence arise frenzies, as in Ajax, apoplexy, sudden death, the falling sickness; and the actions of all the members are hindered, sins are increased, and blasphemies against God and our neighbour, contempt of God, and murmuring, as may be seen in ungodly ***στρατιώταις*** (soldiers). **III.** The consideration of our nature, which was created unto gentleness and unto life. Where anger is, there is death; where meekness is, there is life. And **Seneca** saith rightly: *"Nothing is great which is not gentle."* Likewise:

> The greater any man is, the more appeasable is he in his anger.  

For the more excellent natures are, the milder they are; the weaker they are, the more desirous they are of vengeance, as **Juvenal** saith of women:

> — Forasmuch as vengeance  
> <!-- p.182 --> Is ever the pleasure of a petty and feeble and little mind:  
> Gather it straightway from this, that in revenge  
> None rejoiceth more than a woman.  

**IIII.** The example of God, of Christ and of the Saints. God forgiveth us our sins. Christ saith: *"Learn of me; for I am meek and lowly in heart."* **V.** Our own infirmities and sins, for which we have need of divine and human forgiveness, patience and meekness. Wherefore, since we would have others be kind to us, let us also be kind, as **Nazianzen** admonisheth:

> ***Εἰδ' οἶδας ὄφλων, καὶ πρόσχρησον τὸ πρᾶον,***  
> ***Οἴκτῳ γὰρ οἶκτος καὶ θεῷ σταθμίζεται.***  
> If thou knowest that thou art a debtor to other mortals,  
> And that others forgive thee thy blemishes,  
> Do thou also use meekness toward others:  
> For toward the meek God also is a meek father.  

And it is rightly wont to be said: *"He easily giveth pardon, who hath need of pardon."*
