## *De Tertio, id est, de libertate voluntatis in res sibi subiectas* · Of the Third Point, that is, of the Liberty of the Will over the Things Subject to It

A man, even unregenerate, can in some manner render outward discipline, and do outward honest works which agree with the Law of God, as experience testifieth in political, domestic and Ecclesiastical administration and in the whole of life; the Examples of the Heathen also testify it; and **Paul** likewise testifieth the same, **Rom. 1** and **2**. Now of this liberty there are two hindrances, most grievous and most frequent, namely: the stubbornness of the affections, which often of their own accord, often also by reason of certain objects, rush against the judgement of the mind and carry off the will to their own side; and the tyranny of the Devil, who is effectual in the ungodly, and in manifold ways hindereth the governance of discipline, and driveth many to rush blind and raging into the most sorrowful evils, as did Cain, Saul, Judas and others without number. And those great furies in the worship of idols, in the cruelty of tyrants, and of civil wars, seditions, discords, blasphemies, lusts, and bloody and violent counsels, are manifest works of the Devil, *"which destroy body and soul"*, to use the words of **D. Luther**, who with a deep groan, when he had read a letter of **Vitus Theodorus**,[^P16-1] had said: *"Alas, how Satan rageth, and driveth secure men to horrible crimes, which destroy body and soul."* Now in that letter there had been related a tragical case of a certain widow, who, having been got with child by a scholar, when the infant, carried to Baptism, had been sent back home to its mother without baptism by the Priest, who demanded that the father of the infant be declared to him, was moved by the indignity of the thing and stirred up by the furies, and both slew the infant and straightway hanged herself; which being discovered, the father of the infant also ran himself through; and the Priest, learning what tragedies <!-- p.322 --> had arisen from his own fault and his vehement demanding, hanged himself also. And the like things happen everywhere in the human race, which ought to rouse us to humility, and to the fear and invocation of God; even as the Poets gathered from tragical histories of this kind their thesis, which they would have to stand for the argument of all tragedies, and for a rule in the whole of life: *"Being warned, learn ye righteousness, and not to despise the gods."* For the Heathen also observed that there are many and great hindrances, whereby civil and outward discipline and honesty is the less cherished and kept with that love, honour and zeal with which it ought to be, and they bewailed the blindness and weakness of man. Hence were written so many tragedies, of **Aeschylus**, **Sophocles**, **Euripides**, and among the Latins of **Seneca**; and to the same end there are extant the Odes of **Pindar**, the monuments of **Homer**, of **Hesiod** and the like, than whose writings, in the doctrine of the Law and in human life, after the Sacred Scriptures, nothing more profitable, better, weightier and sweeter can be found.

Now this also must of necessity be added: as often as mention is made of the liberty of the human will, whether in natural things — namely in meat, drink, sleep, waking, speech, walking, motion, rest, gestures, and the like things, which are most of all according to nature, and in the producing whereof the understanding is not greatly occupied — or in civil things, which regard and conduce to the civil and outward life, discipline, polity, household government and common society, there must always and diligently be laid down and held fast the difference between the unregenerate man and the regenerate. For in each the powers of free will (*liberum arbitrium*) are moved after a different manner. For in natural things the unregenerate man — who nevertheless, without at least the general help of God, sustaining and preserving nature, not sin, can do nothing in them (**Acts 17**: *"For in him we live, and move, and have our being"*) — erreth often in manifold ways, and most rarely holdeth the mean, while he rusheth now into excesses, presently into defects; whence many grievous sins, stubbornness, rebellion, impatience, <!-- p.323 --> hatred, violence, whoredom, adultery, theft, surfeiting, riotous living, lying, slander, backbiting, and others of that kind arise; to which, nevertheless, God hath set a measure and a bound, that such a man may not go on further than hath seemed good to the divine ordinance; even as experience also testifieth that the attempts and endeavours of secure men are often hindered, turned aside, broken off, and wonderfully confounded, as it is wont to be said: *Man proposeth, God disposeth.* And the proverbs are well known: To fail upon the threshold. To run aground in the haven. The nag in the gateway. The turning of the potsherd. Many things lie between. *Now it raineth, and now Jupiter shineth in the clear heaven.* *Many things fall between the cup and the edge of the lips.* Between the hand and the chin. Between the mouth and the morsel. The flower is ashes. Many wheels shall yet turn. *The day itself is sometimes a mother, and sometimes a stepmother.* From the oar to the judgement seat. Back to the former mangers. The catastrophe of the play, etc. And it must be confessed that many things are fated and ordained, as the punishments of wicked deeds, as is truly said in the Greek verses:

> ***Εἱμαρμένον γὰρ τῶν κακῶν βουλευμάτων***  
> ***Κακὰς ἀμοιβὰς ἐστὶ καρποῦσθαι βροτοῖς.***  
> (For it is fated that of evil counsels  
> mortals should reap evil recompenses.)

And it often cometh to pass, as is commonly said: that a man is the smith even of his own fortune, and that he who spitteth out the wine drinketh at last the dregs. Now although in the regenerate also there cleaveth great uncleanness and weakness, insomuch that not even they can be shut out from common adversities and from events oftentimes unlooked for, yet the regenerate man not only is able by nature, being aided by the general help of God, to understand, will, choose and do somewhat, or to refuse and reject it, but is also so moved by the Spirit of God in his understanding and will, that he setteth about all things more religiously and more circumspectly, and directeth even his natural actions to the glory of God, to necessary uses and his own welfare, to the profit of the Church or of his neighbour, and always acknowledgeth his own weakness, defect and excess, and bewaileth human infirmity, and daily asketh of God pardon and forgiveness, and doth not of ***προαιρέσει*** (set purpose) indulge evil affections, depraved appetite, and <!-- p.324 --> abuse. This, namely, is what **Paul** said, **1 Corinth. 10**: *"Whether ye eat, or drink, or whatsoever ye do, do all to the glory of God."* And what the prayer of the godly is, that saying of Solomon showeth, **Proverb. 30**: *"Two things have I required of thee; deny me them not before I die: Remove far from me vanity and lies: give me neither poverty nor riches; feed me with food convenient for me: lest I be full, and deny thee, and say, Who is the Lord? or lest I be poor, and steal, and take the name of my God in vain."* Next, in civil and ethical[^P16-2] or moral actions and disciplines, although the unregenerate man can understand, will and perform somewhat, and hath clear notions (*notitiae*) born with him, or principles speculative and practical, as **Paul** expressly testifieth, **Rom. 2**, and can attain the knowledge of many arts and disciplines, frame honest laws, and present excellent and specious virtues: yet, being left to himself and to his own carnal judgement, he always seweth on to those notions, studies and virtues very much of errors and vices, as the experience of all times testifieth, and **Paul** saith, **Rom. 1**: *"They became vain in their imaginations: professing themselves to be wise, they became fools."* And in the book of Wisdom, **chap. 13**, the absurd and idolatrous institutions of the nations are recounted. But on the contrary the regenerate man in civil actions, being aided by the special help of the Holy Spirit, enjoyeth a course and success that is ordered, happy and prosperous, all things being directed to this end, that the glory of God may be made illustrious, the true doctrine of the Law and of the Gospel propagated, godly and honest discipline preserved, the oppressed, the needy and others counselled and succoured, and that all things may be done orderly, rightly, modestly, peaceably, temperately and sincerely, according to the rule of the word of God and the leading of the Holy Spirit, without the invocation, presence and help of whom the regenerate begin, take in hand, or accomplish nothing whatsoever. And what the prayer of the godly is in the discharging of these civil actions, **Psal. 119** showeth at length and excellently, and the **9th chap. of the book of Wisdom**.

<!-- p.325 -->

## *De Quarto, id est, de contingentia in actionibus vitiosis* · Of the Fourth Point, that is, of Contingency in Vicious Actions

**Augustine** saith several times that the unregenerate man hath free will in evil things, or in sins, but lacketh free will in good things. These words, rightly understood, are true. For man did not sin, nor doth he sin, by compulsion, or by a certain fatal necessity, but he sinneth freely, obeying both his own affection and the persuasion of the devil. God, moreover, neither willeth, nor effecteth, nor helpeth, nor approveth sin, but forbiddeth and punisheth it severely, and determineth the bounds how far and how long, by His own most free permission and by His forsaking of the ungodly, He will bear with those sins. But the unregenerate man hath not free will in good things, because, namely, he is the servant of sin, not yet made free, **Rom. 6**. And **Bernard** saith: *"The evil will is free, in that it is evil, and it is servile by this very thing, that it is evil."* In this manner of speaking of **Augustine** and **Bernard**, *free in evil things* is set over against *compelled*, not against *enslaved*. And, that I may add this also, the disputations are plainly diverse, and to be understood in a diverse respect, when *free* is set over against either *compelled* or *enslaved*. *Man hath free will in evil things.* Here *free*, set over against *compelled*, showeth that a man, if he commit any evil, ought to ascribe it to himself and to his own voluntary choice, and that he doth not sin being compelled by God, or by a certain fatal necessity, or God so willing it and instigating the man to sin, seeing that God is not the cause of any sin: although He suffereth sins to be done in a certain measure and within a certain bound, as in **Acts 14** it is manifestly written of that permission: *"God in times past suffered the nations to walk in their own ways"*; and **Psal. 81**: *"My people would not hearken to my voice; and Israel would none of me. So I gave them up unto their own hearts' lust, that they might walk in their own counsels."* And in no wise let us, in a Church now rightly instructed, suffer to be snatched from us that most profitable and most necessary explication or doctrine of the permission of God in the hardening, the blinding and <!-- p.326 --> the destruction of the ungodly; likewise, of contingency, and of the necessity of the consequence (*necessitas consequentiae*): which terms or Phrases of speech many plainly mad men at this day, doing despite unto God, would snatch from us, and endeavour to transfer all things, as well evil as good, all actions and events, good and evil, unto God, as unto their fountain and cause. Away and farewell with the Stoic blasphemies, which affirm that all things are done by absolute necessity, and feign that man, even in his worst actions, cannot do otherwise than as he is ruled and led by his rider. Let us not be so blasphemous, mad and unthankful, but let us love and reverence God, and seek His glory in our thoughts, sayings, writings and deeds, and let us spare God Himself, and our own conscience and salvation, and the ears of the godly, to whom blasphemous speeches of this kind are poison and death. But of these things it will perhaps be needful to speak elsewhere. Man therefore hath in sinning a will that is free, or spontaneous, which is not compelled, nor by outward motion violently borne, drawn, snatched and driven unwilling to sin. But man hath not free will in good things: not, to wit, that he here doeth that which is good by compulsion, if only he do it; but that he is sold under sin, carried off into bondage, even as captives were sold and made slaves, **Rom. 7**, and cannot free himself, until the Son of God pay the ***ἀντίλυτρον*** (ransom). And the saying of **Bernard** is worthy of remembrance: *"If we would discern, being left to ourselves, between good and evil, we are deceived; if we attempt to do good, we fail; if we endeavour to resist evil, we are cast down and overcome,"* etc., *"unless we be borne up on the wings of Christ."* But others have set free will over against the enslaved will. Concerning this, therefore, there now ariseth another question, which shall be declared in what followeth.

<!-- p.327 -->

## *De Quinto, id est, de libero hominis arbitrio, et serva voluntate in motibus, actionibus, et operibus divinis* · Of the Fifth Point, that is, of the Free Will of Man, and of the Enslaved Will, in Divine Motions, Actions and Works

Hitherto it hath been said in what things and how far man hath free will, namely, as touching the substance of the mind and will; next, as touching the difference whereby man is distinguished from the other creatures that are void of reason; afterward, as touching civil and outward discipline; fourthly, as touching liberty in respect of compulsion. For if *free* is set over against *compelled*, the will of man is truly free. For a compelled will is not properly and in very deed a will; and necessity giveth place to will. But now the question is of spiritual motions and actions, whether the unregenerate man can do and effect anything in them; that is, whether a man can convert himself to God, believe, and begin and render true godliness, even as the word of God prescribeth and requireth conversion, faith and godliness. That answer may be rightly made to this Question, we shall briefly consider fifteen Propositions. **I.** The unregenerate man, in actions inward, spiritual, divine and heavenly, can perceive nothing with the understanding, choose nothing with the will, and by his natural powers begin, render or accomplish nothing, as manifest testimonies against the Pelagians and the monks are everywhere extant. **1 Cor. 2**: *"The natural man receiveth not the things of the Spirit of God."* **2 Corinth. 3**: *"Not that we are sufficient of ourselves to think any thing as of ourselves; but if we are sufficient unto any thing, it is of God."* **Rom. 8**: *"The carnal mind is enmity against God: for it is not subject to the law of God, neither indeed can be."* **John 3**: *"That which is born of the flesh is flesh."* **Rom. 7**: *"I know that in me (that is, in my flesh,) dwelleth no good thing."* And **8**: *"They that are after the flesh do mind the things of the flesh."* **Eph. 2**: *"We were by nature the children of wrath,"* etc.

**II.** There is therefore in the unregenerate man no preparation <!-- p.328 --> for the receiving of grace, no understanding or voluntary choice of spiritual good, nay, not even any first motion toward spiritual good, as arising from the man himself. And hence have sprung the phrases:

In spiritual actions, the unregenerate man

- Hath not free will.
- Can do nothing, effecteth nothing, nor meriteth anything.
- Is not apt or fit for grace.
- Hath no manner of working (*modus agendi*) from his own nature.
- Hath no synergy (*synergia*).
- Beareth himself merely passively (*mere passive*) before grace.
- Hath a nature that resisteth, and beareth itself stubbornly.
- Hath only an enslaved will (*servum arbitrium*).
- Hath not the faculty of rightly obeying God.
- Cannot truly assent to the word of God.
- His will is an unwillingness (*noluntas*), etc.

These and the like phrases are used by some, often without discernment and understanding of the respect and of the end unto which they were of old uttered and used by great and truly God-taught men. Now it is true that man, as pertaineth to preparation for grace, likewise to right assent, and to efficacy and merit, or to righteousness, true faith, true conversion and salvation, is, as they call it, a patient subject (*subiectum patiens*). But this also must be known, that man is one thing, and another creature, either irrational or void of sense, is another thing. Man indeed hath nothing whereby he may either prepare himself for salvation, which he by his own reason neither knoweth nor understandeth, or merit salvation (for which cause all human reason is deservedly and rightly made little of and set aside by men and Theologians who were of old great); but he hath, as shall be said below, that whereby he may receive the benefits of God by the help and motion of the Holy Spirit, and he doth not bear himself toward the election of God as a stock or a stone, etc. But that the carnal man is sometimes named a stone, a stock, clay, a beast of burden, wood, a carcass, blind, dead, this is done figuratively and metaphorically, that it may be shown <!-- p.329 --> that the unregenerate man is as little able of his own nature and faculty, without the Holy Spirit, to begin and accomplish any of the spiritual actions — seeing that he is wholly, in soul and body, infected and defiled with the original leprosy — as a stone is able of its own nature to prepare, form or fit itself to the intention of the craftsman.

**III.** Since therefore man could of his own powers, after the fall, do nothing in spiritual actions, God, who made man not for destruction, but for life and salvation, having compassion on us, hath shown and proved Himself unto us a faithful Father, Physician and ***ἀλεξίκακον*** (averter of evil), by the promise, by the written word as by a handwriting of the things promised, and by the sending of the Son, the gift of the Holy Spirit, and the institution and use of the Sacraments and of the whole ministry. For thus doth God raise us up again, deliver, cure, heal, enlighten, quicken, lead, rule, preserve, save. Us, His enemies, He calleth by His word and inviteth to reconciliation, peace and friendship; us, the children of wrath, He receiveth into grace, and maketh us beloved in the Beloved; us, dead in sins, He quickeneth, and adorneth with the righteousness and life of His Son; us, polluted in soul and body, He cleanseth with the blood of His Son; us, blind and wandering, He enlighteneth, and bringeth back into life; us, deaf, He rouseth, and pierceth and openeth our ears, that we may hearken; us, dumb,[^P16-3] He furnisheth with speech, and openeth our lips to show forth His praises; us, weak, He strengtheneth and healeth; us, incapable, He maketh capable, and bendeth and changeth us by the word, by faith and by the Holy Spirit; us, condemned, He snatcheth out of the jaws of the devil; us, sticking fast in the deep mire, He draweth out with His own hand and power; us, destitute of counsels, He governeth; the hungry He feedeth, the thirsty He giveth to drink, the naked He clotheth, them that are exposed to perils He delivereth, the weary He refresheth, the unlearned He teacheth, the fainting He strengtheneth, the suffering He comforteth, them that groan He kisseth, them that pray He heareth, them that are in pain and sick He cureth, the dying He endoweth with eternal life and gladness; and all these things He doeth of mere mercy and goodness, only for the Son's sake, that every one that glorieth may glory in the Lord alone, not in himself, or in others. And for this cause God preserveth the <!-- p.330 --> ministry of the word and Sacraments in the human race, that the body of Christ may be edified, till we all come in the unity of the faith and of the knowledge of the Son of God, **Ephes. 4**.

**IIII.** Now God requireth nothing else of us, but that we hear the Son, and believe on Him: which if we shall do, we ought to be certain of our righteousness and eternal salvation, because it is written: *"Hear ye him."* And: *"He that believeth on the Son hath everlasting life."* And the Son saith: *"Come unto me, all ye that labour and are heavy laden, and I will give you rest."* Let us therefore come and hear the Gospel of Christ, that we may be refreshed and live, and let us include ourselves, all and each, in the universal voice and promise; let us not dispute of particularity, or of the eternal predestination of God outside the word, but let us abide in the revealed word, and look upon God revealed, and hear the voice of our Shepherd, who calleth thee and me and us all, none excepted, and willeth us to come, to hear and to believe. In this universal voice thou oughtest to include thyself, and not to feign in God contradictory wills, nor to charge Him with a lie, nor to search out other things besides those which He Himself hath made manifest to thee by the giving of the word and the sending of the Son.

**V.** God willeth of His ***εὐδοκίᾳ*** (good pleasure) that all men be saved, and that none at all perish, only this being set forth as it were for a condition — not a legal one, which hangeth upon works, but an Evangelical one, hanging upon mere divine grace — namely, that we believe on the Son. For as all are concluded under sin, none excepted, so God hath mercy upon all, none excepted, who believe on the Son. This goodness is poured out unto all and upon all, and the promise is universal, and the oath of God is holy and immovable: *"As I live, I will not the death of a sinner, but that he be converted, and live."* And Christ willeth all nations to be made His disciples, saying: ***Μαθητεύσατε πάντα τὰ ἔθνη*** (make disciples of all the nations). God therefore willeth that we be converted unto His word, believe on the Son, and be saved. This is His ***εὐδοκία*** (good pleasure), and the will of His mercy, revealed in the Gospel. But that many are called, but few chosen, cometh to pass by the <!-- p.331 --> fault of men. And this saying speaketh of the effect, not of the cause, of election, and comforteth us, that we be not offended at the fewness of them that believe. For few receive the offered promise of the Gospel, even as Christ, groaning and weeping, saith: *"How often would I have gathered thee, and thou wouldest not!"*

**VI.** From these things it is clear that outside and beside the word and Sacraments there ought to be no speculating or speaking of the will of God, of predestination and of other secret things, nor are enthusiastic raptures to be looked for, or new motions without the word preached, heard, read, pondered. For in conversion new motions are not imprinted upon the mind, will and heart in such wise as a seal is imprinted upon wax, so that no new motions follow in the mind, will and heart, and there is plainly no action or any motion of the mind, will and heart — as a brute beast is driven, as when Balaam's ass speaketh, or as a stone is violently thrust forward, or a ball is shot forth out of a Bombard, or as of old the possessed gave forth oracles and prophecies which they themselves neither understood nor remembered, and as the devil useth the members and tongue and eyes of the possessed, often without their will and knowledge. Wherefore let us cleave to the word, and according to the doctrine of the Gospel let us judge, think and speak of this whole matter, and of the other things pertaining to righteousness, life and salvation.

**VII.** Now it must be diligently considered what the voice of the Gospel saith and setteth before us, namely: **I.** That we all labour and are heavy laden, and that no man can snatch and deliver himself out of his miseries, sin and eternal death, either by his own powers and merits or by those of other creatures. **II.** That GOD, having compassion on us, hath given us His Only-begotten Son, and willeth that we hear Him and believe on Him, and so, and not otherwise, through faith in the Son be saved and have eternal life. **III.** That, whereas even in that very thing wherein we ought to believe on the Son we are weak and faint, insufficient and cold, He Himself willeth to kindle in us, through the word heard and the Holy Spirit, faith <!-- p.332 --> and consolation, if only we hear the word, and receive the benefits offered through it, or at least will and endeavour to receive them, and do not, whether by overmuch security, or by shameful sloth, or by Epicurean contempt, resist the motions of the Holy Spirit, who willeth to work upon us through the word; but, the word being heard, know that it is not preached to us in vain, and that it ought not to return void to the throne of God, nor vanish into the air, but is preached to this end, that thou mayest hear, read and ponder it, and so through the word preached, heard, read and pondered the Holy Spirit may be effectual in thee, and may kindle, and from time to time confirm, true faith and consolation, and seal it in thy mind and in thy heart, seeing that He Himself is the earnest (*arrhabo*), that is, the pledge and surety ratifying and confirming the suretyship or promise, that sure and undoubted credit may be given to it. But if thou hearest, readest, ponderest the word, and endeavourest to lay hold of the benefits offered therein (which thou oughtest and canst do, even though thou name it only a carnal will and endeavour, such as was in Nicodemus, and such as must needs be present), then thou art not forsaken, nor art thou now left alone, but straightway and always the Holy Spirit is present through the word, is in thy mind and heart, worketh and effecteth a true and right and spiritual assent, and out of the word which thou hast heard with thine ears and considered with thy thought, and to which thou hast willed with thy will to assent at least in some manner, even carnally, He kindleth in thee new motions of repentance, of invocation, of thanksgiving, of faith and of new obedience. But if thou neither hearest, nor considerest, nor readest the word, nor appliest any endeavour, though but a carnal one, and a carnal desire of assenting to the word heard, but always bearest thyself toward the word resistingly, and stubbornly exercisest the utmost enmities with God, then the Holy Spirit is in no wise effectual in thee, because He findeth thee not only by nature a foe and enemy of God, and a child of wrath, but also findeth thee an ***ἄσπονδον ἐχθρὸν*** (implacable enemy), who canst be brought to concord by no peacemaking, by no means, by no promises, by no covenant and compact, by no invitation, by no threatenings even, <!-- p.333 --> and lastly by no gift, such as God the Father hath given, the highest and most precious, namely His only-begotten Son. But how great a wickedness this is — thus stubbornly to resist the word and the Holy Spirit, and to apply no wish, desire or endeavour that, the word being heard, we may be able to assent, but to feign enthusiastic motions and raptures, and compulsion, or a certain fatal necessity, and to speculate of the persons and the number of the persons predestinated, and in the mean while to set aside the ministry of the word and Sacraments, or at least to esteem it the less — let godly minds, I pray, consider; and the grievousness of this evil will surely be made manifest in the last judgement, in which the matter shall no longer be of original sin only, and of the Law, or of thy nature, corrupt and guilty of death and damnation; but, since all judgement is given to the Son, it shall be asked concerning this, why thou wouldest not believe on the Son of God set before thee and sent of the Father; and sentence shall be given according to these two sayings: *"He that believeth on the Son hath everlasting life"*: *"He that believeth not, the wrath of God abideth on him,"* and he *"is condemned already"* — to wit, not so much because of the original evil, and other sins, as because of unbelief. For on account of unbelief shall all be damned, and not on account of the depravation of nature, the leprosy or the guilt, which the Son of God turneth away upon Himself from all who hear Him and believe. It shall be said therefore: Because thou hast not believed, thou art damned. But thou shalt not be able to say in thy turn: I could not believe, because faith was not given, and Thou wouldest not kindle it in me. Thou liest, thou unbeliever. For *"the word is nigh thee, even in thy mouth, and in thy heart: that is, the word of faith, which we preach."* Likewise: *"Faith cometh by hearing."*

**VIII.** But some man will say: Faith is assuredly the gift of God, as it is written, **John 6**, **Ephes. 2**, **Coloss. 2**, **Philip. 1** (*"Unto you it is given to believe on Christ"*), **1 Pet. 1**: *"Ye are kept by the power of God through faith unto salvation."* And: *"Without me ye can do nothing,"* **John 14**. And **Jerem. 31**: *"Turn thou me, and I shall be turned; for thou art the Lord my God."* Likewise: *"It is God which worketh in you both to will and to do,"* etc. We answer: <!-- p.334 --> Let him be Anathema who thinketh or speaketh otherwise. For in no manner and by no means can we have faith of ourselves. And foul is the opinion, and deceitful the persuasion, of all who suppose that they can by their own powers or faculties, as often only and whensoever they will, believe and obey God. And it is certain that this foolish persuasion ariseth by the instigation of Satan, and striketh hurtful roots, and degenerateth into security, and into the abuse of this most holy name, which is Faith, as experience testifieth. But this is what we say: That the means or instrument through which the Holy Spirit is effectual, and worketh in the mind, will and heart of men, is none other than the word heard, read and pondered by men. And God willeth that this word of His Law and Gospel be heard, read and pondered, and He will not otherwise give the Holy Spirit. Now this thou oughtest and canst hear, read, and in some manner ponder; which if thou doest not, in vain dost thou look for the inward motions of the Holy Spirit. But the affection (*affectus*) only is one thing, the effect (*effectus*) another, namely, out of hearing to conceive faith and other motions, and rightly to will, rightly to assent, and to obey God, to begin a new obedience acceptable to God, or to render worships acceptable to God. Here it is necessary that the grace of God and the Holy Spirit be present. For faith is the gift of God, yet not an enthusiastic or immediate gift, but one which is given in a sure and lawful order, that is, it requireth the obedience and subjection of our will, namely the hearing, reading and consideration of the word, as the Son of God Himself saith: *"No man cometh unto me, except the Father draw him,"* that is, faith is the gift of God. But in what order and manner? *"Every man that heareth of the Father, and learneth, cometh unto me."* And to this purpose it is said: ***ἕλκει θεὸς, ἀλλὰ τὸν βουλόμενον ἕλκει*** (God draweth, but He draweth him that is willing). And **Nazianzen**: ***τὸ κατορθούμενον παρὰ θεοῦ δέδοται τοῖς καλουμένοις καὶ οὕτω νεύουσιν*** (that which is rightly accomplished is given of God to them that are called and that so incline). And: *"Grace going before, the will accompanying."* Likewise: ***μόνον θέλησον, καὶ θεὸς προαπαντᾷ*** (only will, and God cometh beforehand to meet thee). For neither is our will, or our hearing and endeavour, or our zeal and action in these things, any cause of the <!-- p.335 --> motion and working of the Holy Spirit; but God preventeth us, and is present beforehand, offering unto us in His word the benefits of His Son: which word we ought and are able to hear and to consider, and to assent to it in some manner, and at least carnally, as Nicodemus did; which if we do, God doth not forsake that assent of ours, carnal though it be, but rightly instructeth, thoroughly teacheth, confirmeth, directeth, moveth, bendeth it, that we may assent willingly, savingly and rightly, and obey God, and may far more rightly than before hear, read and meditate upon the word of God. For in one manner doth he hear, read and consider the word of God in whom the renewing is begun through the Holy Spirit, and in another manner he who heareth and meditateth upon it without the Spirit.

**IX.** Again some man will say: Are not many often converted by a certain compulsion and as it were violence, as **Paul**, resisting Christ and the Holy Spirit, is converted? Next: Man assuredly by his own nature resisteth God; therefore he cannot have any will, even a carnal one, whereby he may desire or endeavour to assent to the word. Afterward: Something is nevertheless ascribed to man, if it be said that he ought and is able in some manner to assent, although not rightly or savingly, and that he ought to lay hold of and receive the offered good; whereas yet all things are to be ascribed to the grace of God alone, to the merit of the Son alone, to the efficacy of the Holy Spirit alone. To these three objections let this be the brief answer.

### *Responsio ad obiectionem de coactione voluntatis humanae in conversione* · Answer to the Objection of the Compulsion of the Human Will in Conversion

Violent compulsion hath no place at all in the doctrine of the Gospel, or in the conversion of man to God, unless we will understand compulsion of the thunderbolt of the law; which nevertheless is only the term from which (*terminus a quo*), or the beginning, of conversion, as when the sinner is first terrified and cast down by the thunderbolt <!-- p.336 --> of the Law, by which he seemeth as it were to be compelled to confess that he is guilty, and, repenting, to ask forgiveness; which when he doeth, he is now no longer compelled, nor doth he resist, but, as **Paul**, willingly asketh what he ought to do thenceforth. For in the Gospel medicine and healing are shown without any compulsion.

But whereas — that I may add this also — in the Gospel, **Luke 14**, it is written ***ἀνάγκασον***, *compel*, or constrain men, that they may come in and come to my feast, that is done once, and in a parable only, and in that place doth in no wise denote violent compulsion, nor even the inward conversion of a man (because, as **Matthew** showeth, **chap. 22**, when all were present, a certain man among those who were compelled, lacking the wedding garment, that is, not converted, is found out, and is cast into outer darkness), but signifieth only the calling, the invitation, and the ardent entreaty of the outward ministry. For the grace of God is not simply set before us, but to the doctrine there are joined withal the goads of exhortations, entreaties, complaints of our slothfulness, and threatenings, according to that saying, **Jerem. 49**: *"Lament, gird you with sackcloth, and run to and fro by the hedges"*: in which manner of Evangelical invitation there is seen the wonderful goodness of God, who calleth, entreateth, exhorteth, soliciteth and urgeth us, that we may be made citizens and members of the Church, and who therefore also preserveth the estates of parents, of Teachers, of the Magistrate, and the whole order of the ministry, that by a sure discipline and ***νουθεσίᾳ*** (admonition) we may be bent, and kept in obedience, as **Augustine** against the Donatists interpreteth the word *Compelling*. And **Luther** calleth that compulsion and constraint a wonderful one, as it were almost none at all, and saith, marvelling: *"If we will call it compulsion, the compulsion is this, that they themselves wish for nothing else, and cannot rest, unless they be called to a feast so sumptuously furnished,"* etc. Wherefore there is no cause why, in the doctrine of the Gospel, we should use the word *compulsion* of the conversion of man.

<!-- p.337 -->

### *Responsio ad obiectionem de repugnante hominis voluntate* · Answer to the Objection of the Resisting Will of Man

Man resisteth by his own nature, because *"the carnal mind is enmity against God."* But the case is now otherwise, when God setteth forth the voice of His word. For why is the word of God preached, why is it heard and read? Is it not that the rebellion and perverseness of thy nature may be changed, restrained and repressed, and that thou, from an enemy of God by nature, mayest be made a friend and son of God by grace? Hear therefore the word of God, that the Holy Spirit may through it heal and bend the stubbornness of thy nature; which if it be not done, the fault is thine, who obeyest not the word, and it is not the fault of the Holy Spirit, whose instrument, namely the word preached, thou stubbornly refusest and rejectest.

### *Responsio ad obiectionem, aliquid tribui humanae voluntati* · Answer to the Objection, That Something Is Ascribed to the Human Will

All things are ascribed to the grace and mercy of God alone. And all the godly confess that all good things are given of God, and not from elsewhere. Nor is there any man, if only he be sound and godly, who thinketh or speaketh otherwise. And *"Pleasing to God is the mind which confesseth that it oweth whatsoever it hath, whatsoever it possesseth, all to God,"* as is said in the most sweet verses of **Eobanus**. For what have we that we have not received, whether we understand life, soul, body, mind, will, or faculties, powers, members, tongue, eyes, hands, feet, or any other things whatsoever, so far as they are good? Wherefore, that we understand, that we will, that we are affected, that we feel, speak, move, walk, and whatsoever it is that we do, or are able to perform, we do by the benefit of God, so far, namely, as it is good. No man therefore can arrogate anything to himself, <!-- p.338 --> unless, being lifted up with excessive and devilish pride, he will be unthankful to God, and be justly cast off by God, even as the devil, cast headlong from heaven for that same arrogance, now payeth eternal penalties in eternal gnashing and horror. Now it must be diligently considered, as we admonished above also in the Catechesis, that the good of creation (*bonum creationis*) is one thing, the good of grace and salvation (*bonum gratiae et salutis*) another. Of the good of creation, therefore, do we speak, when we say that we can by our own powers hear the word, and in some manner consider it, and in some manner, and at least with a carnal mind, assent to it, because God hath so made us, and of His goodness still preserveth in us the outward and inward senses, and those faculties and powers, whereby we may in some manner lay hold of the body of the word preached and heard. But that we may have the soul of the word, it is necessary that the Holy Spirit work, who also Himself, as **Clement** godly speaketh, is the soul of the word, and kindleth in us new spiritual motions, true faith, consolation, invocation, patience, obedience, etc. But if now any man would glory, and say that there is in him the faculty of hearing the word, without which hearing the working of the Holy Spirit would not proceed, and that therefore he can deservedly ascribe to himself somewhat of power, assuredly that man would be both mad and unthankful, and a man of the worst disposition, and wholly out of his wits, just as some beggar who should cast it in the teeth of him that giveth him an alms, by way of upbraiding and reviling, that unless he himself stretched forth his hand and took the gift, the other could not give it. Could that beggar be accounted worthy of the benefit? Would he not be hissed away by the judgement of all sound men, and deservedly held in hatred? Or if that wretched man who fell among thieves, **Luke 10**, had said to the Samaritan: Thou couldest not have cured me, unless I had both been wounded, and lain prostrate, and suffered thy labour and help to be bestowed upon me? What manner of glorying, I pray, would this be? Would it not be worthy of the thunderbolt of eternal condemnation and rejection? Let us know therefore that we both ought and are able to hear the word, and that we must labour and strive with all our powers that this be done diligently and continually, and yet that we can ascribe nothing to ourselves, <!-- p.339 --> nor set it down to our own account. For what have we that we have not received, whether through creation and preservation, or through the new grace of the Gospel, or through the gift of the Holy Spirit? *"Far be it that that should be, or be called, a good will, which glorieth in others, or in itself, and not in the Lord,"* saith **Augustine**.

**X.** Further, that which is objected concerning the eternal predestination of God, which no man can change, whatsoever indeed he may will, strive after, endeavour, do or believe — all that, assuredly, so far as it is brought forward outside the word and revelation of God, is blasphemous. Let us indeed hold and confess that God from eternity foresaw all things, and predestinated the godly unto life and eternal salvation, and rejected and reprobated the ungodly unto eternal damnation, because God foreknoweth and foreseeth, determineth and ordaineth all things. But who of men, I pray, even of them that rightly believe, attaineth these eternal things in this life, unless he betake himself to the word of God? Lest therefore, by speculating overmuch, and by searching into the majesty of God and His eternal and secret counsels, we should be overwhelmed by the glory, and stray more and more from the way, unto us a child is born, and unto us a Son is given: ***ὁ λόγος*** (the Word), the herald, ***κῆρυξ καὶ ἑρμηνεύς τῶν μεγάλων βουλημάτων τοῦ θεοῦ πατρὸς*** (the proclaimer and interpreter of the great counsels of God the Father), the Orator, Counsellor and Evangelist, who hath brought forth and made manifest unto us, out of the secret counsel of the whole Godhead, the manner and way ***τῆς εὐδοκίας, φιλανθρωπίας καὶ χρηστότητος*** (of the good pleasure, love toward man and kindness) of God the Father. Him the Father commandeth us to hear. This is the book of life. Whosoever believeth on Him, and persevereth in true faith, he is predestinated unto life and eternal salvation. But whosoever believeth not on Him, he is reprobated unto death and eternal damnation by the just judgement of God, not because he was by nature a sinner, a child of wrath, and guilty of eternal punishments, but only because he hath not believed on the only-begotten Son of God, whom God hath set forth to be a ***ἱλασμὸν*** (propitiation) for the sins of the whole world. Nor indeed doth predestination proceed otherwise than in that order which we have shown above, namely, that thou be a lover of the ministry of the word, hear and read the word, and through the word and the use of the Sacraments <!-- p.340 --> receive the Holy Spirit, believe on the Son, call upon God, and ask that thou mayest be and abide a vessel of mercy, a saving instrument to thine own soul and to others, a temple, palace and dwelling place of the Holy Spirit. Hear therefore the Son incarnate, and predestination will offer itself of its own accord. Look upon the wounds of Christ, and the blood shed for thee, and straightway out of these the certainty of predestination will shine forth. In vain, and to the peril of his own soul, often even to its destruction, doth he labour about predestination, whosoever seeketh the knowledge of it outside Christ. For the Father hath committed all judgement unto the Son. Whosoever believeth on Him, he is not cast out, but hath everlasting life, and that by the will of the merciful God. But he that believeth not, he is condemned already, and guilty of eternal damnation, and that also by the will of God, who putteth forth His righteousness upon all that believe not on the Son.

But of these things, God willing, we shall have to treat more largely elsewhere, especially since many restless and ambitious men are calling back hurtful and perilous phrases and opinions, long since laid to sleep, which **Luther** named the juggleries of the Devil, and are in manifold ways casting scruples into consciences, in this too feeble and secure old age of the world, by denying all contingency and laying down a Stoic necessity in all things, and an effective blinding of the nations and of others, proceeding from the will of God, without the will of them that are blinded. These foul and blasphemous things let us detest, and let us pray God that He would keep us, set at the cradle of Christ, in true faith and in certainty of doctrine.

**XI.** But in what order the conversion of man proceedeth and is wrought, the Sacred Scriptures show. First there is preached the doctrine of the Law, accusing all and each of sin, and condemning us because of the depravity of nature. By this doctrine the Holy Spirit pricketh and smiteth the heart, that, sin and the wrath of God being acknowledged, it may be sore afraid, and may despair of itself, and of its own works and merits and those of all men, and may seek counsel of life and eternal salvation. Secondly there is shown the Son of God, the light of <!-- p.341 --> men, Himself seeking us lost sheep by the voice of the Gospel, which is: *"Come unto me, all."* This voice, to which we join also the use of the Sacraments, thou oughtest not to resist, because no conversion is wrought so long as a man resisteth the word of God. Now the Holy Spirit is unchangeably present with the voice of the Gospel, teaching, moving and drawing hearts to true assent and faith, both[^P16-4] that justifying faith (which is of grace, that is, the imputation of righteousness, wherein man beareth himself merely passively), and that effectual and living faith — yet one and the same faith — which is of the gift, etc., wherein man no longer beareth himself purely passively, but is a ***σύνεργος*** (fellow-worker), being made out of the old carnal man a new spiritual man, born of God. *"For as many as are led by the Spirit of God, they are the sons of God,"* **Rom. 8**.
