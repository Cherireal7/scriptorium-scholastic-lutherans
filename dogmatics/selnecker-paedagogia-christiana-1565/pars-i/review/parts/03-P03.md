<!-- p.61 -->

Now, in this depraved nature, since the order of all the affections is greatly disturbed, this affection also — both as touching God, namely that we should truly love Him, is very dull and languid; and as touching our neighbour, is either excessive and inordinate, or less than is just, as that saying of the **Mime** hath it concerning women: *"A woman either loveth or hateth; there is no third thing."* Seeing therefore that our love is entire neither toward God nor toward our neighbour, are we for that cause condemned?

This Question is the same with this: Whether we be condemned, seeing we cannot satisfy the Law? For love is the fulfilling of the Law, **Rom. 13**. And **1 John 5**: *"This is the love of God, that we keep his commandments."* Likewise **1 Tim. 1**: *"The sum of the commandment is love out of a pure heart, and of a good conscience, and of faith unfeigned"*: so that entire love is obedience toward the whole Law of God, or, as they call it in the Schools, universal righteousness (*iusticia universalis*), comprehending all the virtues.

**I answer.**

As touching the judgement of the Law, the Rule abideth unmoved: *"Cursed is every one that hath not done all things which are written in the book of the Law."* But the promise of the Gospel maketh us certain of the grace of God, and showeth unto us the Son of God, who hath satisfied the Law in our stead, both as touching obedience and as touching punishment, and that in four manners, as we have said above. **I.** By His own obedience. **II.** By the turning of the punishment upon Himself, as it is written: *"Behold the Lamb of God, which taketh away the sin of the world."* **III.** By the restoring of righteousness and life in us. **IIII.** By ratifying and confirming the precepts of the Law; that is, to speak more briefly, actively, passively, imputatively, and inchoatively (*active, passive, imputative, et inchoative*). And at the last there shall be added the consummate perfection, in the life eternal, in which the Law shall be fulfilled by us consummatively.

Here there falleth in the question: **Whether we be justified by love?** This question is the same with this: Whether we be justified by the Law, or by our obedience toward the Law, or by good works.

<!-- p.62 -->

I answer: If we are righteous by love, that is, by the Law, there is no need of the Gospel. This is most evident. Next, seeing that obedience toward the Law is not entire in us, we cannot be justified by it. For if thou wilt be made righteous by the Law, thou must do the whole Law, according to the saying: *"Cursed is he that doeth not all things which are in the Law."* There is need therefore of another remedy, which is shown in the Gospel, namely, faith in the Son of God.

But here thou sayest: Yet faith also is a work, as we said above, that faith is the worship of the first Commandment? I answer: Faith, so far as it pertaineth to the Law, that is, so far as it is a work, or a quality, hath not this praise, that it justifieth. But when it is said that we are justified by faith, there must be understood the relation unto the Son of God, in whom and through whom the mercy of God is exhibited unto us. How this cometh to pass, the voice and promise of the Gospel alone showeth.

Let us here remember the Argument of **Cochlaeus**:

> God justifieth,
> God is charity:
> Therefore charity justifieth.

An excellent thing surely, and worthy to be proclaimed! Distinguish the times, and thou shalt reconcile the Scriptures. Distinguish the time wherein God before loved thee[^P03-1] from this time wherein thou beginnest to love God. God justifieth. Who dareth to utter aught else? God is charity. Hear, thou Papist, and consider, if thou art a Grammarian, that love is here understood passively, of the love wherewith God embraceth us, as we said above; that is, of the efficient cause. But whence doth it follow: Therefore our charity justifieth? This is just as if thou shouldest say: The painter hath painted this picture; therefore this painting also is able to paint. We say therefore: Love justifieth — understanding love passively. But it doth not justify actively, as our quality.

### *Sed quare nostra dilectio non iustificat?* · But Wherefore Doth Not Our Love Justify?

<!-- p.63 -->

**I answer.**

Because thou, wretched man, hast not entire love, neither performest obedience to the Law. If thou canst perform unto the Law obedience inward and outward, it is well with thee; thou hast no need of the merit of Christ. But that thou canst not, thou findest by experience, and willing or unwilling thou art compelled to confess it. *"God hath concluded all under sin, that he might have mercy upon all."* And worthy is the word of **Bernard**, in Sermon 50, of the twofold charity, actual and effectual:[^P03-2] *"It was not hidden from the Master that the weight of the precept exceeded the powers of men; but He judged it profitable that by this very thing they should be admonished of their own insufficiency, and that they should know indeed unto what end of righteousness they ought to strive according to their powers. Therefore, in commanding things impossible, He made men not transgressors, but humble, that every mouth may be stopped, and all the world may become subject unto God: because by the works of the Law there shall no flesh be justified in His sight. For we, receiving the commandment, and feeling our defect, shall cry unto heaven, and God will have mercy upon us, and we shall know in that day that, not by works of righteousness which we have done, but according to His mercy He saved us."* Thus far Bernard.

Seeing therefore that our love is not entire, it is impossible that we should by it be able to stand before God, who requireth not a shadow only of actions, but obedience inward and outward.

Godly is the speculation of certain men, who have said that the man who believeth in Christ hath three colours. First, man had a white colour in his integrity, being full of light and heat. For the white colour ariseth from abundance of light and of heat. Such was man in his perfection before the fall. Afterward, being turned away from God by the snares of the Devil and by his own will, he fell into the other extreme, namely, into the black colour, which ariseth from scantness of light and from abundance of cold. Lo, in this colour thou hast man without Christ. The Painter is God, who painted man after His own image in a <!-- p.64 --> white colour, full of light and love. The Devil came, and defiled the white colour with his own black mire. Whence then is the restoring? Here the third colour cometh in, the red, which is of the commixture of white and black, the two extreme colours. This colour is the mean. And this is the blood of the Son of God, which restoreth the white colour, and blotteth out the black, yea, taketh upon itself and turneth upon itself the sins of mankind. He Himself cometh upon it, and transferreth the black colour unto Himself, so that the white colour is again seen in mankind, which was before in mankind. For when that which came after is removed, that which was before can again be seen. The black colour being removed, the white is laid open. Now it is removed by the red and precious blood of the Son of God. Yet there remaineth in us, so long as we are in this life, a commixture of the white and the black colour; that is, the consummation and perfection is not yet made, but sins still cleave in us, which we resist, and which we know to be remitted unto us through the blood of the Son of God, until the entire perfection be made at the coming of the Son of God, whom we with groaning wait for. Moreover, the man that is ***ἄθεος*** (without God) hath the black colour, that is, he is an instrument of the Devil. The heathen man, and he that leaneth upon his own virtues, hath a green colour, of much black and no red. The man baptized, trusting in his own works, hath a tawny colour, of much white (as touching his confidence) and a little red, as the Papists and the Monks. The man who boasteth of faith without the fruits of faith hath a saffron colour, of much red (as touching his boasting) and a little white. The Christian alone who truly believeth hath a colour red and purple, that is, he hath faith, and bringeth forth the fruits of faith. For purple is of much red and a little black. He hath indeed sins, but he resisteth them. These things are not unpleasant to godly Divines and to Natural Philosophers, if there be any who understand the differences of the extreme and of the mean colours.

## *De Tertio, videlicet de Timore Dei* · On the Third Point, Namely, of the Fear of God

To fear God is nothing else than to rule one's life by the word of God, and to obey the will of God in the whole life and in all actions, and to tremble at the thought of the wrath against sin, and so to subject oneself unto God, and to shun offences, both for love of godliness and for dread of the wrath and judgement of God, as it is said:

> The good hate to sin for love of godliness.  

Of this fear it is said, **Prov. 1**: *"The beginning, or the head and sum, of wisdom is the fear of the Lord."* And this fear is filial (*filialis*), joined with faith in the Son of God, and not despairing. And from this fear the life of Christians beginneth, as **Gregory** godly saith: *"In the way of God the beginning is from fear, that a man may come unto fortitude. For as in the way of the world boldness begetteth fortitude, so in the way of God boldness begetteth weakness. And as in the way of the world fear begetteth weakness, so in the way of God fear begetteth fortitude."* And **Cassiodorus** upon **Ps. 25**, *"Sweet and upright is the Lord"*: *"When thou hearest,"* saith he, *"that thy Lord is sweet, mark what thou art to love: when thou hearest that He is upright, mark what thou art to fear; that, being stirred up by the love and fear of God, thou mayest keep His Law."*

Another fear is servile (*servilis*), whereof there are two degrees. One, which shunneth offences only for dread of punishment and judgement, with no love of godliness. For rightly saith **Augustine**, *Against Pelagius*, book 2: *"When good is done for fear of punishment, not for love of righteousness, the good is not yet well done; neither is that done in the heart which seemeth to be done in the work, which a man would rather not do, if he might with impunity."* Of these it is said:

> The wicked hate to sin for dread of punishment.  

But these effect nothing in the kingdom of God. The godly join both together, the love of godliness and the dread of judgement, that is, the love <!-- p.66 --> of God and the fear, as hath been said. The other degree is a terror which without faith fleeth from God and rageth against God, that is, despair, as in Judas, Saul, Cain. Of this it is said, **1 John 4**: *"Love casteth out fear: because fear hath **κόλασιν**, or torment and punishment. But he that feareth is not perfect in love."* And **Rom. 8**: *"Ye have not received the spirit of bondage again to fear; but ye have received the Spirit of adoption, whereby we cry, Abba, Father."*

Now these causes ought to drive us unto the fear of God:

**I.** All the calamities of mankind, public and private, as it is said in our little verse:

> No house is free from tragic examples,  
> ***Νόσφιν ἄτερ τραγικῶν οὐ δόμος ἐστὶ κακῶν.***  

And:

> Be warned, and learn righteousness, and not to despise the gods.  

**II.** The preachings of the Law concerning repentance, the curse, and the punishments of wicked deeds, and concerning the wrath of God.

**III.** The death of the Son of God. This cause is the most illustrious of all. And in sum, because God is sweet and upright, is Father and avenger, **Ps. 25**.

**IIII.** Now unto the fear of God there is joined humility (*humilitas*), which hath its name from the ground (*humus*), whence man also (*homo*) seemeth to be named, even as Adam from the earth, or a man of a ruddy colour. For the ground out of which man was created was reddish, as in the Eastern parts and in many other places it is reddish by reason of the scorching of the Sun. *Adom*: he was red, it was ruddy. So therefore a man is humble (*humilis*), as it were cast down upon the ground (*humi abiectus*). And humility is: **I.** To acknowledge oneself to be a man, namely one's own uncleanness and infirmity. **II.** To fear God. **III.** To do the works of one's calling in the confidence and hope of the divine help. **IIII.** To acknowledge the gifts of others, and not to despise others. **V.** To bear patiently one's own lot, and the other things which befall in one's calling, and not to covet greater things. Now these things ought to move us unto humility: **I.** The consideration of our nature, who are in truth grass, the flower of the field, phlegm, bile, ***σκιᾶς ὄναρ*** (the dream of a shadow), the slime of the earth, as it is said:

<!-- p.67 -->

> Seeing we are slime and dung, seeing we are bile and phlegm,  
> Why are we proud? We know not when we perish.  

Hither pertaineth ***τὸ γνῶθι σεαυτόν*** (that saying, *Know thyself*). For many know many things, and know not themselves. And godly saith **Cassiodorus** upon that of **Ps. 6**, *"Have mercy upon me, O Lord; for I am weak"*: *"A great ascent unto God is the knowledge of one's own infirmity."* Whence certain of the Jews, desiring to show forth the condition and misery of man, set forth at the first Adam, formed out of the earth; then his offspring, Cain and Abel. Cain is the possession and getting of a man. Abel, the second son, is vanity. For Cain signifieth possession, Abel vanity. So are we all born. We are Cain, that is, men now born, or possessed; but when we consider all things in us, we find Abel, the second born, that is, all things are vanity, dust and shadow.

These things are afterward better declared in the doctrine of the Gospel. Cain is the old man, without Christ. Abel is the new man. Cain is sin; Abel is righteous, and is slain by Cain. So man carrieth this wrestling within himself, and is slain by sin, that is, he dieth. But the blood crieth unto God, and sin resteth at the doors in the sepulchre, until the perfection be made at the coming of the Son of God.

The second cause of humility is the efficient cause of the gifts in us, which proceed from God alone, and are not gotten by our own industry, and are often taken away from us again, that we may understand that these gifts are not men's own, but are given, conferred, taken away and transferred by God. *"Now if we have received, why do we glory, as if we had not received?"* **1 Cor. 4**. *"For one and the same Lord worketh all things, dividing to every man as He Himself will."* God giveth and taketh away, as infinite examples testify, of Nebuchadnezzar, of David, and of others. And let the saying of Job be ever in sight: *"The Lord gave, and the Lord hath taken away; blessed be the name of the Lord."* If thou art mighty, if rich, if excellent in mind, if learned, if thou art sound and strong, think that these are the gifts of God, and give God thanks; be <!-- p.68 --> not puffed up, because He that gave can take away more swiftly than in a moment. Whence **Augustine** saith upon Matthew, Sermon 59: *"Ye ought rather to think what is wanting unto you than what ye have. What thou hast, beware lest thou lose. What thou hast not yet, make supplication that thou mayest have. It is to be considered in how many things thou art less, not in how many thou art greater. For if thou thinkest how far thou hast gone before another, fear swelling: but if thou thinkest how much is wanting unto thee, thou groanest. Thou art healed; if thou be humble, thou shalt walk more safely, thou shalt not be cast down headlong, thou shalt not slip."* Thus far Augustine. Nay more, the greater gifts any man hath, the more humble ought he to be, as **Gregory** rightly saith: *"So much the more humble ought a man to be by reason of the gift, as he seeth himself to be the more bound in rendering an account."*

The third cause is the examples of Christ, and of all who are truly Saints. **Matt. 11**: *"Learn of me; for I am meek and lowly in heart."* *"Learn of me, not to frame the world, not to create all things visible and invisible, not to do miracles in the world itself and to raise the dead, but that I am meek and lowly in heart. Thou thinkest to set up a great fabric of loftiness; think first of the foundation of humility,"* saith **Augustine**, *On the Words of the Lord*. **Phil. 2**: *"Let the same affection of humility be in you, which was in Christ."* Let the lofty therefore blush: where do they believe that pride hath place, when they see heaven and earth possessed by humility?

Examples likewise are afforded by Abel, Moses, Hannah, **1 Kings 1 [1 Sam. 1]**, Jonathan, David in his exile, Mary, John the Baptist — *"I am not worthy to unloose the latchet of his shoe"* — Paul, and Daniel, of whom **Augustine** thus saith: *"Above thee is God, beneath thee are the beasts. Acknowledge Him who is above thee, that those things which are beneath thee may acknowledge thee. And therefore, when Daniel had acknowledged God above him, the lions acknowledged him above them."*

The fourth cause is the effects, that is, the rewards of the humble, and the punishments of the proud. **1 Pet. 5**: *"God resisteth the proud, and giveth grace to the humble."* **Matt. 23. Luke 7. 14.** and **18**: *"Every one that exalteth himself shall be abased; and he that humbleth himself shall be exalted."*

<!-- p.69 -->

Likewise the song of Hannah, **1 Kings 1 [1 Sam. 1]**, and of the virgin Mary: *"He hath put down the mighty from their seat, and exalted them of low degree."* There are also very illustrious sentences of the Heathen, in the first place of **Hesiod**:

> ***Ῥεῖα δὲ τὸν σκολιὸν βριάει, καὶ ἀγήνορα κάρφει.***  

God breaketh the proud into parts and fragments, as it were a straw. Likewise: The companion of pride is Nemesis, or Adrastia, that is, the punishing righteousness of God. For example there are the Devil himself, Cain, Nebuchadnezzar, Belshazzar, **Isa. 14**; Ajax, saying: *"I will conquer without God."* Apries, King of Egypt, boasting that not even God could wrest his kingdom from him, perished strangled. Thulis, King of Egypt, of whom **Suidas** writeth. And infinite other examples, even in our own time. The kingdoms of the world perish, and Kings and Princes live and depart miserably.

The fifth cause is the author of humility; likewise, the author of pride. For the pricking and feeling of pride showeth that the Devil is at hand with thee, who is the author and lover of pride. But God is the author and lover of humility. For the Devil uttered this word: *"Ye shall be gods."* This pricketh us still. The Devil would not serve a creature of clay, namely man, as the rest of the Angels, being commanded of God, have served and do serve. He would not, moreover, acknowledge the Son of God, who was to assume human nature, and worship Him as God, as **Bernard** saith. But what did he effect? The eternal damnation of himself and of his own. He who was light is made darkness; he who was life, death; he who was righteousness is sin.

Let this therefore be the conclusion, of **Augustine**: *"The other vices prevail in evil deeds; pride alone is to be guarded against even in deeds rightly done."* But let true humility be discerned from the false and feigned, such as is that of the Monks and of others, ***τῶν βαυκοπανούργων*** (the affected counterfeiters of lowliness), who would be *"humble without contempt, poor without want, rich without labour,"* as **Bernard** saith; even as a certain Monk had gone with his countenance fixed upon the ground until he was made Abbot, and then walked upright and proudly. Being asked whence came this change in him, he answered that he had before been seeking the keys upon the ground, and that he had found them, and that therefore there was no need of the former show of humility.

<!-- p.70 -->

### *De Patientia* · Of Patience

Moreover, unto humility pertaineth patience. For this maketh the difference between false and true humility, as **Augustine** writeth in the Epistle to Proba: *"It is very easy to have a despised garment, to walk with the head bowed down, to let a veil fall over the hair: but the patience of injury showeth the truly humble man."* Now Patience is exercised in three ways: for some things there are which we endure from God, some from the Devil, some from our neighbour.

From our neighbour are persecutions, losses, slanders, cursings, unjust hatreds, backbitings and reproaches. In these the first degree is, not to begin injustice. The second, not to avenge. The third, not to do unto him that vexeth thee those things which thou hast suffered, although thou art able to hurt him. For it is Christian virtue, not to hurt. It is Christian fortitude, to forgive him that hurteth. It is Christian glory, to spare him whom thou art able to hurt. This also is to be considered, that the neighbour who hurteth and doth the wrong is often more afflicted than he that is hurt, who hath a good conscience, as **Augustine** godly discourseth of this in the Sermon of the Innocents: *"Every man,"* saith he, *"who persecuteth another in the body, is known first himself to suffer persecution in the heart. For even if he who persecuteth hath taken somewhat of the other's substance, he doth a greater loss unto himself: because no man hath an unjust gain without a just loss; where there is gain, there is also loss — gain in the chest, loss in the conscience: he hath taken a garment, and hath lost faith; he getteth money, and loseth righteousness."*

From the Devil, or the old adversary, we endure temptations of every kind; who indeed both stirreth up our neighbour against us, and endeavoureth to move our flesh unto all manner of concupiscences. Here there is need of the highest patience, which consisteth in repressing and ruling our affections through prayer, as **Jerome** saith: *"The affections and perturbations, so long as we dwell in the tabernacle of this body, and are compassed about with frail flesh, we are able moderately to rule; <!-- p.71 --> to cut them off we are not able."* And surely it is a great patience, to be able to bear and endure one's own self, as the voice of **St. Paul** testifieth: *"O wretched man that I am! who shall deliver me from the body of this death?"* Likewise: *"I desire to depart, and to be with Christ."* For the godly know how great are the snares of the Devils going about, and assailing in many ways our flesh, which of itself is frail. And truly said **Gregory**: *"We may be martyrs without sword and flame, if we truly keep patience in the mind."* But we must pray continually, that God suffer us not to be led into temptation, but deliver us from the Devil. This also is to be known, that God permitteth not unto the Devil temptations in us which exceed our measure or powers, if we ourselves be willing, and which are not profitable unto us, if we be willing, as **Tertullian** saith: *"Satan dareth not lay a hand upon the elect of God, unless it be permitted of God; which when it is done, is wont to be done for two causes, namely, that the Devil may be confounded through the victory of faith in the godly, and that it may be made manifest that they who fall in temptations were before in the power of the Devil"*; of which thing Job is an example. And Christ showeth the same, **Luke 22**: *"Behold, Satan hath sought and desired you, that he may sift you as wheat. But I have prayed for thee, that thy faith fail not"*; that is, that the Devil may not be able to break and repress thy faith.

From God we endure scourges, of what sort soever they be; that is, whether they be ***τιμωρίαι***, or punishments for certain offences, as the exile of David, the prison of Manasseh; or whether they be ***δοκιμασίαι***, or exercises and trials of the godly, as the prison of Joseph; or whether they be ***μαρτύρια***, or testimonies of doctrine and of immortality, as the death of Paul, of the Baptist, of Laurence. *"In these the learning of a man is known,"* saith Solomon, **Prov. 19**; that is, how learned and godly thou art is shown by patience in calamities, which come upon thee either by the permission of God, as those which befall from the Devil and from our neighbour, or by the will of God. And of these **Isidore** thus speaketh, book 4 *Of the Highest Good*: *"Let him learn not to murmur, who suffereth evils. And if he knoweth not wherefore he suffereth evils, let him by this judge that he suffereth justly, because he is judged by Him whose <!-- p.72 --> judgements are never unjust."* Hither pertaineth the saying of **Ps. 119**: *"Righteous art thou, O Lord, and upright is thy judgment."* And the history of the Emperor Maurice, in the year of Christ 603, which I will briefly recite.

The Emperor Maurice, about the end of his reign, when the Chagan, King of the Avars or Hungarians, had offered unto him twelve thousand Roman soldiers, whom he had taken, to be ransomed each for one piece of gold, would not ransom the captives, either through covetousness, as they write, or through anger and stubbornness. The Chagan therefore, being wroth, when he saw himself to be despised, commanded them all to be slaughtered. By reason of this exceeding tragical event Maurice came into so great hatred that, entering the church on the day of Christ's nativity, he was well-nigh overwhelmed with stones. Neither did he only lose the good will of the people, but he himself also erred manifoldly in his counsels, as it is said: *"A sick mind erreth always."* Philippicus, his kinsman by marriage, he shut up in prison, suspecting that he lay in wait for the kingdom. His son Theodosius he commanded to be beaten with rods, suspecting that he furthered the counsels of Germanus, whose daughter he had joined to him in marriage. Moreover, prodigies and dreams increased the dismays of Maurice. A Comet *Xiphias* (sword-shaped) was seen. A Monk that foretold things to come, with head uncovered and feet unshod, ran up and down the city of Constantinople from street to street, and with a drawn sword cried out at the statue of the Emperor that the Emperor should perish by the sword. Maurice at length sent unto the Churches of the whole Empire, desiring that prayers might be made unto God for him. Meanwhile he himself dreamed that he was set before the image of the Saviour in a great assembly of the people, and that it was said by Christ, *"Give up Maurice,"* and that he was delivered bound, to be slain, unto his own soldier, whose name was Phocas. Being awaked, he bringeth Philippicus out of prison and asketh him who and what manner of man Phocas is. He answereth that he is a Centurion, yet a young man, ambitious, rash, but fearful. Maurice therefore addeth this old saying: *"If he is fearful, he is a murderer,"* as antiquity hath said: ***πᾶν δειλὸν φονικόν***. A little after, divers tumults having arisen, the soldiers salute Phocas Emperor, whom the Patriarch Cyriacus <!-- p.73 --> crowneth. Maurice, being sick, had fled to Chalcedon with his holy wife and his little sons and daughters. Being dragged thence, he is made the beholder, first, of the most cruel slaughter of his two younger sons, then of his three daughters, afterward of his wife Constantina, the daughter of Tiberius. And ever and anon, especially when he seeth his holy wife smitten, he crieth out: *"RIGHTEOUS ART THOU, O LORD, AND UPRIGHT IS THY JUDGMENT."* At the last Maurice's own head also was cut off. The carcases were cast forth upon the shore, that they might be beheld by the soldiers and the people; but they were afterward buried by a certain Eunuch in a church. Let this history admonish us concerning the wrath of God, that we be modest, and live in the fear of God and in the invocation of Him.

Now two questions fall in. The former is: **Wherefore doth God so subject His own unto the cross in this life, that there is no other way unto heaven save that which lieth through adversities, Acts 14?** I answer: God willeth to keep His own in fear, in faith, and in the other virtues, and He maketh us like unto the image of His Son, and admonisheth us of our infirmity, which without the divine help rusheth into all evils. Next, seeing that this life is only a journey unto the true life and country, lest the godly should love their inns more than their country, God willeth His own to be exercised. But the ungodly flourish in this life, and perish in the way. Therefore **Paul** saith: *"When we are judged, we are chastened of the Lord, that we should not be condemned with the world."* The ungodly, being void of faith, have no wrestling. The godly feel the wrestling straightway. For before the trumpet is blown there is no battle. But after that the trumpet of the word of God hath begun to sound in men's minds, the wrestling ariseth; and so soon as Moses and Aaron begin to speak unto Pharaoh, the people is afflicted. And the Paschal lamb was not to be eaten without bitter meats. Neither could the promised land be possessed without persecutions. For neither the flesh, nor sin, nor death, nor the world, nor the devil are at rest; so that this is the sum: *"All that will live godly in Christ must needs suffer persecutions."* But it is well with him that dwelleth in the help of the most High, etc.

The latter question is: **How ought the godly to bear themselves in calamities?** <!-- p.74 --> I answer: with Patience. Of this, namely, there is need, which alone overcometh Satan and confoundeth the world. Now this Patience cometh by the acknowledgment and invocation of God, as well in prosperity as in adversity. Therefore **Bernard** saith: *"He that directeth his heart unto the wounds of Christ, who is the rock, feeleth not his own wounds."* And **Vincent** to the tyrant: *"Put forth thy fury; thou shalt see me stronger to suffer than thou art to afflict."* For Christians are serpents, which in the conflict guard their head; that is, they let not Christ, Emmanuel and ***παραστάτην*** (Him that standeth by to help), go out of their hearts, as the Son of God Himself thus saith: *"Be ye wise as serpents."*

There are also the heads of the chief consolations to be held fast. **I.** It is to be known that we are afflicted by the counsel of God, God knowing and permitting it, not by chance, nor by matter so stirring it. **II.** A good conscience, if it be present. **III.** Faith embracing the promised reconciliation for Christ's sake. **IIII.** The examples of Abel, of the Son of God, of the Baptist, of the Martyrs, as it is written: *"I am not better than my fathers."* **V.** The consideration of the final causes, whereof the first is the exercise of the fear of God, of faith, of invocation: The second is, that the wrath of God against sin may be acknowledged, in the Church first of all, and that evils may be amended: The third is, that the afflictions of the godly may be testimonies of doctrine and of immortality: The fourth is, that the Saints may be made like unto the image of the Son of God: The fifth, that the help and presence of God may be seen, as Job saith: *"Though he slay me, yet will I trust in him."* These things are more copiously unfolded elsewhere.

### *De Perseverantia* · Of Perseverance

Lastly, unto the fear of God, and unto humility and patience, there cleaveth also perseverance in the true acknowledgment of God and in faith in Christ, or in the truth acknowledged; of which perseverance it is said, **Matt. 24**: *"He that shall endure unto the end, the same shall be saved."* And **Rev. 2**: *"Be thou faithful unto death, and I will give <!-- p.75 --> thee a crown of life."* Likewise **John 10**: *"No man shall pluck my sheep out of my hands."* **Matt. 16**: *"The gates of hell shall not prevail against the Church."* For the Spirit of truth abideth with us for ever, **John 14**.

This perseverance in prosperity falleth not away from God, neither feigneth anything new and strange from the word of God, but defendeth and retaineth the true doctrine: in adversity it murmureth not against God, but endureth evils; in persecutions it casteth not away courage, but is constant, and uttereth its confession with a stout mind even unto death. Here let the saying of Solomon be observed: *"The path of the just goeth forth as a shining light, and increaseth unto the perfect day"*; that is, it is constant, until it be brought through unto the heavenly kingdom.

## *De Peccatis contra I. Praeceptum* · Of the Sins against the First Commandment

**I.** It is asked, **Whether men satisfy the first Commandment, which requireth perfect obedience, according to that: *"Thou shalt love the Lord thy God with all thy heart"*?** I answer: Our weak nature can never satisfy the Law; we never fear or love God so much as was meet. There cleaveth in us doubting, security, distrust, concupiscence, etc. For the nature of man is corrupted by the fault of our origin. *"There is none righteous, no, not one."*

**II.** **Can there not be begun some obedience of this Commandment?** I answer: Unless the Gospel concerning Christ and concerning the Remission of sins be heard, no obedience can be begun. For human hearts cannot believe God, they do not feel that they are heard, they do not love God, unless they first understand that God is appeased through and for the sake of the Son, who was made a sacrifice for us. Whence it is said: *"Christ is the end of the Law."* Likewise: *"The Law is established through faith,"* etc. For when we hear that God is propitious unto us through the Son, we are filled with sure consolation, and begin to believe and to trust in GOD, we <!-- p.76 --> acknowledge His mercy, we hold that we are regarded and heard, we love and fear God, and live in continual conversion, and begin an obedience which pleaseth God by faith for Christ's sake.

**III.** **But what are the sins against the first Commandment?** Before we speak of the sins in particular, it is necessary to hold the general doctrine concerning sin, which is thus defined, **1 John 3**: ***ἡ ἁμαρτία ἐστὶν ἀνομία***, sin is whatsoever agreeth not with the rule of the divine Law, that is, whatsoever is done: **I.** Against the Law. **II.** And without the Law; as feigned and Pharisaical works are done without the Law: they are not those works which God hath commanded, but are vain worships and commandments of men, wherewith God is worshipped in vain. Now this definition is delivered: Sin is a defect, or an inclination (these genera agree to the evil of our origin), or an action (comprehending all actual sins, inward and outward), fighting with the Law of God (the common difference, **Rom. 7**), offending God, condemned by God, and making men guilty of eternal wrath and of eternal punishments (these are the properties), unless remission be made for the sake of the Mediator, the Son of God.

Here first it is asked, **Whence did sin arise?** Namely, from the Devil, who sinneth from the beginning, and from the free will of our first parents. For the Devil abode not in the truth. Likewise: *"When the Devil speaketh a lie, he speaketh of his own. For he is a liar, and a murderer from the beginning."* Likewise: *"He that committeth sin is of the Devil."* And **Ps. 5**[?]: *"Thou art not a God that hath pleasure in wickedness."* God neither willeth, nor worketh, nor approveth, nor helpeth sin; which thing the sounder Heathen also, **Plato**, **Homer**, **Pindar** and others, have often expressed in most excellent sentences.

And here the evil of guilt (*malum culpae*) is to be discerned from the evil of punishment (*malum poenae*), of which it is said: *"There is no evil in the city, which the Lord hath not done."* Likewise: *"The Lord hath made even the wicked for the day of judgement."* And thus of the evil of punishment is understood in the Church this phrase, that God punisheth sins with sins; as the Turk is the scourge <!-- p.77 --> of God, such as Tamerlane also said that he was, King of the Parthians, in the year 1398, who seized upon Asia the less with a huge army, of 40,000 mailed horsemen and 60,000 footmen, having overcome Bajazet, Emperor of the Turks; whom, being taken, Tamerlane bound with chains of gold, and, having thrust him into an iron cage, carried about through all Asia and Syria, and, whensoever he mounted his horse, used him for a footstool.

Now I will add this by the way. Tamerlane, when he had besieged any Town, on the first day pitched tents of a white colour, signifying grace and kindness; on the second day, tents of a red colour, signifying blood and slaughter; on the third day he set up black tents, denoting the laying waste of the Town. It happened upon a time that a certain Town put off its surrender unto the second day, on which there were sent unto Tamerlane women and maidens, with others, infants, wearing white garments, and bearing the olive in their hands, in token of a peaceable surrender. But because the tents of red colour were already pitched, no kindness was shown, all being slain and thrust through. Tamerlane, being asked by one of his Counsellors with what mind he exercised this tyranny, answered: *"Thinkest thou that I am a man? I am the wrath of God, and the laying waste of the earth. I counsel thee that hereafter thou speak not unto me of this matter; otherwise thou shalt feel my cruelty upon thine own self."* In this year of Christ 1559 there are passed since then 161 years. So also the tyrant Attila inscribed himself *The Scourge of God, and the terror of the peoples*, in the year of Christ 451. He laid waste Rome, a great part of Germany, the Gauls, etc. So at this day the Pope is the punishment of security and ungodliness. The Turk likewise, and other enemies. And privately also, in families, sins are often punished with sins by the law of retaliation. As is the offence, such is the punishment. He that sinneth in his seed is punished in his seed. The fraudulent are deceived by frauds. Disobedient children beget stubborn children.

Here let the **Stoics** also be refuted, who cast the cause of all evils upon God, and feign a necessity <!-- p.78 --> in all things natural and voluntary.

Moreover, the kinds of sin are the sin of origin (*peccatum originis*) and actual sin (*peccatum actuale*).

Original sin is, in all men born of the seed of man, the loss of light in the mind, the turning away of the will from God, and the stubbornness of the heart against the Law of God, born with us, and following upon the fall of Adam; by reason of which corruption of nature men are guilty of the wrath of God and of eternal punishments, unless remission be made for the sake of the Mediator, the Son of God.

**Rom. 5**: *"By the offence of one all are dead. Death passed upon all, for that all have sinned,"* that is, are guilty, and have sin, a thing evil and condemned, and the evil is propagated unto all.

**Rom. 8**: *"The mind of the flesh is enmity against God: for it is not subject to the Law of God, neither indeed can be."*

**Eph. 2**: *"We were by nature the children of wrath,"* that is, guilty and condemned. **Ps. 50 [51]**: *"Behold, I was shapen in iniquity; and in sin did my mother conceive me."*

**Gen. 8**: *"The imagination of man's heart is evil from his youth."* *[Margin: **כָּל יֵצֶר** (kol yetser, every imagination)]* Now it is named *Iezar*, a thought, ***πλάσμα***, a workmanship, a thing formed, a fashioning, the lump of the heart, all its ***ὁρμαί***, or impulses. **Job 9**: *"I know of a truth that a man is not just before God."* **Jer. 17**: *"The heart of men is perverse above all things"* (that is, it is turned away from God, and is an enemy unto God) *"and wretched and unsearchable,"* or full of sorrows, full of trouble and ulcerated (these are the punishments of the defects). The greatness of this evil cannot be sufficiently understood by us.

**Ps. 116**: *"I said in my transport,"* that is, in my consternation, or my dread: *"All men are liars."*

Now it is asked, **Whether after Baptism it be rightly said that sin is in infants, or that concupiscence is sin?** I answer: In Baptism sin is taken away as touching the guilt, or the imputation; but the disease itself remaineth. The formal is taken away, the material remaineth (*tollitur formale, manet materiale*), as <!-- p.79 --> **Paul** clearly saith: *"In the members is a Law warring against the Law of the mind,"* and against the Law of God. Let those things also which are works of God remaining in man be discerned from the things which are of themselves vicious; as the knowledge of numbers is a good thing, because it was implanted in man by God. So also of the affections some are good and commanded of God, as the love of offspring, of spouses, of parents; others are forbidden by the Law of God.

Let the argument of **Julian** concerning Concupiscence be refuted:

> Concupiscence begetteth.
> Concupiscence is sin.
> Therefore sin begetteth.

I deny it of the ***ἀταξία*** (disorder) of nature. Concupiscence doth not beget, but the order which remaineth in nature, established by God; but that Concupiscence cometh in besides is ***ἀταξία***, and cometh to pass by accident.

Let there be refuted also the raving of the more recent Manichees concerning sin, that it is the very substance of man, created by the Devil; which is horrible to be heard, foul, and plainly contumelious against Christ, who assuredly did not assume our nature as a thing formed by the Devil, and who is Himself our creator. For the Devil can create nothing out of nothing.

Let the Monks also be refuted, who said that the appetites which are good, and created by God in man, are the material of original sin, and said that this remaineth after Baptism, but that the defect is taken away. They understood not what they said. They named Concupiscence the senses themselves, or the natural appetite, whereas they ought to have understood it to be the ***ἀταξίαν*** (disorder) of the appetites, and withal to have comprehended the darkness of the mind and the evils of the will. Thus much of original sin.

Actual sin is an action, either inward or outward, fighting with the Law of God. This ariseth out of the former.

Now here a distinction is to be made between the unregenerate and the regenerate. All sins in the unregenerate, original and actual, are mortal. For this Rule abideth unmoved: *"He that believeth not on the Son, the wrath of God abideth on him."* Here no virtues avail without the acknowledgment of the Son. But the sins in them that are reconciled — as the original evil, and many inward actual sins, fighting with the Law of God, which yet the regenerate resist, and many sins of ignorance <!-- p.80 --> and of omission — are venial, that is, the guilt is remitted unto them that retain faith and by the Spirit repress the depraved affections.

Next, the sins which remain in the regenerate in this life are to be discerned from those by reason of which grace, the Holy Spirit and faith are lost. There remain in the saints sins, the original, and many inward actual sins, but not against conscience. But when the elect rush on against conscience, there are made mortal sins, that is, grace, the Holy Spirit and faith are lost, unless there be made anew a conversion unto God, and remission for the Mediator's sake, as in Adam and Eve, in Aaron, David, Peter and many others. Neither is judgement to be made concerning sin according to election, which we perversely endeavour to search out, but according to the word of God delivered unto us. Adam and Eve were elect, and yet they did in very deed lose the Holy Spirit in the fall, being turned away from God, and were made guilty of eternal wrath. And of Aaron it is said, **Deut. 9**: *"The Lord was very angry with Aaron to have destroyed him; but Moses prayed for him."* These most sorrowful words of the Holy Spirit are not to be eluded. And **Paul**, **Rom. 8**, delivereth the difference: *"If ye live after the flesh, ye shall die: but if ye through the Spirit do mortify the deeds of the flesh, ye shall live"*; that is, in the saints there are many vicious inclinations, which must be resisted by spiritual motions, by the invocation of God, by fear, by faith, by a spiritual endurance that looketh unto God, and by a chastity that looketh unto God. And the regenerate abide in grace, although those vicious affections be in them, if yet they resist, and by faith acknowledge the forgiveness for Christ's sake; but if they resist not, Paul affirmeth that they shall die.

Now that it may be understood who they are that resist not sins, he setteth down a degree, namely, them that offend against conscience, that is, those who knowingly and willingly indulge those vicious motions and flames, or even obey them in outward action. And that which is here named sin against conscience is named elsewhere reigning or ruling sin (*peccatum regnans seu dominans*), of which **Paul**, **Rom. 6**: *"Let not sin reign in your <!-- p.81 --> mortal body."* And **Ps. 118 [119]**: *"Order my steps, and let not any iniquity have dominion over me."* And the name seemeth to be taken from the 4th chapter of **Genesis**: *"Let thy appetite be under thee, and rule thou over it."* Now sin hath dominion, when the guilt is not remitted, but kindleth the wrath of God, and plucketh man away from God, and man, being forsaken of God, is driven by his own infirmity and by the Devil, so that he rusheth out of some evils into others, and heapeth up wicked deeds and punishments, as Saul, Cain, Judas, Ahab, Oedipus, Atreus, Thyestes, Nero, and other plagues of mankind. Whence it is said in the verses:

> ***ὅταν γὰρ ὀργὴ δαιμόνων βλάπτῃ τινὰ,***  
> ***τοῦτ᾽ αὐτὸ πρῶτον ἐξαφαιρεῖται φρενῶν***  
> ***τὸν νοῦν τὸν ἐσθλὸν, εἰς δὲ τὴν χείρω τρέπει***  
> ***γνώμην ἵν᾽ εἰδῇ μηδὲν ὧν ἁμαρτάνει.***  

> When God in anger draweth any unto punishment,  
> He is wont first to take away the mind from such,  
> And poureth darkness over them, that they may rush  
> In fury into their own ruins, which with hurtful  
> Evil counsels they have of their own accord brought upon themselves.
