I. He hath the commandment of God, saying: *"God forbid that I should sin against God, who hath adorned us with so many benefits."* O woman, consider how great an offence it is to cast away the reverence and fear of God, and to offend Him for the sake of a light and slender pleasure.

II. He considereth the dignity of his Lord: *"Look upon him,"* saith he, *"whose wife thou art."*

III. Afterward he allegeth the benefits of his master: *"He,"* saith he, *"hath made me lord and governor of the whole house: how great a wickedness therefore would it be to forget these benefits, and so to deceive his expectation."* Now since we ought to be thankful and faithful toward men who have deserved well of us, as it is said: *"To deceive good men is a thing that calleth for expiation."* And: *"Thou namest all vices, if thou name a man unthankful"*: how much more ought we to bear ourselves toward God, the author, giver and preserver of all benefits, of life, of fortune and of salvation, reverently, and in all things adorned with all subjection and obedience? Rightly saith **Bernard**: *"No vice is more pernicious than ingratitude, because it drieth up the fountain of goodness."* And Christ saith: *"From him that hath not"*, that is, from the unthankful, *"even that which he hath is taken away."* Now of the benefits these verses have been written:

<!-- p.362 -->

> What the benefits of God are, whosoever thou art, thou seest:  
> Creation, Preservation, Redemption,  
> And the gift of the Spirit, the glorious light  
> Of the Word joined with the sacred rites of the holy things:  
> Solace, and deliverance out of evils:  
> Peace and food, and the quiet of one's calling,  
> A good end, and the possession of salvation,  
> The good name of a son abiding after burial.

Although therefore we strain all our sinews and powers to give thanks unto God, and in our turn to bear ourselves such as it becometh us to be, yet must we say with Jacob: *"I am less than all thy mercies."* Likewise: *"What shall I render unto the Lord?"* *"I am not worthy that thou shouldest come under my roof"*, or that I should unloose the latchet of thy shoe. One little drop of thy benefits is greater than the deep and the sea of all my powers.

The fourth is the sure hope of the glorious Resurrection, unto which all ought to sanctify themselves.

The fifth is the glory of GOD, which is horribly violated, and is evil spoken of among others by our sins, or by reason of our security, and the minds of many are estranged from the doctrine.

The sixth: That we make not shipwreck of faith. For all violators of discipline, of chastity and of honesty lose faith.

How weighty these causes are, and whether they suffer themselves to be washed away by wine, by the hope of concealment, or by the covenant of evil company, let every man consider. Would that, as these things are manifest, so they were smeared upon the minds of all. And whoso is not plainly mad, let him always set this looking-glass before his eyes, lest against conscience he violate discipline, which is a schoolmaster unto Christ. Let these things be said of the impelling cause (*causa impulsiva*).

Next, the difference standeth in the efficient cause. For in the unregenerate the efficient cause of honesty is the mind, or the judgement <!-- p.363 --> of right reason, understanding the duties of virtue and weighing the causes which incite unto virtue. Likewise some liberty of the will, which is able to bridle the outward members, that they rush not against the judgement and prescript of right reason. But in the Church other causes are added, to which the Heathen and Ethical causes yield by far, namely the word of God, and the Holy Spirit, who is as it were the soul of the Word, as **Clement of Alexandria** godly saith. For the Holy Spirit kindleth in the minds of believers the thought of the divine word and commandment, and moveth and helpeth the will, that it may obey the word of God and restrain the outward members, and that the will itself, by its own endeavour also, may resist enticements and ***τῇ ἀνομίᾳ*** (lawlessness), and obey the commandment of God.

Afterward there is a difference in the form, whereof there are several respects. For **first**, the godly have certain works of which men without the Church know either nothing or little, as are chiefly the works of the first Table of the Decalogue: the true acknowledgment of God, true faith, the love and fear of God, humility, invocation and thanksgiving directed unto the true God, and the other inward virtues. Of chastity also there are few admonitions among the Heathen. **Secondly**, the godly have the whole choir of the virtues, and the perfection of these, if not in quality, yet in knowledge, number and relation. But the Heathen have certain small parcels of the Law, and many wicked deeds, whereof Paul setteth down the ***συναθροισμὸν*** (heaping together) and Catalogue in **Rom. 1**, they hold not for sins; even as it is manifest also of **Socrates**, the chief ***ἐξάρχῳ*** (leader) of moral Philosophy, that he had so great a blemish that the mention of it is to be shrunk from. **Thirdly**, the regenerate bring not a dyed and painted or a tricked-out virtue, or an unclean hypocrisy, under which may lie hid a ***κακὸν ὕπουλον*** (festering evil beneath the skin), but before all things they study and labour for this, that they may purge out the old leaven, that is, not only the old Epicurean and false opinions concerning God, but also depraved lusts. **Fourthly**, the regenerate render a willing obedience in faith, not feigned, not <!-- p.364 --> wrung out by compulsion or fear, but proceeding from the true love of God, as Job saith: *"Though he slay me, yet will I trust in him."* But the unregenerate, although they vaunt the dignity of virtue as a thing to be desired for itself, yet for the most part abstain from sin rather through dread of punishment than through love of virtue, as the Philosophers themselves often testify. **Fifthly**, the form of the virtues in the regenerate is universally joined with continual invocation of the divine help. The Heathen think themselves sufficiently propped by their own strength, their own wisdom and virtue. **Sixthly**, the regenerate, while they perform all things that they are able, humble and subject themselves, and in the acknowledgment of their own weakness frankly confess that they are unprofitable servants; they are not proud or arrogant, but pray with David: *"Lord, keep back thy servant from presumptuous sins."* Such are not the others, who are destitute of the word and of the Holy Spirit as Ruler.

Lastly, there is a difference in the end. The godly hold nothing greater than God, unto whose glory they direct and refer all their labours and actions, as all know who are not without experience of the exercises of godliness. But the Heathen will have themselves praised, and make idols of their own virtues, and refer them not principally unto God, but unto themselves and unto their own glory.

## *De Secundo, videlicet de Utilitatibus doctrinae Ethicae* · Of the Second Point, namely, of the Uses of Ethical Doctrine

Most ample and unexhausted is the wisdom of God, which is set before us shadowed forth in the moral Law, or in the Decalogue; and although it cannot be sufficiently comprehended, adorned, or drawn dry by the wit and eloquence of any creatures, yet God willeth and commandeth that we acknowledge the beginnings of this His wisdom in this life. For therefore did He both create men, and scatter into the minds of men the rays of His own light, wisdom, righteousness and the other virtues, which Moses nameth the breath <!-- p.365 --> of rational life, that they might be firm and illustrious testimonies that God is, and might show what manner of God He is, and might govern the life and actions of men. Therefore also God redeemed men by His Son, that they might acknowledge and celebrate Him, and obey Him according to the Law which He implanted in the minds of men at the first creation, and which, being darkened after the fall, He made clear anew by express voice and by the writings of the Prophets, of Christ and of the Apostles.

Now since Ethical doctrine, or moral Philosophy, is truly a part of the divine Law, and because God willeth His Law, which He hath implanted in the minds of men, and which is also called the Law of nature, to be known, let us consider that great and many are the uses of this Philosophical doctrine, which concerning honest outward actions — such as reason understandeth to agree with the nature of man, and to be necessary in the civil conversation of life — the fountains of the Precepts being sought out by art and by demonstrations as far as may be, delivereth precepts, and gathereth the Laws of nature, and showeth the order.

Let there be set in view, therefore, these four degrees of uses: The **first** toward God. The **second** toward us all, or toward the whole human race. The **third** toward us severally, and toward each man's life, counsels, actions and manners, as well toward himself as toward others. The **fourth** toward the knowledge of the other arts necessary and profitable to human life.

### *Primus Gradus in se continet has utilitates* · The First Degree containeth in itself these Uses

The first is: The knowledge of moral Philosophy is a testimony that God is. For the sure and perpetual notions shining in the mind, which establish the order and differences of actions, command virtue, forbid things dishonest and base, and decree punishments for them that violate this order, could in no wise either arise by chance,[^P18-1] or flow together out of the Elements or any other matter, or out of the Atoms of **Democritus**; but <!-- p.366 --> it is necessary that the efficient and architectonic cause be more excellent, and it is necessary that we acknowledge that in this corrupt nature there is nothing in man more wonderful than is the knowledge of all principles, but chiefly of the practical ones, or of the virtues; which shineth not in one man only, or a second, nor endureth only for some certain time and is afterward extinguished, but shineth in all, and is carried about by all in all times. It is necessary therefore that God be the author of those notions, who both implanted them in the minds of men, and preserveth the same. And it is worthy of diligent and continual consideration, that the mind of man, and that light implanted in the mind concerning the difference of things honest and base, and the other natural notions, of order and of numbers, is the chief testimony concerning God in all nature. And indeed among the natural notions this also is as it were a star, and the first Law of nature, that God is, as Paul expressly saith: *"God hath shewed it unto them"*, that is, God hath willed Himself to be acknowledged by the human race. And that saying of **Cicero** is known: *"The first fellowship of man is with God."* And in the first book *Of the Laws*: *"Among men there is no nation, neither so untamed nor so iron-hard, which, although it know not what manner of God it ought to have, yet knoweth not that one is to be had."*

The second is: The knowledge of the virtues teacheth what manner of God He is. For since there shine in our mind the notions of the Laws, which certainly judge that wisdom, righteousness, chastity, truth and beneficence are things good and to be desired, and that the things contrary to these notions are evil and to be shunned, and destroy nature, it is assuredly necessary that the architectonic mind also be such: understanding, wise, just, true, a lover of order, beneficent, chaste, a judge and an avenger of wicked deeds. For it cannot be that the better nature should arise from the worse, or that the efficient cause should be worse than its own effect. And although this thought doth not yet bring us wholly unto the nature of God, yet it admonisheth us of the attributes and properties of the divine essence, which we ought to use in the Church also, and in daily <!-- p.367 --> invocation. And out of this very consideration floweth that saying of **Plato**: That he who hath the purpose of living justly is most like unto God, and is never neglected of God.

The third use: The knowledge of the virtues is a testimony concerning the providence and concerning the judgement of God. For in vain were the Law implanted in minds, if punishments did not follow the violation of the Law. And this also hath been proved by experience, that there is no use of any Law, unless it be strengthened by the sinews of executions against offenders, as the saying of **Aeschines** testifieth: ***οὐδὲν ὄφελος πόλεως, ἥτις μὴ νεῦρα ἐπὶ τοὺς ἀδικοῦντας ἔχη*** (there is no profit in a city which hath not sinews against them that do wrong). Since therefore God hath imprinted the rays of His Law, and the practical notions, upon the minds of men, it is certain that He willeth also to be the executor of these notions, and trifleth less with His own Law than even the severest Magistrate is said sometimes to trifle by winking at offences. And this very execution He denounceth and proveth, partly by the torments of conscience which accompany offences, even though no human judgements be at hand or feared,[^P18-2] as we see in Orestes, Oedipus, in Cain and Judas, and others; partly by the regularity of punishments in this life, as Job saith: *"I know, Lord, that thou sparest not the offender."* And in the *Terpsichore* of **Herodotus** it is said:

> ***Οὐδεὶς ἀνθρώπων ἀδικῶν τίσιν οὐκ ἀποτίσει:***  
> No man of the unjust shall fail to pay the penalty.

Likewise:

> Great crimes are ever attended by heavy punishments.

And these are testimonies of the divine judgement to come, in which all the wicked shall be punished, and the just shall be adorned with rewards, as Solomon saith: *"The Lord shall bring every work into judgment, with every secret thing, whether it be good, or whether it be evil."* And the verses are known:

> Sin not; God Himself seeth; thine Angel standeth by;  
> Satan accuseth, and the law, and the mind conscious of guilt;  
> Uncertain death rageth; ghastly Hell tormenteth thee;  
> And damnation abideth for ever with grievous punishment.

<!-- p.368 -->

Hither pertain the most sweet verses of **G. Nazianzen**:

> ***Ἔνθα τὲ πάντ' ἀναφανδὰ τὸ δὲ πλέον ἰσοτάλαντον***  
> ***Τῆμος ὅτ' ἐν χείρεσσι θεοῦ ζυγὸν ὀρθοδίκοιο:***  
> Before the throne of Christ all things together shall be made bright;  
> And, the just Judge holding the balance in His hand,  
> All things shall in judgement be weighed to an even scale.

### *Secundus Gradus has utilitates comprehendit* · The Second Degree comprehendeth these Uses

The first is: The knowledge of moral Philosophy showeth who and what manner of persons we were, and what manner of persons we now are. The second: It is an occasion of seeking another doctrine, out of which we may recover the light that was lost. The third showeth what manner of persons we shall at length be. And this second degree pertaineth properly to the Church. For although the Heathen also sometimes in some manner perceive that much light is wanting to the mind of man, and that there is great stubbornness and inconstancy of the will, yet they are wholly ignorant of the true causes, and the medicine which the voice of the Gospel showeth they for the most part deride. But the godly in the Church are taught, by the consideration of the notions of the Law, unto what the nature of man was created, and what manner of nature it was before the fall, or what that very righteousness was which is commonly named Original (*iustitia Originalis*), namely the agreement and conformity of the mind, of the will, and of all the powers, faculties, thoughts and actions of the whole man, with the will and the whole Law of God. God had poured over His own image, that is, the perfect knowledge of God, and the difference of things honest and base, into the minds of men, as into a looking-glass, and these notions would have shone with most clear light in the mind, and the will would have burned with the love of God, and would have been adorned with all virtues, if the nature of men had remained uncorrupted. The godly are therefore put in mind, in this Ethical doctrine, from how great a dignity men have fallen, seeing that the notions are exceeding dark and <!-- p.369 --> maimed, and the judgement weak, the will often resisteth the sound judgement of reason, and in perceiving, discerning, attaining and performing divine and spiritual things the whole man is utterly cold, unless he be divinely enlightened, taught, ruled and helped.

The second use is: When the godly acknowledge the disease of sin, and the slenderness of the moral Precepts, which ought to have been in all points most perfect, they seek the Physician and Helper, the Son of God, and know that there is need of a far other Philosophy, or doctrine concerning obedience toward God and concerning duties toward one's neighbour, than that which is delivered in the Philosophical rudiments, though most wisely and most exactly composed. But chiefly, when the godly acknowledge so great a weakness of human powers, that often and easily the nature of man, either baited with light occasions of sinning, or seduced by depraved company, or circumvented by the snares of the devil, or cut off by outward perils and hindrances, or entangled in the frequent examples, the security and the licence of others, troubleth the most fair order of nature, they assuredly see not only that there is need of great study, singular prudence, accustoming from tender years, circumspection and watchfulness for the study of virtue, seeing that it is not a thing ***εὐπόριστον*** (easily come by), nor is it easy, as many think, to put on uprightness of manners; but they also join with the daily Reading and meditation of the divine word most fervent prayers, wherein they ask that the Son of God Himself, who was sent, would heal us, and, His Holy Spirit being given, would kindle in our hearts motions agreeing with the divine will, wholesome to us and to others, and would not suffer us to rush into errors or into wicked deeds. This prayer, which God requireth and heareth, the godly find by experience to be effectual, and to show what true virtue is, while the godly daily go forward, and, beholding the light of God, are changed into the same image from glory to glory, even as by the Spirit of the Lord, given and poured out upon us, as Paul speaketh, **2 Corinth. 3**. For *"the path of the just is as the shining light, that goeth forward, and <!-- p.370 --> increaseth even unto the noonday"*, saith Solomon, **Proverb. 4**.

The third use in the second degree is, that the godly gather out of Ethical doctrine that eternal life remaineth. For since the perfect fulfilling of the Law is not done in this life by the nature of man, and yet God willeth the Law to be at some time fulfilled by men, as is said in **Matth. 5**, it is necessary that the nature of man be repaired, and that there remain an inheritance incorruptible, and undefiled, and that fadeth not away, reserved in heaven, **1 Petr. 1**; in which, as **Augustine** excellently speaketh (**lib. 22 de C. D. cap. 29**), *"there shall be no evil, no good shall lie hid, there shall be leisure for the praises of God, who shall be all in all."* And **cap. 30**: *"The reward of virtue shall be He Himself who gave the virtue, and who hath promised Himself, than whom nothing can be better and greater."*

### *Tertius Gradus has utilitates comprehendit* · The Third Degree comprehendeth these Uses

The first is: Ethical doctrine showeth how men ought severally to bear themselves toward God, that they may both say and do things acceptable unto God, namely godly, reverently, submissively, humbly and modestly, as the Arguments of all the Tragedies testify, of **Aeschylus**, **Sophocles**, **Euripides**, **Seneca**, and the Odes of **Pindar**, and the histories of all nations. The second is: The same doctrine instilleth into the ears and minds of men severally the manner of bearing oneself chastely, temperately and rightly toward oneself, lest a man hurt or defile his own conscience, life and good name with wicked deeds, or do harm to himself and his own, to his health, and to his other affairs; which is wont easily to be done by those who have not learned this doctrine. The third is: Moral Philosophy is the rule of the life and discipline of all men in outward actions, that manners may be honestly governed, that men may live rightly and justly with other men, and that in the whole of life and in human society things may be done according to the Laws. And in this respect moral Philosophy profiteth the more unto discipline, because not <!-- p.371 --> only with short sentences, such as are contained in the Paraenetic books of **Hesiod**, **Phocylides**, **Isocrates** and others, or with a short saying doth it restrain, as the Laws do, but with long handling it inquireth into the causes, considereth the order of nature, and contemplateth the beauty of virtue, and by this means inviteth minds and maketh them gentler, as it is commonly wont to be said: *"Studies pass into manners."* And hence also Ethical doctrine hath its name, namely from continual exercise and accustoming unto moderation. For ***ἐθίζειν*** properly signifieth to exercise and to do things now of old received by judgement and custom, and approved by all who are endued with a sound mind. And ***ἔθος*** or ***ἐθισμὸς*** is nothing else than an exercise ***πρακτικὴ*** (practical), which is done by them that are endued with reason. Now the effect ***τοῦ ἔθους*** (of custom) is ***τὸ ἦθος*** (character), which is used of a constant habit, whether that be good or evil, and of outward actions. For practical exercise is followed by an effect, which either is constant and a habit, and is named ***ἦθος***; or is inconstant, and a disposition only, and therefore is called ***τρόπος*** (turn, or manner). The constant, again, either is good, and is called ***χρηστὸν ἦθος*** (a good character), that is, virtue, when it obeyeth the judgement of right reason; or is vicious, when it indulgeth the affections, and is named ***μοχθηρὸν ἦθος*** (a bad character), or vice. But each is gotten by accustoming. And because the denomination is wont to be made from the better part, ***ἠθικὴ***, the doctrine, is referred chiefly to the virtues, which are also called ***ἠθικαὶ*** (moral), because, being gotten by liberal doctrine and learning, they show forth a gentleness[^P18-3] and sweetness of mind that calmly moderateth actions, according to what is becoming and according to the variety of circumstances. And that saying of **Aristotle** is known: ***Δίκαιοι γίνονται φύσει, διδαχῇ καὶ ἔθει*** (Men become just by nature, by teaching, and by custom).

### *Quartus Gradus continet has utilitates* · The Fourth Degree containeth these Uses

The first is: This same moral Philosophy is profitable to Theologians, that, first, in the comparing of the heavenly doctrine and <!-- p.372 --> the Philosophical, they may rightly discern each kind of doctrine; next, that concerning those matters which properly pertain to the Law they may speak dexterously, and may learnedly and prudently handle the political places: of the dignity of civil things, of the Laws, of Magistrates and governments, of the manners necessary in this civil conversation, of contracts and other civil matters. For the doctrine of the Gospel doth not take away polities and civil things, as the Anabaptists have doted. The second: This doctrine profiteth Jurisconsults, because it containeth the fountains of Right and of the positive Laws, or the first demonstrations, out of which the whole doctrine of Right is built up. Whence some have not feared to name these Elements of Ethical doctrine a *Catechesis of Right*, because it containeth the foundations out of which justice, whose Priests the Jurisconsults ought to be, proceedeth, and out of which the Laws arise. And most rightly did **Plato** say that Commonwealths would be happy if Philosophers bare rule, that is, such Men, Counsellors and Jurisconsults as know the fountains of the Laws, and the causes and duties of the virtues, and depart not from the first Right, which is the Right of nature, rightly and properly so called; as **Aristotle** saith: That there is one best polity, which is according to nature. And it is commonly wont to be said: *"Things that fight against nature have not the force of Law."* And the saying of **Alcidamas** is cited: *"Philosophy is the bulwark of the Laws."* The third: This moral doctrine is the epitome and sum of the Philosophical writings, the Orations and the writings of the Poets, in which wise men have comprised Ethical and political doctrine, precepts and sentences concerning the honest and wholesome form and governance of Commonwealths, and concerning the rewards of virtues and the punishments of wicked deeds. This doctrine therefore profiteth Orators, who take hence many notable ornaments, as the Orations of the chiefest men show, of **Demosthenes**, **Isocrates**, **Cicero**, and the like. Moreover this doctrine accustometh learners to the method and form of unfolding the virtues, and of dexterously resolving most Questions.

These four degrees, therefore, of twelve uses, which <!-- p.373 --> show the use of moral Philosophy, being considered, let us learn to make great account of the Ethical Elements of the virtues, and let us give thanks unto God for these remnants in the mind of man; and withal, in the acknowledgment and bewailing of our darkness and weakness, let us study to raise up these very rubbish-heaps ***καὶ ἐρείπια*** (and ruins) with the pillar and props of the heavenly doctrine, and let us pray God that for His Son's sake He would enlighten our minds by His Holy Spirit, bend our hearts unto godliness, and rule and direct our actions unto the praise and glory of His name, and unto the amendment of others and our own salvation. Amen.

## *De Tertio, id est, de Usu Philosophiae* · Of the Third Point, that is, of the Use of Philosophy

It is certain that truth, that is, the demonstrations in every art, is the gift of God, and a light divinely implanted in the nature of man; and that it is not only lawful for all, but that all are bound in all things, to use the truth, as in Arithmetic and Geometry; and that they are contumelious against God who either contradict manifest demonstrations, or will not assent unto them, because it is written: *"Thou shalt not bear false witness."* And the Son of Sirach saith, **chap. 4**: *"In no wise speak against the word of truth."* When therefore we hear true sentences in the arts, and in the whole of Philosophy, we ought to embrace them, and certainly to hold that, if we assent not unto them, or contradict without cause, we are liars.

Next it is to be known that it was most truly said by the Greeks: ***τὸ καλὸν ὅπου ἂν πόθ' εὑρεθείη, τῆς ἀληθείας ἴδιον ἐστί***: The good, wheresoever it be found, ought to be reckoned the property of truth. Wherefore if we find what is good, what is profitable, what is true and necessary in Philosophical doctrine, and in the Writings of the Heathen, so far is it from being unlawful for us to lay hold of those things, that we are even commanded by the divine voice to assent unto them; and we have the examples of Moses, who was learned in all the wisdom of the Egyptians, <!-- p.374 --> of David, of Solomon, of Daniel, of Paul the Apostle, who citeth verses of the Poets, of **Menander**, **Aratus**, **Empedocles**, etc.

But afterward it was inquired among Christians how, and how far, Philosophical letters are to be used. **Tertullian**, who flourished in the year of Christ 209, in the book *Of the Resurrection of the Flesh* affirmeth that he may safely use the Philosophical books, especially the Platonic, even in the things of God, as he himself speaketh, but *"for a testimony of the true, not for a help of the false"*; so far, namely, as the sentences of the Philosophers either are known by nature, as is the immortality of the soul with many, as is our God with all, or otherwise are not against the divine disposition. **Basil the Great**, who flourished in the year of Christ 369, in the Oration *Of the Profit of Reading the Writings of the Heathen*, setteth against the despisers of Philosophy the examples of Moses and Daniel, and at length affirmeth that the study of Philosophy is like unto the leaf, wherewith are covered and adorned the fruits which are plucked from the doctrine of the Gospel. The same saith **G. Nazianzen**, the hearer of Basil; and he saith that at Athens he applied himself to Philosophical studies, and gave diligence to get solid learning, and to complete, as far as might be, the ***ἐγκυκλοπαιδίαν*** (whole circle of learning), that afterward he might cast down all the erudition of learning, as a suppliant, upon the pavement and at the feet of Christ, to whose word all human doctrine ought to give place. And in the funeral Oration upon Basil, **Gregory** saith: *"Philosophical learning is not to be despised; nay, those are to be reckoned perverse and unlearned who despise it, seeing they would have all others to be like themselves, that so, in the common throng of those like themselves, they may lie hid, and escape the reproof of their own ignorance."* For most true is that which is said: That art hath no enemy save him that is ignorant of the art. And we see that most of those who at this day traduce liberal studies are unlearned. But let us hear one, **Augustine**, who flourished in the year of Christ 399, the disciple of **Ambrose**, in **lib. 2 de doctrina Christiana, cap. 40**, comparing Philosophy to the spoils of the Egyptians, which are to be <!-- p.375 --> turned to the use of the godly; and that in such sort, that one going out of Egypt rich, that is, learned and taught, be not puffed up, but keep the Passover, without which he cannot be saved, that is, serve Christ, who is our Passover, and who calleth unto Himself those whom He seeth labouring in Egypt: *"Come, all ye."* The same **Augustine** in the exposition of **Psalm 140** saith: That the Philosophers are indeed to be congratulated when they have spoken true things, but so to be congratulated, that their Writings give place to the rock, which is Christ.

Now whereas there is objected the saying of Paul, **Coloss. 2**: *"Beware lest any man spoil you through Philosophy"*, it is certain that Paul speaketh of the abuse and transforming of the heavenly doctrine into Philosophy, and of the confounding of the kinds of doctrines, and doth not censure demonstrations, which are truth divinely shown.

There is objected also that saying of **Gregory of Nyssa**, who was the brother of Basil the Great: *"We ought not to desire the monuments of men who were not comprehended in the true assembly of God upon earth, because it is written: My sheep hear my voice."* And **Bernard** saith: *"God hath commanded us that we learn, not the subtleties of Plato, nor the wily turns of Aristotle, but the doctrine of the Son of God."* **I answer.** These things are altogether true of the certainty of the heavenly doctrine, of the Christian life, of true Faith, of consolation, and of our salvation. But that doctrine can be adorned, unfolded and learnedly set forth by Philosophical letters, in those things which are required for the consideration of nature as created by God, and for the illustration of many sentences and similitudes taken from created things, and for outward discipline, is manifest, and confirmed by the judgement of all godly and sound men. So far therefore as Philosophical letters fight not with the word of God, they may truly be called the baskets into which the fragments are laid up; and when any Ethical doctrine falleth in, or similitudes are to be taken from Natural things, or deeds are to be illustrated by examples, those things which seem to make to the purpose may safely and conveniently be fetched out of the Writings, histories and sayings of the Philosophers; yet soberly, <!-- p.376 --> sparingly, modestly, and without ostentation of learning or of lofty speech. But as touching the rest, when the heads of saving doctrine are to be simply set forth, and the Articles of faith are to be handled, let the woman keep silence in the Church, that is, let not Philosophy the handmaid be preferred before her mistress, nor be mingled into the doctrine proper to the Church, but let her hear, be subject, and obey the voice of the Son of God.

## *De Quarto, de Fine scilicet Hominis* · Of the Fourth Point, namely, of the End of Man

The whole of Ethics is directed to the informing and instructing of minds concerning the understanding of the nature of virtue, and the exercising of honesty of life. Wherefore that doctrine beginneth from the Question of the end of man, showing in what Catalogue of good things virtue is seated, and by how much it is nearer to blessedness, or the chief good (*summum bonum*), than the other things for the sake of which it is by very many violated. This Question is of most ample argument, concerning which, if it were possible, godly minds would have wished to hear our first parents, the Patriarchs, the Prophets or the Apostles discoursing. But since we are destitute of the first sailing, we must use the second, and must speak of this Question as far as in this infancy and weakness we are able. Now before we recite the conjectures of the chief Philosophers, **Epicurus**, **Plato** and **Aristotle**, concerning the end of man and the chief good — passing over the opinions of others, whereof **Augustine**, *Of the City of God*, **lib. 19**, testifieth that two hundred and eighty-eight were surveyed and pointed out by **Marcus Varro** — it is worth the labour to set down beforehand the true sentence, founded in the word of God and drawn thence, and well confirmed by many arguments which cannot be broken; which being established, the Philosophical devices may afterward easily be compared with the true sentence, as coals with fire. But first <!-- p.377 --> there is to be acknowledged and bewailed not only the blindness of the mind of man, which knoweth not the true end unto which the nature of man was created, and wandereth in a marvellous manner hither and thither, and strayeth from the true end without limit; but the negligence also and security of the greatest part of men is to be bewailed, which will not consider wherefore men were created by God, and why they live, and unto what end and mark all actions are to be directed. That out of this security and profaneness there ariseth all ***ἀταξίαν*** (disorder) in life, all heinous deeds and all calamities, and punishments public and private, is not doubtful, while man followeth after and undertaketh things diverse from his end, and often contrary to it, preferring before the chief good either pleasures and unlawful delights, or the most trifling trifles of riches, splendour, eminence, and other things frail and very often vanishing more swiftly than men think. This madness and dulness, seeing that even the sounder Heathen themselves have either derided and hissed it off, as it is manifest that **Democritus** laughed at the follies of men, and for that cause was named *Gelasinus* (the Laugher); or have bewailed it with weeping and continual complaints, as we read that **Heraclitus** did; or have altogether contemned it, as **Diogenes**; or have most sharply reproved it, as **Persius** crieth out:

> O the cares of men! O how much emptiness there is in things!

And **Juvenal**, **Satyr. 10**, saith:

> In all the lands which reach from Gades even unto  
> The Dawn and the Ganges, few are able to discern  
> True goods, and things much differing from them, the cloud  
> Of error being removed, etc.

Likewise **Lucretius**:

> O wretched minds of men! O blind breasts!  
> In what darkness of life, and in how great perils,  
> Is passed this span of age, whatsoever it be!

or have also studied to amend it, as the books written *Of Ends* and *Of Manners* testify, and the Paraenetic and <!-- p.378 --> Ethical poems of the Poets, the sum and argument whereof **Persius** reciteth, **Satyr. 3**, saying:

> Learn, O ye wretched, and know the causes of things:  
> What we are, or for what manner of life we are begotten; what order  
> Is given; or where is the gentle turning of the goal, and whence;  
> What measure there is for silver; what it is right to wish; what the rough  
> Coin hath of profit; to thy country and thy dear kinsfolk  
> How much it is fitting to bestow; whom God hath bidden thee  
> To be, and in what part of the human estate thou art placed,[^P18-4]  
> Learn, etc.

Seeing, I say, that the Heathen have done this, we who are members and citizens of the Church ought deservedly to labour with all our powers, that by the study of the divine word, by invocation, and by the exercise of godliness we may amend our madness and sloth, and may consider our true end; which how it can and ought to be done by us, is now to be subjoined.

In the Church the chief good is God Himself, the eternal, the fountain, wellspring and origin of all good, as the Son of God saith, **Luc. 18**: *"None is good, save God"*, namely in origin and in perfection. This chief good is from everlasting, without beginning and end, needing no other good, but ***αὐταρκὲς πρὸς εὐδαιμονίαν***, as **Plato** speaketh, that is, to which nothing is wanting unto felicity. Now since good is communicative of itself, at a certain time He created man after His own image, that he might acknowledge and celebrate God. Before the fall of man, therefore, the chief good was God Himself, or the entire and perfect acknowledgment and celebration of God, or the very image and likeness of God in man, resting, living and rejoicing in God his Creator. And this was the end of man as created by God, namely, that man should come most near unto the chief good, that is, should entirely and perfectly acknowledge and celebrate God, and should refer all his thoughts, words and deeds unto His glory, and should wholly live, rest and rejoice in God, the chief good. But after the fall, seeing that man hath blotted out and lost the principal end, and the use, for <!-- p.379 --> which he was created, straying from the mark and from the straight leading unto the chief good, being made in truth the mask of the Devil, and now tending unto the uttermost evil, namely unto damnation and eternal death; although he is yet endued with some knowledge of God from the law, yet he hath nothing any more that is solid or entire, but strayeth and wandereth in a fleeting and vanishing shadow, and hath nothing else save the ***φρόνημα σαρκὸς*** (mind of the flesh), even when he thinketh that he hath the chief good, or at the least that by virtue, or by the action of virtue, he can come unto the chief good. For *"all flesh is grass, and all the goodliness thereof is as the flower of the field: the grass withereth, and the flower fadeth"*, **Esa. 40**. And in **Psalm 14** it is written: *"They are corrupt, and are become abominable in their doings; there is none that doeth good. The Lord looked down from heaven upon the children of men, to see if there were any that did understand, or seek God. They are all gone aside, and are become unprofitable; there is none that doeth good, no, not one."* And in **Esa. 53** it is said: *"All we like sheep have gone astray; every one in his own ways"*, that is, no man knoweth the true end of his Creation and life, much less the highest blessedness. Whatsoever therefore hath been disputed concerning the end of man, or concerning ends and the chief good, by wise men destitute of the light of the Gospel, that whole thing is only the ***φρόνημα σαρκὸς*** (mind of the flesh), and a pleasant little flower, which a little after groweth yellow. Now since the natural property of the chief good is this, that it is not only communicative of itself, but also gathereth unto itself and preserveth what is its own, therefore God, who is the chief good, of His immeasurable and highest goodness sent us the Son, who should bring forth out of the bosom of the Father, and accomplish, the decree concerning the restoring of the end of man, and the bringing back of man to the acknowledgment of the chief good; which thing the Son of God performeth, the word and the Holy Spirit being given. In the word He first leadeth us to the acknowledgment of our darkness and of our miseries. Next He leadeth us forth out of this darkness, showing unto us His own self, the Redeemer and Saviour, and so He gleweth, incorporateth and ingrafteth us unto Himself, bringing us back, through Himself and in Himself, to the acknowledgment and the help <!-- p.380 --> of our end. Afterward He giveth us the Holy Spirit as enlightener, who in this life beginneth in us the actions which properly pertain to the true end of man, that is, He kindleth and confirmeth in us the true light, the acknowledgment of God, invocation, patience, consolation, and the other virtues which in the Church we name the fruits of faith and of the Holy Spirit; and in this manner He leadeth us through this life, until we attain the last end, which is eternal life, wherein, face to face, we shall acknowledge, behold, embrace, possess and celebrate unto all eternity the chief good, namely God the Father, the Son, and the Holy Spirit. Of this end of man, and of the chief good, these sayings preach: of **Psalm 73**: *"If I have but thee, I care not for heaven or earth; and though my flesh and my soul waste away, yet art thou the rock of my heart, and my portion, O God, for ever."* And hither pertaineth the prayer of Moses, **Psalm 90**, the sentence whereof surpasseth the wisest thoughts of all unregenerate men concerning the end of man. And **Psalm 4**: *"There be many that say, Who will shew us any good? But, Lord, lift thou up the light of thy countenance upon us. Thou hast put gladness in my heart, while others abound in wine and oil."* Likewise, **Matth. 6**: *"Seek ye first the Kingdom of God, and his righteousness."* And **John 17**: *"This is life eternal, that they might know thee the only true God, and that he whom thou hast sent, Jesus, is the Christ."* And **1 Petr. 1**: *"Ye shall receive the end of your faith, even the salvation of your souls."* This is the one and true sentence concerning the end of man, and concerning the highest, first and last good, which the voice of God alone, comprehended in the Prophetic and Apostolic writings, showeth unto us, and from which all other monuments stray and wander in their dreams, while one seeketh this end, another that, and one good and then another. But let us pray the Son of God, that He would so rule us by His Holy Spirit, that we may daily think upon our end, and consider the causes why we men were created, why we were Redeemed, washed and sprinkled with the precious blood of the Son of God, why we are named and are the temples and dwelling-places of the Holy Spirit, why we <!-- p.381 --> live, why we are endued with mind, judgement and reason, with will and affections, whither we are tending, where and before what Judge we shall one day appear; and that so we may direct all our thoughts, all our studies, counsels and actions unto the glory and praise of the divine name, may be humble and circumspect, and may resist the unlawful enticements of the devil, of the world and of our own flesh, which lead us away from our end, being mindful of that most sweet saying which the Son of God uttered as He hung upon the cross, **Psalm 22**: *"I was cast upon thee from the womb"*, namely, not only that thou mightest preserve me, but that I might serve thy glory. And excellent is the saying of the Emperor **Constantine**: *"I certainly believe that our whole soul altogether, and whatsoever I breathe, and in a word whatsoever stirreth inmost in the mind, is owed by us unto the most high God."* For a man is not a true man, unless he acknowledge and worship God, and serve Him. And worthy is that word of **Ludovicus Vives**, to be most often considered by all the godly: *"Love, as much as ever thou canst, Him who is above thee, and him who is nigh thee not otherwise than thyself; which if thou do, thou alone shalt know and keep all laws and rights, which others with great sweat scarce learn, and by many spurs and terrors scarce keep. Thou shalt be a greater Philosopher than Pythagoras and Plato with all their travels and numbers; greater than Aristotle with his disputations and the books he wrote."* Let us ask also that our weakness, negligence and sloth be forgiven us, and be amended from day to day, that we may grow up daily more and more in true acknowledgment, true fear, true obedience, faith and virtue, and may see God gracious unto us in all eternity.
