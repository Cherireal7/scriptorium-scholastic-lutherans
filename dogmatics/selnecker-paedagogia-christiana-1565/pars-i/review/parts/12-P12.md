<!-- p.241 -->

Now seeing that usuries cannot be approved and defended even by natural judgement, **Aristotle** rightly saith: *"Usury is against nature, since money was made for the sake of exchange, not that it should stand in the place of merchandise."* At this day also Germany is choked with this wickedness, and other nations with her. And it is to be feared lest this evil at the last destroy Germany, although now all men laugh when such things are spoken; even as that wealthy merchant had said, when **Luther** had written of usuries: *"That good man and Divine ought to have committed this business to me, who practise usuries, and know what they are and what they effect."* There are also crafty men who seek out evasions, and for the most part put forward these objections as their pleas.

**The First Objection.**

**Deut. 23.** God forbiddeth that any man lend upon usury to his brother; but unto a stranger and an alien he permitteth it. Therefore usuries are not altogether forbidden of God?

**I answer:** I deny the consequence; and the reason is, because that commandment was a singular one, out of which a Rule cannot be built, which is rather to be built out of the universal commandment of God. God granteth that usury be practised upon strangers, or the Gentiles, for this cause, that by that wrath of His, and by just judgement, He may claim and appropriate the goods of the Gentiles unto His own people. And this is an Example of the wrath of God, when He willeth to strip nations of their sinews, as is now done in Germany. So God did many peculiar things among His people, and by them, as when He commanded to slay the Gentiles and to spoil the goods of the Gentiles. But the Rule is to be observed: *A special Law giveth place to a general Law.* And **Epiphanius** disputeth rightly, showing the ***Δικαιοκρισίαν τοῦ θεοῦ*** (righteous judgement of God), who restored anew unto His own people the goods seized by the Gentiles, as when He restored Palestine, or the land of Canaan, which had been seized by the sons of Ham, unto the sons of Shem.

**The Second Objection.**

The sentence of **Luke 6** speaketh not of usuries, but of free <!-- p.242 --> offices of kindness. Therefore they do ill who affirm that usuries are forbidden by that sentence.

**I answer.**

Although it were true that that sentence speaketh not of usuries, yet there are other sentences, more in number and more evident, which forbid usuries, as hath been said above. But that the sentence of Christ speaketh of usuries also, is manifest from the particle *Nothing*, when He saith: *"Hoping for nothing again."* And so Christ comprehendeth two things most briefly, whereof the former is, that we succour our neighbour, even though we know that he cannot do good to us in return, and so distinguish charity from carnal friendship, or a free office from a mercenary affection, whereby, as **Plato** saith, we bend back upon ourselves the love wherewith we ought to follow others. The latter is, that we seek not gain above the principal (*sors*), but know that usuries do truly displease God. And this particle *Nothing* is in no wise wrongly expounded of usury. For under the genus the species also is comprehended, a thing which is known to boys.

**The Third Objection.**

> The dispensation of the Magistrate is of force.
> The Magistrate hath granted usuries, and hath appointed a measure unto them.
> Therefore usuries are lawful.

**I answer.**

The major speaketh of a dispensation which doth not fight with the word of God and with the Law of nature. But seeing that the Magistrate hath at this day in most places granted usuries, let that be, if you will, a political and judicial Law, and a certain determination, that I may name it with a gentle word. And because custom hath so prevailed that this matter is found scarce able to be changed, nothing further can be answered to the minor of this argument, save that which Christ <!-- p.243 --> answereth to the Question concerning the bill of divorcement granted by Moses, namely, that it was granted because of the hardness of the hearts of the people. The same do I answer in this place also: that because of the security of men, and their hardness, that custom hath grown strong, but yet against the first will of God expressed in the word of God. Likewise: against natural judgement. Likewise: against the Laws, which deliver this Rule: *A man ought not, as a rule, to receive anything beyond the principal.* Yet that ordinance also could and ought to be made, that by right of sale five should be paid upon the hundred, if at this day this political permission were not violated beyond measure, and other sins heaped up under the pretext of sale.

**The Fourth Objection.**

> Usury which burdeneth not a neighbour, but profiteth him, is lawful.
> Now oftentimes a poor man becometh richer by the money for which he hath paid usury.

**I answer.**

Seeing it hath been said that usuries are forbidden of God, the distinction between a burdening usury and a profiting usury cannot stand. Neither are sins to be judged by the advantages of men, but by the word of God, and by conscience. Nor doth that hold either which is said, that usury is called by the Hebrews *Nesec*, from biting, and is forbidden so far forth as it burdeneth a neighbour. For it is named also *Marbith*, or *Tarbith*, from augmentation, or increase, **Leviticus 25**: *"Thou shalt not give him thy victuals Marbith,"* that is, upon usury. *"Thou shalt not take of him Nesec and Tarbith,"* usury and increase. Any augmentation whatsoever, therefore, exceeding the principal, is forbidden. And so fall all Sophistical shifts, which against the Law of God and the offices of charity seek out malicious evasions.

<!-- p.244 -->

**The Fifth Objection.**

> He that provideth for his children, wife and kinsfolk whereof they may live, doth rightly.
> He that putteth out his moneys to usury provideth for his own, that they may have whereof to live, even when he himself is dead, the principal nevertheless remaining.
> Therefore it is not evil to take usuries, as a sustenance of life.

**I answer.**

This argument many use at this day, and think themselves well excused by this pretext. But they consider not that every man ought to provide for his own in a just and lawful manner, not forbidden of God, not unlawfully against the commandment of God and the Law of nature. And although they say that they provide lawfully, seeing they give their moneys upon usury by the grant of the Magistrate, yet ought each man to take counsel for his own conscience, and to see what God approveth; neither ought he to distrust God, as though He were not willing or not able by His blessing to nourish and sustain children also and posterity. But we speak of usuries exceeding all measure, which many use at this day under that pretext, as though they so provided for their own. For as touching the poor, and chiefly the ministers of the Church, who have no certain seat, and who oftentimes have need of a sure provision for the way, and who lay up at interest for their children — for whom after their decease scarce any man careth — those things which God supplieth unto them in the sweat of their face (if only there be any that are worthy of the name): the case is otherwise with them, unto whom alms also are otherwise due, as is said elsewhere.

**The Sixth Objection.**

> A scruple is not to be cast into consciences, when usuries are reproved as though they were condemned of God. For at this day there are many good men who put out moneys to usury, moved both by custom, and by the grant of the Magistrate, and not[^P12-1] (which is the <!-- p.245 --> principal thing) by ***πλεονεξίᾳ*** (the desire of having more) and covetousness.
> Therefore they do not rightly who reprove usuries universally.

**I answer to the major.**

Consciences are not to be troubled when they are simple and teachable; not so when they wilfully persevere in sins. He therefore who knoweth that lending upon interest and usury is forbidden of God, and yet ceaseth not to lend upon usury, the same troubleth his own conscience himself, and maketh himself guilty of theft. This sin is assuredly to be blamed by the word of God, and the conscience is to be admonished, that it may repent, and being at length awakened may cease to do that which it knoweth God hath forbidden. So are all sins reproved, that consciences may be awakened and repent.

**The Seventh Objection.**

> That which cannot be changed is not to be stirred.
> Usuries at this day cannot be taken away, neither can merchandise any longer be exercised without usuries.
> Therefore it is better to keep silence concerning usuries than to reprove them.

**I answer.**

This is the most sorrowful argument of secure men, of whom it is said: *"Men will no longer suffer themselves to be ruled and bowed by my Spirit,"* **Genes. 6**. And yet the godly are to be admonished even unto hoarseness, that they consider these calamities and punishments, both present and impending. For it is altogether to be feared lest this be the punishment of Germany, to say nothing of other nations, that she be first sucked dry, and afterward, exhausted and bereft of sinews, be able to resist neither the Turk nor other barbarous enemies, seeing that neither lords nor subjects have whence to furnish the charges. So much, forsooth, shall we bring to pass by our merchandisings and usurious exchanges. There are also many other <!-- p.246 --> sins which cannot be changed, even as neither can the whole kingdom of the Devil be turned away in this life; and yet sins are to be reproved by the voice of God, that some may be converted and be saved.

### *Unde autem crescunt tot et tantae usurae?* · But Whence Do So Many and So Great Usuries Grow?

I. From the hatred[^P12-2] of the Devil against God and against mankind, who blindeth men.

II. From the distrust of men toward God.

III. From the security of men.

IIII. From covetousness and ***πλεονεξίᾳ*** (the desire of having more).

V. From excessive pomp, pride, prodigality. For **Cicero** saith truly: *"Largess hath no bottom."* Likewise: *"Rapines follow upon largess."*

### *Quae causae nos maxime deterrere debent ab usura* · What Causes Ought Most of All to Deter Us from Usury

I. The Commandment of God.

II. The light of nature, or natural judgement.

III. A good conscience, faith, the Holy Spirit, etc. For these are shaken out by sins against conscience.

IIII. Temporal punishments, of which it is said: *"In goods ill gotten the third heir rejoiceth not."* *"Ill gotten, ill gone."*

And **Ovid**:

> Sordid booty hath no good issues.  

And **Publianus**:[^P12-3]

> Gain with an ill name is to be called loss.  
> Gain in the chest, loss in the conscience.  

To these are to be joined eternal punishments also, **1 Corinth. 6**: *"Thieves and covetous shall not inherit the kingdom of God."*

V. The snares and noose of usuries, so that if thou have once been accustomed to them, thou canst not easily leave off from them, but art cast out of sin into <!-- p.247 --> sin. Hence usuries are named *sweet* by the Poets. And **Chrysostom** saith: *"The money of the usurer is like unto the bite of the asp. For he that is smitten of the asp goeth as one delighted unto sleep, and through the sweetness of slumber dieth, because then the venom runneth secretly through all his members: so he that receiveth upon usury feeleth for the time as it were a benefit, but the usury runneth through all his substance, and turneth the whole into debt."*

VI. The judgements of honest and excellent men, who have always detested usuries. Four Christian councils, of Nicaea, of Constantinople, of Ephesus and of Chalcedon, have forbidden usuries. **Gregory** writeth: *"We judge that they are not to be ordained who are convicted of having received usuries."* **Cato** said that the usurer ought to be punished fourfold. **Cicero** saith that to lend upon usury is all one as to rob and to kill men. **Aristotle** nameth usurers base hucksters. **Cato** cast them out of the island of Sicily. And there are many other examples in the histories.

VII. The Devil is the author of usuries, and the chief usurer, gathering unto himself out of usuries and thefts the immense treasures of the world. Oftentimes also he giveth a specimen of his usury in them who entrust themselves to him that they may have money. At Wittenberg, while **Luther** lived, a certain boy is reported to have been sent by his parents unto his studies. When his parents gave not money into his hands, but committed it to his Preceptor, and the boy nevertheless would not be without money, he calleth upon the Devil, and having made a contract with the Devil, who appeared, he bound himself by a bill written in his own blood, which he delivered to the Devil. The boy, having money, is asked oftentimes whence he hath received it; and when nothing certain could be wrung from him, at last, being often and earnestly questioned, he confesseth with tears his ungodliness. Here was the task, here the toil. The boy is brought to **D. Luther**, who, having called together other godly men, heareth from the boy how the thing was done. Then, earnest prayers being made together unto God with the boy, the Devil is at last compelled to give back the bill written in the boy's blood. Afterward <!-- p.248 --> the boy was instructed, absolved, and so delivered from the tyranny of the Demon. And of Roswitha, Abbess of Gandersheim, daughter, as is reported, of a Greek Emperor, a like history is related: that she wrested from the Devil a bill, written in his own blood, whereby a certain boy had bound himself to him. And elsewhere more things have happened, which it is too dreadful and horrible to rehearse. And let thus much have been said concerning usuries; which whoso defendeth, and gathereth together probable reasons, let him do, if he will, according to his own conscience, and have always in sight the Rule: *"Whatsoever is not of faith"* — that is, whatsoever is done with a sticking and doubting conscience — *"is sin."*

But this also is to be known, that when usuries are condemned, by no means are all revenues and contracts condemned without any difference. For many revenues are the tributes of governments,[^P12-4] laid upon lands at the beginning, that thence the Magistrates may have their necessary charges. **Pisistratus** at Athens took the twentieth part of the fruits, that is, of an hundred measures five. Joseph appointed the fifth part, **Genes. 47**. The Law of Moses appointed the tenth. These tributes, other things being equal,[^P12-5] are not condemned, because unto the Magistrate a stipend is due, **Luke 3**, **Roman. 13**. Howbeit let those tributes be not excessive, not immoderate exactions, such as are at this day made everywhere to the great hurt of nations, of lords and of subjects; of which calamity **Micah 3** complaineth. But of these things somewhat hath been said in the fourth Commandment, and now the matter is come so far that no man knoweth what more can be said or done. But let every Magistrate, who hath but any care of his own salvation, beware lest he run upon that horrible judgement of God which is in **Psal. 107**: *"He poureth contempt upon princes."* And let not subjects be seditious, but let them acknowledge their sins and punishments, and call upon God that He may mitigate the punishments; who assuredly will not be wanting with His help unto them that call upon Him, according to the saying: *"When the bricks are doubled, Moses cometh."* Likewise: *"The hours of God are slow, but they are greatly longed for."* Likewise: *"Needs must the help of God be at hand, where the help of <!-- p.249 --> man ceaseth."* Of the remaining contracts it is spoken elsewhere in its own place.

IIII. Now hither pertaineth an admonition unto all hearers concerning other thefts, as are those which are done in the corrupting of weights and measures — a thing most usual at this day in the shops of merchants. Likewise: in the selling of things too much raised and enhanced in price, as do Handicraftsmen, Tailors, Shoemakers, and others. Likewise: the Countrymen, even with their pot-herbs and onions, to say nothing of other things. Likewise: as they do who are the greatest of all thieves, who buy up corn in hope of dearth to come, that they may sell at the greater price, as many nobles and citizens do; unto whom oftentimes that corn also is at last scattered to the winds. Likewise: they who sell things dearer than is wont to be done in the public markets, or who even buy up all things in the markets, that they alone may have them and alone may sell at their pleasure; which thing is forbidden by the Civil law also, even as all monopolies of that kind are forbidden. And these manifest Thieves, Robbers and Usurers are unworthy to be and to live in the assembly of Christians. And therefore also the Jews are not to be suffered by the Civil Magistrate, both for this cause, and also for other causes: that they are blasphemers against God, the Father of our Lord Jesus Christ (and blasphemy is not to be suffered); that they are manifest thieves and usurers; that they are most hostile to the blood of Christians, and oftentimes even kill little boys, that they may get possession of their blood. Likewise: that they are for the most part traitors; and in sum, that their usefulness, as regardeth the preservation of the Christian name, is none, but the hurt, as regardeth the destruction of that name, is exceeding great.[^P12-6] These things by the way.

Now somewhat must be said of private thefts. There is indeed yet one kind of public thefts, the thieves whereof wear golden chains about their necks, and administer the highest affairs in gold and Purple, and press those that are subject to them with many burdens, and have great titles — they are called valiant, magnificent, noble, wise, and most high; but these also are deservedly comprehended in the mention of private thieves, and are numbered with them, whether they take it ill or quietly. <!-- p.250 --> Now such are they who, regarding their own advantages, are unfaithful to the lords whom they serve, and are bitter and hard to the subjects over whom they are set, and (as is at this day most usual in courts) are most niggardly and most sordid as touching the helping of others, whether in the Schools, or in the Churches, or in common life; and are on the contrary most prodigal as touching themselves, and will have all things bestowed and given unto themselves. And so by their niggardliness and sparing they save a fly; but they themselves swallow down Camels, and serve aright neither their lords nor the subjects, but themselves alone, and procure what they can, and are craving and ravening. And in this number are the chief Stewards of affairs, and Householders, Chamberlains, Judges, Nobles, Governors, Captains of soldiers, Treasurers, and infinite others, of all whom it is said: ***τάμιαι λάμιαι*** (stewards are Lamias, devouring monsters), for that their throat can never be filled and satisfied; not that the office itself is unjust, and the name of the office dishonest, but because the abuse is to be reproved. And at this day well-nigh all these are such as have long since fallen into these three sins:

I. That they suffer not themselves any longer to be admonished, nor anything to be said unto them, but are proud, lifted up, stubborn and arrogant, and deem that all things lie in themselves, like that Judge, **Luke 18**: *"Thus I will, thus I command; so let my will stand for a reason."*

II. They bend and bend back the judgement of God according to their own affections, and that they do in four ways: either from fear, or from bribery, or from love, or from hatred. For from the fear wherewith they fear their superiors, they succour not the oppressed, nay rather they press down the inferior, and at the presence and dignity of superiors they are dumb, even as Pilate, fearing Caesar, whom the Jews put forward as a pretext, delivereth Christ to be crucified. And such there are at this day very many everywhere, who would sooner suffer an immense multitude of Citizens and Subjects to be afflicted with a thousand wrongs than that by one little word they should offend those whom they know to be their superiors, as it is said in the seventh of **Micah**: *"Whatsoever the Prince willeth, that saith the Judge, that he in turn may obtain something of the Prince; and the Great men give counsel according <!-- p.251 --> to their wantonness, that they may do evil, and bend the law at their pleasure, etc."* And from bribery and from covetousness, Judges corrupted with gifts neglect the causes of the inferior sort, and favour the richer; they hang Thieves into the purse, and behead Robbers with thalers; they are companions of thieves, **Isaiah 1**; they take gifts, and follow after rewards, and neglect the Fatherless and Widows, and will not hear them; they judge according to gifts, **Mic. 3**, **Exod. 18**. And from love they pervert the law, when, moved by ***προσωποληψίᾳ*** (respect of persons), they keep not equality, but favour and spare these, and are bitter and hard to those; they take away from the wretched, and bestow largely upon friends. Friendship indeed there ought to be, but not save *as far as the altars*, as antiquity hath spoken. Friends are to be gratified so long as it can be done without ungodliness and wickedness, as **Pericles**: when a friend asked that he would confirm his false cause by perjury, he said: ***Δεῖ μὲ συμπράττειν τοῖς φίλοις, ἀλλὰ μέχρι θεῶν*** (I must work together with my friends, but only as far as the gods). But most men at this day imitate **Agesilaus**, commending a friend to a Judge in these words: *"Nicias, if he hath not an unjust cause, dismiss and acquit; but if he hath an unjust cause, dismiss him for my sake; but by all means dismiss him."* But meanwhile where abideth the poor and needy man? Lastly, from hatred they bend the law, when they are angry with any man, being a little offended, and so condemn many wretched men, and wipe them clean with a fine, the cause nevertheless not being heard, even as Ahab slew the godly and simple Naboth, his cause unheard, upon the accusation of Jezebel, **3 Kings 21**. Such are many found among those Thrasos, Treasurers, Nobles, who have command over beasts — over the wretched countrymen — of whom it is said, **Job 5**: *"Wrath, hatred and envy slay the foolish, or the mad man."* For very rage these men, once offended, know not whether they be in their right mind; they neither hear nor see. And these are the ways whereby at this day the law is bent beyond measure, after the manner of wax. The third sin is, that these men oppress the poor, and them that are subject to them, in three ways chiefly: either with excessive labours and burdens, as the Nobles and Treasurers do without care; or with excessive pillagings and fines, concerning which <!-- p.252 --> **Mic. chap. 3** preacheth most severely, which sermon is diligently to be considered; or lastly by robbing them of the just wage for the labours of the subjects, a thing which at this day is not only usual, but is even ascribed unto praise. And of the avoiding of this theft speaketh Moses, **Levit. 19**, when he interpreteth the seventh Commandment, in these words: *"Thou shalt not defraud thy neighbour, neither rob him: the wages of him that is hired shall not abide with thee all night until the morning."* And **Deut. 24**: *"Thou shalt not keep back the hire of the needy and poor, whether he be of thy brethren, or of the strangers; but thou shalt pay him his hire by day, neither shall the sun go down upon it, lest he cry against thee unto the Lord, etc."* And **Sirach 35**: *"The poor man hath nothing else save a morsel of bread; he that robbeth him of this is a murderer. He that giveth not the labourer his hire is cruel, and guilty of blood."*

Now the wage is withdrawn, besides this manner of which we have now spoken, yet in two ways more, namely in the courts of judgement, and in private life or conversation. In the courts of judgement, when the causes of the poor are too much prolonged and deferred; and so, although that which is deferred is not always taken away, yet the poor man cannot obtain his portion until beyond the time of the wage that was earned or of the goods that were owing. And that this is a heinous sin is plain from the Mosaic Laws, **Levit. 19** and **Deut. 24**. Yet the prolonging itself is not accused, when it is necessary and the matter hath need of counsel; but the negligence is reproved whereby, in favour of the more powerful, the Judges will not make haste unto the deciding of the cause. Next, that which is owing is withdrawn in private conversation, when debtors will not satisfy their creditors at the time determined, and either pay slowly and grudgingly, or, as oftentimes cometh to pass, flatly deny the debt. And such for the most part are they who easily and gladly take upon loan.

Furthermore, unjust wages and stipends also are thefts, which are devoured by the slothful and idle, while in the mean time they that serve faithfully well-nigh pine away with hunger. So in the courts of Princes many are maintained who are unworthy that the earth should bear them: Thrasos, whisperers, players, informers, robbers also, and <!-- p.253 --> fools. Likewise: they who in the public ways spoil the goods of wayfaring men, and slay many. In Households, when the fathers of families afford victuals to many without difference — to *good fellows*, as the Germans speak — who empty out all their substance, as it is said in the verse:

> ***Σώματα πόλλα τρέφειν, καὶ δώματα πόλλ' ἀναγείρειν.***  
> ***Ἀτραπὸς εἰς πενίην ἐστὶν ἑτοιμοτάτη.***  
> (To feed many bodies, and to raise up many houses,  
> is the readiest path unto poverty.)  

Among the learned there hath lately begun at this day the theft of certain drones, who dedicate some one written phantasm unto many patrons, that, the title only and the name of the dedication being changed, they may deceive many, and wipe them of some small gift. A thing most unworthy and most audacious, and a slothfulness joined with unspeakable impudence and most foul covetousness.

But of slothfulness and idleness somewhat hath been said above also. Now unnecessary beggary also is to be censured in sturdy beggars, who had rather seek their living from door to door than discharge honest labours. And of such beggars Germany is at this day full, who are oftentimes betrayers of their country, robbers, and the most faithless of all men; as in the year 1560 Wittenberg showed an horrible example in a certain raging beggar, who slew his hostess, a woman lying in childbed, in the beer-cellar, having given her well-nigh threescore wounds.

Hither also it pertaineth that mention be made of those gamesters who, for profit and gain's sake, give themselves to this one thing, so that they think gaming to be their only calling. Such are not to be suffered by the Magistrate.

But now, being wearied, I may not number more kinds of thefts, seeing that every estate and order in every kind of life is full of them, insomuch that by some the world also hath been called a stable of thieves. And how great craft and subtilty there is in the committing and the covering of thefts, **Herodotus** hath shown ***ἱστορικῶς*** (after the manner of history), when he rehearseth the notable theft committed by the Egyptians, **book 2**.

## *De Regula Augustini: Peccatum non dimittitur, nisi ablatum restituatur* · Of the Rule of Augustine: The Sin Is Not Forgiven, Unless That Which Was Taken Away Be Restored

This Rule is simply true in general of all who knowingly and willingly keep back that which is another's, and have whence they may restore, or are able to restore, as **Paul** saith: *"Let him that stole steal no more."* Now he that hath stolen is so long a thief as he knowingly and willingly keepeth back that which is another's; even as he that hath carried off another man's wife is so long a ravisher, or an adulterer, as he keepeth her. And seeing Paul forbiddeth to steal any more, he willeth that which is another's to be restored, that he who hath invaded that which is another's may cease to be a thief. This general Rule is to be held. Howbeit impossibility hath its excuse, and there are divers cases in which ***ἐπιείκεια*** (equity, moderation) is to be used; as in certain secret businesses offences are to be avoided, and infamy is to be avoided, because the loss of good name is greater than the loss of goods.

But it is to be looked to that consciences also be provided for. For he that keepeth a theft cannot have a quiet conscience, although he provide for his good name; concerning which the Germans say: *To steal and to restore is a matter of infamy.*

In old time manifest thefts were punished with a fourfold restitution of that which was taken away; but private thefts were punished with a twofold restitution. And so Zacchaeus also corrected himself, **Luke 20**: *"Behold, Lord, the half of my goods I give to the poor; and if I have defrauded any man, I restore him fourfold."*

No theft therefore ought to be remitted, unless that which was taken away be restored. Let the sacrilegious and the usurers, public and private, restore their thefts, and the sin shall be remitted unto them. Let those great thieves restore the Ecclesiastical revenues, and they shall be absolved; but if they will not, and have regard unto their good name, let them keep, if they will, gain in the chest, loss in the conscience, and let them know that they possess those goods neither godly, nor justly, nor honestly, howsoever they say that they hold them by prescription. Rather let the Rule be known: *A possessor in bad faith prescribeth at no time.* Now he is a possessor <!-- p.255 --> in bad faith who knowingly trafficketh against the prohibitions of the Laws and against equity, and possesseth anything by an evil title, as it is wont commonly to be said. Likewise: *Those things which are done against law ought assuredly to be held as not done.*

Now seeing that at this day, by the Ordinance of the Emperor Frederick the Second, because of the exceeding frowardness and contumacy of men, thieves are beheaded, it is wont to be asked whether to restore the thing taken profiteth the taker toward the avoiding of the punishment. To which Question it is wont to be answered: First, that the degrees of thefts, or the circumstances, are to be considered. For there are certain common and usual thefts, which are committed, either by day or by night, by the secret taking away of a thing. Worse than these are the thefts which are done with doors and chests broken open, by certain instruments. Thirdly, there are yet worse and more heinous than these, those whereby goods are snatched away in the public ways; and such men are beheaded without any difference, even though they restore the thing, as the Lawyers dispute concerning *goods taken by violence* (*de vi bonorum raptorum*). And hither pertain sacrileges also, which in many places are punished with fire. Fourthly, the most heinous thefts are theirs who slay men for the getting of goods. And these are robbers, and are punished absolutely, both for the theft and for the murder, as he was punished at Vienna who had slain a matron, her maidservant, and her little daughter, who besought him with the promise of her own treasure. And other examples there are everywhere, too many.

These degrees being considered, of the first two it may be said that sometimes ***ἐπιείκειαν*** (equity) may be used, if restitution be made, or even if it be not made, according to the circumstances of the person and of the thing. And where ***ἐπιείκεια*** may be used hath been said above in the fifth Commandment. Now hither pertaineth the Rule of the Lawyers: *Pardon of a sin is not given, save to him that is corrected.* And correction, as they themselves speak, is threefold: *Verbal*, when he that hath offended is sharply reproved, just threatenings being added, that if he return to the same offence he is to be punished more sharply. *Real*, with infamy, or with restitution, or with punishment. *Mental*, in a contrite heart, of which it is said: *Neither the <!-- p.256 --> verbal nor the real profit, unless the mental correction come between, as touching God.*

It is asked also what is to be done with him that cannot restore. For it is said: *No man is bound to that which is impossible.* I answer: If the theft be so great that it is worthy of punishment, why should not execution be done? But if the circumstances be considered, ***ἐπιείκεια*** may be used, worthy of a godly and prudent Magistrate; yet so that licence be not confirmed. And it is rightly said: *Natural impossibility (as if I promise to any man the Sun, or the Moon, or other things of that kind) excuseth the debtor; not the difficulty of the person.*

It is asked likewise whether things taken in wars are to be restored. I answer: They that wage just wars become lords of things and of bodies, and justly hold the bodies and things which they take. Now those are just and lawful wars which are undertaken by lawful powers, for the repelling of unjust violence and robbery, or for the punishing of the manifest wickedness of some subject people, even as the Israelites moved war to punish the tribe of Benjamin because of the defiling of the Levite's wife. But seeing that war is one of the bitterest punishments wherewith God punisheth the world, very many things fall out in wars more heinous than the exact rule of justice requireth. Neither can the mean in these actions be precisely determined; but if the principal cause be just, the Warrior shall know that that warfare is lawful, and that the things seized are justly possessed. Howbeit a godly Warrior will not undertake any save necessary wars, and will moderate unnecessary cruelty. Let the verse also be in sight:

> The better cause biddeth us hope for favouring gods.  

And let thus much have been said of the first question, namely, what ways of getting riches are lawful. Now of the two that remain somewhat is briefly to be added.

## *De Quaestione Secunda: Quomodo nos Deus erga divitias velit esse affectos* · Of the Second Question: How God Willeth Us to Be Affected toward Riches

Unto riches that are gotten a singular art is to be applied, of which **Psalm 61 [62]** preacheth: *"If riches increase, set not your heart upon them."* And **1 Timoth. 6**: *"Charge them that are rich in this world, that they be not highminded, nor trust in uncertain riches, but in the living God."* Away therefore with deceitful hope in uncertain riches, and let there be present a true hope in the bestower of riches, who is able to give and to take away; away with pride and contempt of the poor, and let there be present a godly and humble mind. But where is this done by the rich? Therefore Christ saith: *"It is easier for a camel to go through the eye of a needle, than for a rich man to enter into the Kingdom of heaven."* And **Augustine** saith: *"It is hard that he should not be proud who is rich; take away pride, and riches shall do no hurt."*

## *De Quaestione Tertia, videlicet de legitimo usu divitiarum* · Of the Third Question, Namely of the Lawful Use of Riches

### *Quadruplex est legitimus usus divitiarum* · The Lawful Use of Riches Is Fourfold

I. For the furthering of religion and of the glory of God. For seeing we are born to glorify God, so much the more ought we to bestow thereon whatsoever we have. Here is to be reproved the niggardliness of Princes and of the Magistrate toward Churches, Schools, Ministers, Doctors, Professors and Scholars. And all the godly are to be admonished that with their substance they help the studies of learning, both for God's sake and for the profit of the Commonwealth. Religion without learned men is nothing. When pure religion perisheth, all consolation and life perisheth. A Commonwealth without learned men is ***ἀναρχία*** (anarchy, want of government). If faithful Divines, Lawyers that love godliness, and learned Physicians be wanting, what is life, what is discipline, what is the government of the welfare of soul and body? <!-- p.258 --> What need of many words? **Judg. 5**, in the Song of Deborah and Barak, it is read: *"Out of Zebulun came down they that govern with the pen of the writer."*

II. For the helping and adorning of the Commonwealths in which we live; and therefore tributes are to be paid, that they may be the ornaments of peace, the succours of war, and the sinews of the Commonwealth, as hath been said above in the fourth Commandment.

III. That we may provide for ourselves and for our own, without sordidness and without prodigality; that we may take care of our house and household. Let us not be Codruses, nor Euclios,[^P12-7] nor yet prodigals, but let us be Servatiuses, according to the old saying: *A good Servatius maketh a good Bonifacius.*[^P12-8] Likewise: *A good storer, a good server.*

IIII. That our fountains may be dispersed abroad, as **Solomon** saith; that is, that we may be liberal toward the poor, that many may rejoice in our goods, and together with us give thanks unto God, who supplieth all things abundantly.

So **Xenophon** also speaketh of the use of riches: I. That thou mayest be able to sacrifice. II. That thou mayest help the Commonwealth. III. That thou mayest be able to succour thyself. IIII. That thou mayest help thy friends. And **Pindar**, in the 5th Ode of the Pythians: *"Riches joined with virtue are good, and avail for doing good unto friends."*

Here it is asked what liberality and beneficence is acceptable unto God.

I. That which is not of goods ill gotten, but of goods godly and rightly acquired. For **Gregory** saith rightly: *"It is not to be accounted alms, if that be dispensed unto the poor which is gotten out of unlawful things."*

II. That which is done with a cheerful and humble mind. For *"God loveth a cheerful giver."* And it is commonly said: *He giveth twice who giveth quickly.* And Christ saith: *"If thou wilt give alms, do not cause a trumpet to be sounded before thee."*

III. That which is bestowed upon the good poor and the afflicted, not upon the slothful. For it is to be looked to, unto whom it is given. And regard is to be had of the poor; whom whoso neglecteth, despiseth, assaileth <!-- p.259 --> and evil entreateth, is a dog, and not a man. **Prov. 21**: *"Whoso stoppeth his ears at the cry of the poor, he also shall cry himself, but shall not be heard."* **Isaiah**: *"Deal thy bread to the hungry; then shalt thou call, and the Lord shall answer."* **Mat. 10**: *"Whosoever shall give a drink of cold water unto one of the least for the doctrine's sake, he shall not lose his reward."* **Luke 6**: *"Give, and it shall be given unto you."* **Matth.**: *"Come, ye blessed of my Father, inherit the Kingdom prepared for you from the foundation of the world: for I was an hungred, and ye gave me meat; I was thirsty, and ye gave me drink."* **2 Corinth. 9**: *"He which soweth bountifully shall reap also bountifully."* And hence it is sometimes said: *Alms deliver from sin*, that is, they merit a mitigation of the calamities of this present life, according to that saying: *"Be thou a father of the fatherless and of widows, and God shall love thee more than thy mother doth."* Likewise: *"Alms delivereth from death,"* that is, it meriteth that we be not overwhelmed by dangers.

IIII. That which is not an immoderate and courtly lavishing; as **Caligula** lavished 675 tuns of gold in one year. **Antony** at one supper divided a thousand boars. **Heliogabalus** at one supper set forth six hundred heads of ostriches. **Vitellius** at one banquet set on 2000 fishes and 7000 birds. And we, prone unto riot, are niggardly in succouring the needy, as may be seen in Courts chiefly, and in common life. And the common voice, not of drunkards only, but of all prodigals also, is: *Pour in abundantly; care not whence.* And for the most part our affairs are brought to the usual Proverb: *Sparing at the bottom.*[^P12-9]

The Greeks name it ***χάριν*** (favour, grace), from ***χαίρω***, I rejoice; the Germans have rendered it *Gnad* (grace), from the Hebrew *Chen*, which signifieth a benefit: *gnädig*, that is, beneficent, *gutthätig*, *wolthätig* (doing good, well-doing). And the ancients signified by the deities of the Graces that men are born to do good. Therefore they had temples of the Graces in the midst of their cities, that they might signify that there ought to be a perpetual fellowship in mankind and among citizens; and they feigned that the Graces are the daughters of Jupiter and of Eurynome. Now ***εὐρυνομὴ*** signifieth, as it were, *she that distributeth well*. <!-- p.260 --> Her daughters are the three Graces, or Charites:

> Aglaia, brightness,  
> ***Εὐφροσύνη***, gladness,  
> Thalia, delight.  

**Pausanias** painteth them thus: The first beareth a rose; the second a die (*astragalus*); the third a myrtle.

Aglaia is brightness. For benefits are to be given with a bright countenance and willingly, as it is said:

> ***Ὠκεῖαι χάριτες γλυκερώτεραι, ἢν δὲ βραδύνη,***  
> ***Πᾶσα χάρις κενεὴ, μήδε λέγοιτο χάρις.***  
> (Swift favours are the sweeter; but if it tarry,  
> every favour is empty, nor let it be called a favour.)  

**Ausonius**:

> If thou do any good thing, do it quickly; for what is quickly done  
> shall be welcome: tardy favour maketh it unwelcome.  

And the picture of the rose pertaineth to the mind and the countenance. In giving let there be a candid mind and a cheerful countenance. For that which is not given with a candid mind is not welcome, as when an enemy giveth something to pursue or to deceive a man. Therefore it is wont to be said: ***Χαρίτων δέ τε θυμὸς ἄριστος*** (but of favours the heart is the best part). And: ***Τῶν ἐχθρῶν δῶρα, ἄδωρα*** (the gifts of enemies are no gifts). And let there be a cheerful countenance. For he giveth stony bread who giveth not willingly. And **Isocrates** saith: ***Μήτε τὰς χάριτας ἀχαρίστως χαριζόμενος*** (nor bestowing favours ungraciously). Aglaia is so called also from comeliness, or an honest cause. For there must be some honest cause of giving, lest it become a lavishing, of which **Epicharmus** saith: ***Νόσον ἔχεις, χαίρεις διδούς*** (thou hast a disease: thou takest pleasure in giving). And **Solomon**: *"Let thy fountains be dispersed abroad, but remain thou the Lord of them."*

Likewise:

> ***Εἰ τῶν σῶν κτεάνων ἐθέλεις πλέον, ὦ βροτέ, ἐκχεῖν.***  
> ***Καιροῦ ἐν ὀξείᾳ ἶρος ἄϊρος ἔσῃ,***  
> ***Καὶ τάχει εἰς κόρακας σπεύδεις, πενίης τε ἑταῖρος***  
> ***Τολμᾷς τοῦ ξύλου ἄξια τοῦ διδύμου.***  

> If the surfeit of thy belly loseth thee more money  
> than thy hand by its diligence can make,  
> this is the last estate of things: that as a beggar within  
> the walls, through thy native town, thou go, O prodigal brother;  
> <!-- p.261 -->or that, hanging, thou end thy life with a halter, like a jar,[^P12-10]  
> and give the entrails of thy body for food unto the ravens.
