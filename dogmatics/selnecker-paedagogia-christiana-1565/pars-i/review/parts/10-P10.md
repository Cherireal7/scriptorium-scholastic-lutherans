I pass over for the present that it is against civil honesty. What shall I say of the will of God, to which it is as contrary as anything can be? For He willeth that among His people there be no whoremonger.

For this cause also was matrimony instituted of God, that fornication might be avoided. For thus saith Paul: *"To avoid fornication, let every man have his own wife."* And there is extant a most weighty saying of **Ignatius**: ***Τοῖς νεωτέροις ἐπίτρεπε γαμεῖν, πρὶν διαφθαρῶσιν εἰς ἑταίρας*** (Suffer the younger men to marry, before they be corrupted unto harlots). There are extant also examples everywhere, whereby the Scripture showeth the wrath and plagues of GOD dreadfully inflicted, not only upon the whoremongers themselves, but also upon them that consent unto fornication and wink at it.

And the Gospel assuredly requireth of us purity and holiness of life. For it saith: *"Be ye holy; for I am holy."* And **1 Thess. 4**: *"God hath not called us unto uncleanness, but unto holiness."*

What shall the weak in faith, what shall our adversaries, what shall the Jews and other nations say of us Evangelicals, who against the word of God and public honesty do nourish and cherish harlots? Whereas yet the word of God so often presseth it upon us, that we should so live that the name of God and His doctrine be not blasphemed, **Ezek. 36**, **Rom. 2**. Likewise: *"Let your good works shine before men, that by them my heavenly Father may be glorified."*

But the most impure Sadducees, and the Politicians also, object: It is better, and far more advisable, that one house be granted unto lust, than that all the houses of the town be everywhere dishonoured.

To these it must first be answered: *"Think not more highly than ye ought to think; but think soberly."* Likewise: *"Lean not unto thine own <!-- p.202 --> wisdom."* Likewise: *"My counsellors are thy word, O Lord."* According to the word of God, therefore, must judgement be made, as Christ saith: *"Seek ye first the kingdom of God, and his righteousness."* And David: *"Blessed is he that feareth the Lord, and walketh in his ways."* These and the like sayings pertain chiefly unto Princes, Senators and Counsellors.

Next, let the voice of Paul be thought upon: Evil things are not to be done, that good may come.

Likewise: Seeing that in Baptism we have been baptized into Christ, it is not lawful for the Magistrate to suffer the members of Christ to be made the members of an harlot. And Paul biddeth us flee fornication, lest we defile our members, which are the temple of the Holy Ghost. And what differeth he that consenteth from him that is guilty? Paul being judge, nothing. Add that, Paul being witness, no sin is nearer and more hurtful to our body than fornication. For *"every sin that a man doeth is without the body; but he that committeth fornication sinneth against his own body."* And **Jerome** expoundeth the words of Paul, saying: *"Fornication staineth not only the conscience of him that committeth fornication, but the body also; and he is made one body with the harlot, and sinneth against his own body, while he maketh the temple of Christ to be the body of an harlot, etc."* And that, for the sake of explication, somewhat of the evils may be considered, if the Magistrate maintain a brothel: let regard at least be had of the youth, which, being committed to the government and trust of Princes and of the Magistrate, is destroyed both in soul and in body. The destruction of the soul hath been abundantly proved from the things above.

But that the youth is destroyed in body, the experience of many evils doth testify. Hence follow thefts, murders, robberies, infinite kinds of diseases, and the enfeebling of the whole nature of man, such as is the weakening of the whole human body. For fornication hath not from God the blessing which they have whom God hath joined together. Likewise: pustules, swellings, ulcers, stenches, the French disease and the falling sickness, epilepsy, swooning. And who can number all the kinds of diseases, and that <!-- p.203 --> wrath and curse of God, which accompany fornication and brothels?

Add lastly, that they who have first exhausted their youthful body with fornication become fit neither for the administration of the Church, nor for the Household, nor for the government of the Commonwealth; to say nothing meanwhile of this, that they who in youth have been accustomed to fornication and wandering lusts do but ill keep the faith that is to be rendered unto their wives, and become before God and men faithless and forsworn. Who then shall safely commit any public office to such? They who keep not faith with God and with their own flesh, how shall they be able to be faithful unto others?

It is no marvel, therefore, if GOD, the chaste and just judge, do oftentimes take away all blessing from Princes, from the Magistrate, and from their government, and oftentimes strip them of kingdoms and of substance by wars and other occasions, because they maintain brothels, and wink at fornications and adulteries.

Now the remedies against fornication, which the Magistrate ought to use, especially in the life of the court, are found to be for the most part these:

I. If the Magistrate diligently and carefully accustom the youth to the word of God and to the use of the Sacraments.

II. If he take care that the youth be instructed in good disciplines and manners, and that this business be committed to certain Schoolmasters hired for the purpose, to the masters of the court, and to other honest men.

III. If he take care that the youth live more sparingly, and suffer it not to be drunk with wine, wherein is excess.

IIII. If he take care that the youth have its place of exercise, and honest exercises, whereby it may also make its bodies nimble and fit for warfare.

V. If he take care that they who are now somewhat advanced in age, seeing they cannot contain, take wives unto themselves.

VI. If he punish adulterers and whoremongers severely, that the youth, made more wary either by its own peril or by the example of others, may abstain from these vices.

<!-- p.204 -->

If Princes and Magistrates do not this, they nourish a Sodom, not a court, not a Commonwealth that is Christian and worthy of themselves, and they make themselves liable to the just judgement of God, according to that of **Ezekiel**: *"Behold, this was the iniquity of Sodom, pride"* — that is, neglect of the word and of the ministers thereof — *"fulness of bread"* — that is, revellings and drunkenness — and thirdly, *"idleness"*; and fourthly, *"she did not stretch out her hand to the poor and needy"* — that is, the Magistrate did not relieve the subjects of their burden. Now as Sodom, so thou also, saith Ezekiel, shalt bear thy shame and thy confusion, and shalt fare no better therein. And Peter saith: *"God, turning the cities of Sodom and Gomorrha into ashes, condemned them with an overthrow, making them an ensample unto those that after should live ungodly."* And Christ threateneth in some sort more hardly them that now have admonishers and hear not, when He saith: *"It shall be more tolerable for the land of Sodom and Gomorrha in the day of judgment, than for that city, etc."* Let them therefore beware and fear the wrath of God, who give out that they are minded to set up dens of this sort anew, and to endow them with certain revenues: men unworthy of the name of Princes and of the Magistrate, nay rather worthy of the foulest dens and of the gehenna of hell. And let these things at length have been said of brothels, for the admonishing of them that suffer themselves to be admonished.

Afterward, let the Magistrate himself shun and restrain incests, and not follow the example of the counsellors of Cambyses, when he was minded to marry his own sister, who said: In no law indeed do we find it lawful that any man should take his own sister to wife; but we find it granted unto the Persian king, that he may do what pleaseth him. Now we know that for incest the nations were first driven out and compelled to give place to the Jewish people; and we know likewise that God threateneth all others also, as He saith: *"Whosoever shall commit this abomination, his soul shall be rooted out from among the people."* The examples are many: Lot, **Genesis 19**; Reuben, **Gen. 35**; Absalom, **2 Kings 16 [2 Sam. 16]**; Oedipus, the Ptolemies, Seleucus, who yieldeth his wife Stratonice to his son Antiochus, etc.

Some years ago there was at Erfurt a certain honest matron, who by an unwary and enormous fall had to do with her own son, and conceived of him a daughter, whom the son afterward, not knowing her to be both his sister and his daughter, took to wife. And of Lucretia, the daughter of Pope **Alexander the Sixth**, it is known that she was the Pope's daughter, harlot, and daughter-in-law. Hence there is extant an Epigram upon the tomb of Lucretia:

> In this tomb sleepeth one in name Lucretia, but in deed  
> A Thais: Alexander's daughter, bride, and daughter-in-law.  

Julia, stepmother of the Emperor Bassianus Caracalla, was married to her stepson Bassianus, and that upon her own inviting of him. For when, as though by negligence, she had bared her bosom before him, and he said, "I would, if it were lawful," she answered, "If it pleaseth thee, it is lawful. Knowest thou not that thou art Emperor, and givest Laws, not receivest them?" Publicly therefore he married her, and held her in the place of a wife. Wherefore afterward the men of Alexandria called her Jocasta, and him Oedipus; and for this jest Bassianus slew many thousands of citizens in the city of Alexandria. And there are extant everywhere in the histories more dreadful examples.

And thus much only have we wished to say of the dignities of marriage, and of other things which offered themselves to be considered by the way.

But as touching the tree — that we may add this also for the sake of students — which was devised to this end, that persons might be mutually distinguished one from another, and that it might be shown, as in a certain series and table, how they either touch one another or differ one from another: it is to be known that the tree is, after the customary manner, set down as twofold; the civil, which regardeth successions, inheritances and guardianships, and the Canonical, which is referred to marriages. This again is either of consanguinity or of affinity. Now in either tree there are principally considered the line and the degree. They call a line (*linea*) a gathering of persons drawn out from one and the same stock (*stipes*), either upward, or downward, or to the sides. Hence one line is of ascendants, namely when from that person from whom the reckoning is begun we ascend from the son to the father, from the father to the grandfather, from the grandfather to the great-grandfather, the great-great-grandfather, the fourth and the fifth grandfather, and the forefathers; another is of descendants, from the father to the son, to the grandson and the great-grandson, the great-great-grandson, the fourth and the fifth grandson, and the posterity; another is of collaterals, either brothers or sisters; cousins on the father's side (*patrueles*), who are the sons of two brothers; cousins-german (*consobrini*), who are the children of brothers or of sisters, *Geschwister kinder*, brothers' and sisters' children; likewise of second cousins (*sobrini*), and of those sprung from these, *ander Geschwister kinder*, brothers' and sisters' children in the second remove. And hither pertaineth the *patruus*, the father's brother; the *amita*, the father's sister; the *patruus magnus*, the grandfather's brother; the *amita magna*, the grandfather's sister; the *avunculus*, the mother's brother; the *matertera*, the mother's sister; the *avunculus magnus*, the *matertera magna*.

<!-- p.209 -->

Now a degree (*gradus*) is called the relation of persons standing apart, whereby it is known by how great a distance of agnation or of cognation two persons differ one from another. And that consanguinity is named agnation (*agnatio*) which ariseth from the male line, by reason of the father, *Schwerdtmahl*;[^P10-2] cognation (*cognatio*), that from the female line, by reason of the mother, *Spielmahl*. It is to be known, moreover, that degrees are reckoned one way in the Civil Law, another way in that which they name the Canon Law. For in the former there are so many degrees as there are persons, the stock being taken away. But in the Canon Law, although the same reckoning is kept in ascendants and descendants, yet in collaterals two persons are taken for one, and they stand apart from one another by that degree by which they stand apart from the stock: as two brothers stand apart from the stock by one degree; therefore they are said to stand apart from one another also by one degree. From these things it is seen that in the Civil tree which containeth the line of collaterals no first degree is constituted, as brother and sister, the nearest collaterals, are by the Civil Law in the second degree; but by the Canon Law they are in the first degree, seeing that they assign a beginning unto the transverse line. The reason of this diversity is that, as was said a little before, the Civil Law considereth successions, which, since they proceed from one person to another, do always in collaterals constitute a new degree; but that other Law considereth the nature of matrimony, in which, as two persons are required, so the same two together constitute one degree. This also is to be held, that the line of collaterals is either equal or unequal. That is equal in which the degrees stand equally distant from the common stock, as brother and sister, cousins on the father's side and cousins-german. But this is unequal, when one person goeth before the other by a degree, as I and my brother's son. Let these Rules therefore be observed:

I. According to the Civil Law, in either line of collaterals there are so many degrees as there are persons, the stock being taken away.

II. According to the Canon Law, by what degree collateral persons in the equal line stand apart from the common stock, by the same degree they stand apart from one another; but by what degree in the unequal line the more remote standeth apart from the common stock, by the same degree they stand apart from one another also.

<!-- p.210 -->

I will add nothing further here. For examples are everywhere at hand. And as touching affinity, let these Rules only be observed: I. In affinity the degrees of consanguinity are retained. For in what degree any one is joined to the husband by reason of consanguinity, in the same degree he is joined to the wife by reason of affinity. II. Affinity hindereth not matrimony between the kinsfolk of the married parties: as a father may marry a woman whose daughter his son hath to wife; and two, three or four brothers may contract with two, three or four sisters. And this is well-nigh the whole of what can most briefly be said of the tree of consanguinity and affinity.

## *De Secundo* · Of the Second Point

### *Quomodo coniugium pie et honeste inchoandum sit* · How Marriage Is to Be Begun Godly and Honestly

It is required:

I. That it be begun in true faith.

II. That prayer be made unto God for a godly spouse.

III. That the consent of the parents be added.

First, it is necessary that faith be present, whereby the word and commandment of God is considered, **Gen. 2**: *"The Lord said"* — that is, He ordained, decreed, and made this Decree manifest, and willeth it to be ratified and firm — *"It is not good"* for human nature, both for the kind and for the several individuals, *"to be alone; but let us make him an help."* He sent therefore a deep sleep and slumber upon Adam, and took one of his ribs,[^P10-3] of which He builded Eve the woman; even as in the redemption of mankind, out of the body of the other Adam, to wit Christ, falling asleep upon the cross and in death, the Church was taken, which was redeemed by His death. And God brought Eve unto Adam, who, straightway knowing Eve at the first sight, by a wonderful wisdom and by divine counsel said: *"This is now bone of my bones"*; that is, Now have I found an help like unto myself. Adam asked not whence the woman was; even as neither shall we in the eternal life, when we shall have come together again, inquire of our origin and kindred, but by a certain wonderful wisdom we shall know what bones have <!-- p.211 --> been, and are, of our bones, what the line is, what the kindred of the seed, etc. *"Therefore"* (saith God) *"shall a man leave his father and his mother"* — not by a certain ***ἀστοργία*** (want of natural affection), but in dwelling together, and in conversation, and in fellowship of life — *"and they two shall be one flesh,"* both by the joining together and in the manner of generation. Unto these words therefore let faith have regard, which may assuredly hold that conjugal conversation pleaseth God, and is the work and ordinance of God, and that all men are bound by these words to enter into marriage, if otherwise they cannot live chastely. And he that in this faith seeketh a spouse findeth truly a good thing, and draweth blessing and favour from the Lord, **Prov. 18**. And although difficulties do sometimes befall, yet he considereth the will of God, and taketh in good part the ***γλυκυπικρον*** (bitter-sweet thing) given him of God — given even in sleep, as for Adam sleeping He builded Eve. For oftentimes those are joined together by a certain divine providence, of whom they that were waking had no hope, as is truly said in the verses:

> Why do I linger? It is not of human help; a hidden thing is  
> The ordering of wedlock: by destiny are marriages made.  

And in **Prov. 19** it is said: *"House, riches and a good inheritance do children obtain; but a godly and prudent wife the Lord bestoweth."* But that in many marriages no success, or very little, can be perceived, cometh truly from this, that faith hath no regard unto the words of the institution and of the will of God, and that many, in a certain blindness of lusts, do but rush to the contracting of matrimony with her who chanceth to be offered. Hence it cometh:

> If any man loveth a frog, he thinketh the frog to be Diana.  

Likewise:

> ***Τῷ γὰρ ἔρωτι τὰ μὴ καλὰ, καλὰ πέφανται.*** (For unto love the things that are not fair appear fair.)  

Hence arise strifes, poverty, faithlessness, and many other evils; which he that beginneth marriage in faith is able patiently to bear, if any such do sometimes befall, and to ask of God a mitigation, and to turn bitter things for himself into a certain sweetness.

A certain one of the Rabbins hath played elegantly, saying that two <!-- p.212 --> words among the Hebrews, *Iisch* and *Ischoh*, are the words of marriage, and contain the name of the author of marriage, *Iah*; but that when *Iah* is not present in marriage, and there is taken away from *Iisch* the middle letter Yod, and from *Ischoh* the last letter He — which together make up the divine Name — then there remaineth *Isch* without *Iah*, that is, fire, and God is absent, and all blessing.[^P10-4]

Next, there ought to be added invocation and prayer unto God, who for Adam and for all the godly doth join and build their spouses. Neither let this matter be esteemed light. And let the parents themselves also be careful, and call upon God that He would give unto their children a godly, concordant and quiet marriage; as Abraham, **Genesis 24**, is careful, and calleth upon God when about to seek for his son Isaac his wife Rebecca: *"God,"* saith he, *"the Lord of heaven, shall send his angel, that thou mayest take a wife unto my son in Mesopotamia, out of the city of Nahor."*

Afterward, let marriage be contracted with the will and consent of godly parents added thereto, and let there be no clandestine contracts. And let us know that there is on earth no power and obedience greater than the father's power. For as in Paradise God Himself brought Eve unto Adam, so He willeth that thenceforward betrothals be made with the consent and will of the parents, as it is said: *"Honour thy father"*; **1 Cor. 7**: *"He that giveth her in marriage doeth well."* And Hermione in **Euripides**, when Orestes asketh her to wife, answereth:

> ***Νυμφευμάτων μὲν τῶν ἐμῶν πατὴρ ἐμὸς***  
> ***Μέριμναν ἕξει, κ' οὐκ ἐμὸν σκοπεῖν τάδε.***  
> (Of my espousals my father shall have the care, and it is not mine to look to these things.)  

And Samson, when about to ask a maiden in a city of the Philistines, goeth unto his parents, and asketh that she be given him to wife. Thus the text in the *Institutes*, title 10, saith plainly and rightly: *"If they be sons under power, let them have the consent of the parents in whose power they are. For that this ought to be done both Civil and natural reason persuadeth, insomuch that the bidding of the parents ought to go before."* Now although this judgement, other things being equal, is true, and hath testimonies as well of divine Scripture as of the public Laws, yet many things are wont to be <!-- p.213 --> objected which seem to fight against it. These it pleaseth me to touch in few words, that the matter itself may be the more clearly set forth.

**Objections.** **Genesis 2**: *"A man shall leave his father and his mother, and shall cleave unto his wife."* And on the other side: The wife shall leave her parents, and shall cleave unto her husband.

**I answer:** Rightly, if indeed she have been made a lawful wife, or he have been made a lawful husband. But not when she is not yet the lawful wife of any man that asketh or wooeth her.

**Likewise,** **Matth. 19**: *"What God hath joined together, let not man put asunder."*

**I answer:** A distinction is to be made between joinings. For one is divine and lawful; another is rash, unlawful, and even Satanic. Those therefore whom God joineth, that is, who are joined together by divine ordinance, according to the Commandment of God and the public Laws, these human rashness ought not to put asunder. But those who are joined together by rashness, by drunkenness, by impostures, by enticements of young folk and of old women — these, as God hath not joined them, so a man endued with divine authority, as the Parent and the Magistrate, may put asunder.

**Likewise:** The consent of the parents is required of honesty (*de honestate*), not of necessity (*de necessitate*).

**I answer:** They that say this manifestly betray their own unskilfulness. For, if they speak in earnest, they understand not the true nature of honesty, neither do they understand their own Laws, which they profess, concerning the consent of parents in the marriage of children. For there is one honesty defined by natural Laws; there is another honesty defined by positive or ceremonial Laws. And this latter honesty indeed is not of itself of necessity. But that other, which is defined by the Law of nature, is assuredly of all things most necessary. And in this order whatsoever is of honesty, the same is of necessity. But if into this order there be admitted that distinction of the Canonists concerning honesty and necessity, what will be more absurd? and what can be said more ungodly? So to worship God will be only of honesty, not of necessity. So not to kill, not to steal, not to commit adultery, will be only of honesty, not of necessity. To the same order pertaineth that also: <!-- p.214 --> *"Honour thy parents."* And that this honour is to be understood also of the seeking of the parents' consent in contracting matrimony, many sayings in the books of Moses concerning the marriage of children do signify not obscurely. But the words also of the public Laws say plainly: *"Let them have the consent of the parents. For that this ought to be done both Civil and natural reason do persuade."* If the Law had said only, "Civil reason persuadeth," it might be thought to speak only of a civil, political and ceremonial honesty; such as is this, that at feasts the worthier sitteth in the worthier place and first taketh meat. Of such things it is rightly said that they are of honesty, not of necessity. But the Law addeth: "Natural reason persuadeth." It signifieth therefore that this is a Law of nature, that the consent of the parents go before. Wherefore they who against this Law undertake to enter into marriage do not simply sin against a political Law, but also against the Law of nature, in which whatsoever is of honesty is also of necessity. Since therefore the Canonists are the authors of this distinction in this order of things, they signify not obscurely how dishonestly they think of true honesty.

**Likewise:** Consent maketh matrimony.

**I answer:** I grant that matrimony is made by consent, but not by that alone. And it is made by a consent not unlawful, not rash, but lawful and of sound mind. Otherwise, if consent without respect of divine and political Laws made matrimony, what if brother and sister should consent, and promise marriage one to another? What if a parent and a daughter? Here assuredly the consent and this espousal would by no means be valid, because they fight with the Law of nature. But the consent of a maid with a young man, without the authority and will of the parents, fighteth also in its own manner with the Law of nature, as was shown a little before. The Lawyers are wont to distinguish between a perfect and an imperfect contract, and they hold the former for valid, and judge the latter void. Why should not a distinction be made in like manner between perfect and imperfect consent, so that that be called perfect consent to which is added the will of the parents and the public confirmation <!-- p.215 --> of the Church; but that the imperfect, which is the beginning, not the finishing, of the work, seeing it attaineth not its lawful end, shall be accounted as none?

**Likewise:** That which hath once pleased can no more displease. Therefore parents ought not to rescind the clandestine espousals of their children.

**I answer:** That which hath once pleased — to wit, lawfully and rightly; not unlawfully, not perversely. For what a confusion of things would this be? Where were the place for repentance? Adultery once pleased David; therefore it ought no more to displease him.

But let these things suffice concerning this Question. Parents, moreover, on their part ought not to be froward toward their children, nor to deliver them unto others against their will, nor to shake off honest suitors; as is to be seen in the history of Isaac and Rebecca, **Genesis 24**. But let the causes of refusal be weighty and honest, not trifling, not doubtful and uncertain. Now also it must be touched how one ought to live in marriage; although from the things above, especially those which have been said of the image of the love of Christ, it is manifest what is to be held concerning the conjugal life.

First let it be considered what are the duties of the husband; next, what are the duties of the wife; afterward, what are the duties of the married pair jointly. The duties of the husband are two: to labour, and to love his wife. Of the former duty it is said, **Genesis 3**: *"In the sweat of thy face shalt thou eat thy bread, till thou return unto the ground, out of which thou wast taken: for dust thou art, and unto dust shalt thou return."* This is a most severe commandment of God, that all husbands should flee idleness as a most hurtful plague; that they should not consume their substance in taverns, nor wander hither and thither begging, but should diligently, faithfully and constantly do the labours agreeable to their calling, and by honest labours procure those things which are necessary in all of life, and help the common fellowship of men, and by the bounty of God nourish themselves and theirs; as it is commanded in the first Epistle to the **Thessalonians, the fourth**: *"Be ye ambitious in this, that ye do your own business, and work with your own hands."* For God hath promised help in the <!-- p.216 --> discharging of the duties of our callings, and showeth that our labours shall not be vain, but wholesome unto us, unto ours, and unto others, as it is said, **1 Cor. 15**: *"Your labour shall not be in vain in the Lord."* And so labour, which is laid upon Adam as the punishment of sin, in the godly not only ceaseth to be a punishment, but is even made a peculiar worship of God, as it is said, **Ps. 128**: *"Thou shalt eat the labour of thine hands: HAPPY SHALT THOU BE, AND IT SHALL BE WELL WITH THEE"*; that is, thou hast God favourable through Christ, and this God willeth to bless thy labours, that thou mayest have food and raiment ***καὶ τὰ ἱκανὰ*** (and the things that suffice), if not unto splendour and magnificence, yet unto the sustaining of thy life and of thine, with a good conscience; like that cobbler of Alexandria to whom Antony is sent away by divine direction. The history is in the *Lives of the Fathers*, and is recited in the repetition of the Augsburg Confession, under the title, Of the Monastic Life. Thus do all the Godly labour and have success: Preachers, Readers, Princes, Nobles, Citizens, Workmen, Tailors, Cobblers, and others. For man is born unto labour, as the bird to fly.

Of the latter duty of the husband Paul speaketh, **Ephes. 5**: Let him love his wife as his own body, and as the companion of his life and his help. Now it is manifest that the body is not cast away, even though it be swarthy, and ill affected, and ***νοσερὸν*** (sickly), but is rather cared for. So also ought the wife to be handled gently and patiently, even though another woman be fairer than thy wife, more eloquent, more prudent, more sound. And here let the husband imitate the example of the vine-dresser, as **Ps. 128** compareth the wife to a vine: which when it must be bound up — seeing it is of itself slender and thin, as the woman is a thin and slender vessel — that it may be fruitful, the vine-dresser useth not chains of iron, nor a net, nor a snare, but a thin straw. So let the husband also be toward his wife, not a Lion, not a Tyrant, not a Euclio,[^P10-5] but let him handle her with reason and prudence, as the weaker vessel, and as a partaker with him of grace and of life; lest, if she be more hardly used, she become fearful, and know not what is to be done, and so prayers unto God also be hindered. And this is that which Paul saith: *"Love your <!-- p.217 --> wives, even as Christ loved his Church."*

The duties of the wife also are two. The former is comprehended in these words, **Genesis 3**: *"I will make thee many sorrows when thou shalt be with child, and in sorrow thou shalt bring forth thy children."* These are all the miseries and sorrows in the bearing of the fruit of the womb, in childbirth, and in the whole conjugal conversation: sicknesses, the ***κίττα*** which they name *pica* (the longing of women with child), loathing before the birth, and torments and pains and perils of life in the birth; which calamities are assuredly so many and so great, that he whom they move not must needs be of an adamantine mind. Rachel, the wife of the Patriarch Jacob, dieth in childbirth, **Gen. 35**, and many are the unhappy births, which ought to put each one of us in mind with how great peril we ourselves were brought forth into the light, and that God is present at generation, giveth life and nourisheth, as it is written: *"In him we live, and move, and have our being."* Let mothers of households therefore learn to bear this cross patiently, and let them be at once valiant and patient in childbirth; and let them consider first of all that this cross, laid upon them as a punishment, is turned into glory and blessing, as it is said, **1 Tim. 2**: *"The female sex is saved through the bearing of children, if it continue in faith, charity, chastity and modesty."* This is a great word and consolation, which all godly mothers of households (for it is not spoken of the Turks and unbelievers) ought to fasten in their inmost breast.

The latter duty of the wife is comprehended in these words: *"And thy husband shall rule over thee."* And Paul: *"The man is the head of the woman."* Before the fall there was no dominion over the woman, but after the fall it first began. Let wives therefore be subject to their own husbands, **Ephes. 5**, **Col. 3**, and **1 Cor. 11**, and let them think that they are pliant vines, which have no need of hard chains, of stripes, and of over-many chidings. Thus **Iamblichus** saith: *"It is fitting[^P10-6] that the male bear rule and the woman obey. But the kind of rule shall be, not such as is that of masters, where the profit of the superior is cared for; nor such as is that of the arts, where there is care for the inferior only; but like unto the civil, in which the common profit is furthered equally."* And **Euripides**:

<!-- p.218 -->

> ***Ηπερ μεγίστη γίνεται σωτηρία,***  
> ***Οταν γυνὴ πρὸς ἄνδρα μὴ διχοστατῇ.***  
> (Which is the greatest safety, when the wife is not at variance with her husband.)  

And there is a notable saying of **Epicharmus**: ***σώφρονος γυναικὸς ἀρετὰ, τὸν συνόντα μὴ ἀδικεῖν ἄνδρα***: It is the virtue of a good woman not to offend the husband with whom she liveth. And thus **Theano**, a student of the doctrines of Pythagoras, answered one who asked her how she should become renowned:

> ***Ιστὸν ἐποιχομένη, καὶ ἐμὸν λέχος ἀντιόωσα.***  

Weaving my web, and caring for my husband, or for the whole of marriage. Hence also Venus is painted standing upon a tortoise.

Furthermore, the duties of the husband and of the wife are that they bring up their children, granted and given them of God, in the fear of God, **Deut. 6** and **11**; that they indulge not their wickedness, as did Eli. In **Gen. 18** God speaketh of Abraham, and commendeth him because he rightly instructeth his children and his household. **Ephes. 6.** But of this duty of parents somewhat hath been said above, in the fourth Commandment.

## *De Tertio, videlicet de Divortio* · Of the Third Point, Namely of Divorce

Lightness among the Jewish people, and among the Gentiles, hath moved this Question. For the Jews, whether the causes were weighty, or light, or none, did often admit divorces. And Moses, seeing some of the Jews moved by covetousness, others by domestic vexation, others by lust, to visit their wives with wrong, and oftentimes with death, that it might be lawful for them to marry new ones — that he might provide for domestic quiet and turn away a greater evil — gave them liberty of making divorce, **Deut. 24**. Hence the bill of divorcement, or of cutting off, as they call it, which the Jews name *Gath*,[^P10-7] wherein the husband resigneth all the right which he had over his wife. When the Jews gave this, they used divers ceremonies, and in the writing they enclosed the bill itself in twelve lines, according to the numerical value of the letters Teth, which is nine, and Gimel, which is three: a value <!-- p.219 --> betokening the twelve Patriarchs and the tribes of the people of Israel. But if the bill was shorter than these letters, it had no more any authority. Now the form of the bill was written after this manner:

> On the fourth day of the week, on the twentieth of the month Sivan, in the year five thousand, etc., from the creation of the world, here and in this city, situate by these rivers, I Jacob, so surnamed, the son of the Priest, who abide this day in this city, have deliberated in the thought of my soul, no man compelling me, and have dismissed, and set free, and cast out, and put away thee unto thyself, thee my wife N., so surnamed, the daughter of Rabbi such a one, who hitherto hast been my wife; and I have granted thee licence and full power, that thou be thine own mistress, and lady of thine own soul, and go at thy pleasure whither thou wilt, that thou mayest be married to any man soever whom thou wilt; neither let any man forbid thee in thine hand, from this day and for ever. And behold, now art thou permitted unto every man. In witness whereof let this be unto thee a bill of divorcement, a letter of dismissal, and an instrument of liberty, and a seal of divorce, according to the constitution of Moses and Israel.

This lightness was among the Gentiles also. Pericles delivereth his former wife to another, and marrieth Aspasia, who was the one firebrand of the Peloponnesian war. Cato yieldeth his wife Martia to Hortensius at his asking, and after the death of Hortensius joineth her again unto himself. **Cicero** putteth away Terentia. Drusus[^P10-9] yieldeth his wife Livia, being now with child, to the Emperor Augustus at his asking; who, when she had been three months with Augustus, bare Drusus. Hence arose the jest: ***τοῖς εὐτυχοῦσι καὶ τρίμηνα παιδία*** (The fortunate have children even in three months).

This lightness among the Jewish people was increased by Polygamies, whereof the author, after the flood, was Lamech,[^P10-10] and which God tolerated for a season, until the Son of God should draw us back to the first institution, **Matth. 19**; who alloweth no divorce, save in the case of fornication, where it is lawful for the innocent person, after the putting away, to marry again: which would easily <!-- p.220 --> come to pass, if according to the Law of God, **Deut. 22**, the adulterer or the adulteress were put to death. For that at this day in many places both remain unmarried is a divorce in name rather than in deed, and wrong is done to the innocent person; as Paul also expressly pronounceth, **1 Cor. 7**: *"A brother or a sister is not under bondage in such cases."*

In the case therefore of fornication and adultery, divorce may be made, when the matter hath been lawfully adjudged and the guilty person is convicted. The remaining cases pertain to the cognizance of the Magistrate: as, if one of the married pair lay snares for the life of the other, the guilty is to be visited with capital punishment, etc. And let these things concerning marriage have been rehearsed by us thus far. Let us pray the eternal God, the Father of our Lord Jesus Christ, that He would preserve the Laws of chastity among mankind, and would graciously govern us, our youth, and our households by His living, chaste and true Spirit, and bend us unto chastity.

And let there move us unto chastity, first, the severe commandment of God, **1 Thess. 4**: *"This is the will of God, even your chastity."* Next, that we may acknowledge God to be a chaste mind, and may so call upon Him as a truly chaste God, discerning Him from the foul and unclean Devils. And I could believe that the prayers of stammering children are therefore most acceptable unto God, because they are chaste, without the flames of lusts which they that are grown do feel. And here there cometh into my mind a most sweet history, which **Doctor Vitus Winsheim** the Physician recited at the funeral of **D. Philip**: When at Torgau, by command of the Princes, there had been appointed in the house of the Pastor a most difficult deliberation among the chief Doctors of our Churches, the adversaries raging and devising this and that, and when there had been long disputation of divers matters, Philip riseth, wearied and sorrowful, being called forth by a certain messenger; and having dismissed him, in returning he entereth a chamber, wherein were together some poor women, the wives of the Pastor and of two Deacons, and their little children, of whom some were sucking the breast, and others, reciting their prayers, were being heard by their mothers. Philip standeth by, and heareth with <!-- p.221 --> wonder and delight, mindful of the saying: *"Out of the mouth of babes and sucklings thou hast perfected praise"*; and — which moved him most of all — he seeth one of the Deacons' wives giving suck to her little son, and at the same time cutting up a parsnip for her husband's dinner, and hearkening to another little one reciting the accustomed prayers and calling upon God. He crieth out: "O three holy labours, and acceptable unto God!" Strengthened in mind by this sight, glad and cheerful, he returneth into the Council with a merry countenance. **Luther** receiveth him, and saith: "What hath befallen thee, Philip, that thou who a little before wentest out hence so sad, now returnest unto us so merry?" He answereth: "Let us not be faint of heart; for I have now seen those who shall fight for us, who shall defend us, and who against force shall all be, and shall remain, unconquered." Luther asketh who these so valiant captains and soldiers may be. "The wives of our Pastors and Deacons," saith Philip, "and their little children, whose prayers I have now heard; which God will not hear with deaf ears, neither hath the most gracious Father of our Lord Jesus Christ hitherto neglected them, nor, as we hope, will He hereafter neglect them." This speech was received as a happy and prosperous omen, and being now more strengthened in their minds they determine that which became godly and constant teachers of the divine word, etc.
