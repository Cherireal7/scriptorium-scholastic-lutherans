# Translator's Note

## 1. The text

This volume renders into English the first part of Nikolaus Selnecker's *Paedagogia Christiana*, the "Christian Pedagogy", in the Frankfurt edition of 1577: *Paedagogiae Christianae, denuo et ultimo auctae, … Pars Prima*. The title calls this edition "newly and finally enlarged, so that it shall need no further revision", and it is therefore taken here as Selnecker's last word on the book.

The text-base is the copy of the Bayerische Staatsbibliothek, Munich, in the library's digital facsimile bsb10175496. The translation was made from the page images of that copy, page by page, and every page was afterwards read a second time against the images by a reader who had not translated it; the library's machine-read text served as an aid only. The printer's abbreviations are expanded silently.

## 2. How the book came to be

Selnecker tells the story in his preface. He began the work in 1561 as court preacher to Augustus, Elector of Saxony, when it fell to him to set the Christian catechism before the court in sermons, and he wrote down certain heads in which his hearers were to be instructed. The book grew out of those sermons. It is dedicated to Georg Friedrich, Margrave of Brandenburg, the Elector's nephew, and the dedication is dated at Leipzig in August 1576. A commendatory poem by Georg Fabricius follows it.

The book is meant, as its title says, "both for them that teach and for them that learn". It is not a schoolbook of short questions like the third part of the *Institutio*, nor a book of *loci* like its first two parts, but a running instruction in the form of sermons and discussions, with many questions, objections and answers, and with a great store of examples from Scripture, the Fathers, the histories of the ancients and the life of Selnecker's own time.

## 3. What the volume contains

- **The preface** to Margrave Georg Friedrich, and Fabricius's poem.
- **The opening of the First Part**: a sermon on the Word of God; how the certainty of the Christian doctrine may be known; what catechesis is and what its parts are; the distinction of Law and Gospel; the divisions of the divine Law; and the use of the moral Law.
- **The Ten Commandments**, each set out with the worships it requires, the sins against it, and its promises and threatenings. Within them stand long treatises of their own: on the kinds of the Sabbath and on human traditions in the Church (Third Commandment); on parents, magistrates, judges, masters and servants (Fourth); on anger and vengeance (Fifth); on marriage, the forbidden degrees and divorce (Sixth); on theft, usury, restitution and the right use of riches (Seventh); on truth, flattery and calumny (Eighth); on concupiscence (Ninth and Tenth).
- **Free will**, in six points, closing with the works of unbelievers and the question whether we can satisfy the Law of God.
- **An appendix** comparing the discipline of the heathen with Christian godliness: the uses of ethical doctrine, the use of philosophy, and the end of man.
- **The indexes** of the print: passages of Scripture, sentences of the Fathers, and things and words.

## 4. A note on Scripture quotations

Scripture quotations are conformed to the King James Version (1611), which is in the public domain, wherever Selnecker's Latin agrees with it in sense. He often cites from memory, joins two places together, or follows the Latin Bible of his day; where his Latin plainly says something other than the King James Version, the English follows his Latin, and nowhere is another English version used. His references are kept as he prints them, including those that are slips. He names the books of Samuel and Kings as the Latin Bible does (four books "of Kings"); where his name or his number of a book or Psalm differs from the King James Version, the latter follows in square brackets.

## 5. Editorial conventions

**Language.** This edition prints the English only. Greek words and phrases, which Selnecker sets into his Latin, are kept in Greek as the print has them, with the English beside them; German words and sayings are kept in German with the English after them; Hebrew words, which the print mostly gives in Latin letters, are kept as printed.

**Headings.** The headings are those of the print, given in Latin and in English. Where the print announces a series of points ("On the First", "On the Second") the subject has sometimes been added after the English from Selnecker's own list.

**Marginal notes.** The Munich copy carries many notes, underlinings and numbers in the hand of an early reader, in red and brown ink. They are not part of the book and are not translated. The printed book itself has almost no marginal notes; the few there are have been kept.

**Misprints.** Where the print is plainly wrong and the right reading is not in doubt, the right reading is translated and the printed one is given in a note. A reading that could not be made out with certainty is marked [?].

**Endnotes.** The notes stand together at the end of the volume. They record readings, explain a number of terms and allusions, and identify a source only where the identification is certain.

**Dates in the text.** The 1577 print still speaks in places of "this year 1559", from the time the sermons were first written down. Such dates are kept as printed.

**A passage on the Jews.** In the section on usury Selnecker argues that the Jews are not to be suffered by the magistrate and repeats the accusation of ritual murder. The passage is translated in full as he wrote it, and a note states that the accusation is a baseless calumny.

**Ornament.** The aldus leaf — ❦ — marks the divisions between the major parts, as in the other volumes of this series.
