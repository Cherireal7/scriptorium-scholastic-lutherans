# Translator's Note

## 1. The text-base

This working English translation follows the **first and only edition** of Valentin Veltheim's *Institutes of Metaphysics*, printed at **Jena, at the expense of the Author, from the Press of the Werthers, in the year of the World Redeemed M.DC.LXXX (1680)**, under the full title ***Institutiones Metaphysicae, Continuis Exemplis Inprimis Theologicis, Iuridicis, Moralibus, Politicis Illustratae, ut omnium Facultatum ac disciplinarum Studiosis eorumque usibus apprime serviant*** — *"Institutes of Metaphysics, illustrated with continuous examples drawn chiefly from theology, jurisprudence, morals, and politics, so that they may suitably serve students of all faculties and disciplines and their uses."*

The source copy used here is **Universitätsbibliothek Kiel, shelfmark K 2791-1 and K 2791-2** (persistent identifier PPN72572448X), digitised by schriftgut.sh. Its plaintext OCR endpoint — some 2.6 MB of UTF-8 Latin, in 67,379 lines — has been pulled as our working extract and reconstructed silently against the OCR errors characteristic of a late-17th-century octavo in Dutch-Fraktur-adjacent Latin fount: long-*s* → *f*, intermittent digit substitutions in all-caps display (e.g. *THEOLOGIC1S* for *THEOLOGICIS*, *polit1cis* for *politicis*), dropped diacritics in the title-page cartouche, split chapter headings at page boundaries (e.g. *CAPUT NON UM* for *CAPUT NONUM*), and running headers interleaved with body text where the OCR column-recognition broke across page breaks.

## 2. What this book actually is

Veltheim's *Institutiones Metaphysicae* is the **inaugural textbook of his Jena chair**. Valentin Veltheim (1645–1700) — *Hallensis Saxo*, Hallensian of Saxony — was elected Public Ordinary Professor of Logic and First Philosophy at Jena in 1679, one year before this volume went to press; it is the first fruit of that chair and the official metaphysical textbook he laid before his students and the four Jena faculties. He trained under the long shadow of two revered Jena predecessors, **Daniel Stahl** (†1654) and **Johann Andreas Scherzer von Slevogt** (†1659) — both prolific textbook metaphysicians of the generation that had codified the orthodox Lutheran *Schulmetaphysik* after the Thirty Years' War — and under the Jena dean of theology **Friedemann Bechmann** (†1703), whose moral and dogmatic *Institutiones* were the shelf-companions to the present volume. The *Institutiones Metaphysicae* is Veltheim's considered attempt to fuse this inherited Jena method with the methodological reformism of the Altdorf school — Philipp Scherb, Ernst Soner, Michael Piccart, Johannes Kobius — whom Veltheim repeatedly cites as authorities for his key methodological claim: that metaphysics has a *special* use in every one of the superior faculties.

That claim gives the book its distinctive shape. Veltheim does not treat metaphysics as a free-standing speculative discipline chained only to Aristotle's *Metaphysics*. He treats it as the **general propaedeutic to every faculty of the University** — theology, jurisprudence, medicine, moral philosophy, politics, natural law, the law of nations — and accordingly fills every chapter with *continuous examples* drawn precisely from the questions those faculties argue about: the causality of justification, the authority of Scripture as *principium primum* of theology, the capacity of the testator and the formal requisites of a will, the causality of restitution in moral cases, the formal object of the beatific vision, the metaphysics of sacrifice and satisfaction, the real and modal distinctions on which the Lutheran Christological and Eucharistic doctrines turn, and much else. The programme announced in the title (*Theologicis, Iuridicis, Moralibus, Politicis*) is executed consistently across all 49 numbered Capita.

## 3. Structural outline

**No** *Libri*, **no** *Partes* at the top level. The body is one unbroken sequence of chapters:

- **Caput Praeliminare** — *On the Nature, Constitution, and Use of Metaphysics* (what metaphysics is, that it is a true science, its material and formal object, abstraction of reason and real abstraction, its division into a *common* and a *proper* part, and especially its *general* and *special use* in the other disciplines).
- **Capita I–XLIX** — the body of metaphysics proper, running from *ens* and its first distinctions, through the transcendentals, cause, substance and accident, the categories, the distinctions (real, modal, rational), duration and time, signs, the predicates of God and of created spirit, to the Pneumatica (*God, angels, the rational soul separately considered*) at the end.

An **Index Capitum** and **Index Rerum** close the volume. Each chapter is subdivided into numbered **§§** (usually 10–40 per chapter), in the exposition style the title promises: definition, corollary, scholastic note, continuous example from one of the four target faculties.

## 4. Register and conventions in this English rendering

- **Numbering preserved.** Veltheim's §-numbers are retained verbatim; where the OCR corrupted a numeral (e.g. §26 printed as "§16"), the restored number is given silently.
- **Scholastic Latin kept where it carries weight.** *Ens, substantia, accidens, ratio formalis objectiva, praecisio, abstractio praecisiva, principium cognoscendi, principium essendi, abstractio secundum essentiam / secundum indifferentiam, actus signatus / exercitus, causa per se / per accidens* — these are kept italicised and glossed on first appearance, not domesticated.
- **Veltheim's Greek handled in the way he himself handles it.** He regularly embeds Aristotelian Greek phrases (ἀναπόδεικτος, ἀκρότατον, πάθος, ποιητική, θεωρητική, &c.); these are transliterated inline with a glossing English equivalent, as his own text would have been read by a Jena student.
- **The vernacular glosses Veltheim prints in Early New High German** — for example his dictum on real abstraction: *es kann wahrhaftig eins sein ohne das andere, auch wenn gleich niemand daran dencken solte* — are preserved and translated inline.
- **The Scholastic Lutherans house conventions are kept:** scripture citations in bold Vulgate/KJV form; §§-level separators by `— ❦ —` only between chapters, not between §§.

## 5. On the auditor-list

The front matter closes with a four-page list of the names of the students who sat under Veltheim in the privatissimum from which this book grew — one of the few places where a 17th-century Jena *Hörsaal* presents itself under its own names. We reproduce it verbatim (silently correcting the OCR of the German tails and preserving the geographic epithets), both because Veltheim himself writes that he could not in conscience print the book without acknowledging them, and because for the historian of orthodox Lutheran education in the generation before Francke's Halle, this is a snapshot of the student body of Jena at the moment it was still the pre-eminent Protestant university of the Empire.

# Dedicatory Epistle

**To the Magnificent Rector and the Whole Senate of the illustrious University of Jena, to the most reverend and most learned Doctors, Jurisconsults, Experienced and Illustrious Men — patrons, promoters, and most honoured colleagues of the highest observance — Valentin Veltheim dedicates and consecrates these Institutes of Metaphysics with the greatest humility.**

*"I pay what I owe, and still I owe what I pay."* For if I have brought forth anything by writing, I owe it to many of You who, partly by the living voice, partly by Your outstanding writings, have unfolded for the common use of all faculties and the single disciplines which my book treats, those very points of doctrine which I have here either explicated or applied. For as **Pliny** truly says, *"It is a kindly thing, and full of ingenuous modesty, to confess by whom one has made one's progress."* Therefore, that I may make my own what **Cicero** said of himself in the oration *pro Archia*, so I may truly say of You: *"If there is in me any talent, which I feel how slight it is; or any practice of speaking, in which I do not deny that I am of some competent habit; or any exercise and discipline derived from the knowledge of the most honourable arts, from which no part of my life has shrunk — from all these things above all these men can rightly claim their fruit from me. If, moreover, I have been of any use in the University, next after God Most High and the munificent nurturers of the University, the most serene Princes and our most clement Lords, I owe it wholly to Your illustrious Order."*

So far, indeed, am I from thinking that any of You has at any time taken amiss my manner of teaching or of writing, that rather from each of the four Venerable Faculties, either single men or most of You (You know these things were written by me with inmost sincerity, under the eye of Conscience) have added spur to me: namely, that You have approved both the goal set before my public labours and the means by which I have been led to that goal — You who have been Patrons, Promoters, Commenders of my studies.

Accept therefore, best of Patrons and Supporters, with a benevolent hand this Book of Institutes, as at once a token of my observance toward You and a pledge of my future cares for the welfare of the students who aspire to a solid erudition. With a most tenacious memory I keep this: that the profession of Logic and First Philosophy has always been esteemed, both by the Most Ample Academic Senate and by the Philosophical Faculty, so highly indeed that the Most Serene Nurturers of this University, our most Clement Princes and Lords, have been given to understand at great length the weight of what both these disciplines, if solidly, faithfully, and diligently cultivated, bring to the studies of all and single faculties and disciplines — and how great and how numerous are the damages which the neglect of this office can bring, since the students of the Academy who fail in these matters cannot thereafter happily succeed either in any other of the philosophical disciplines or in any faculty whatsoever. Which thing also the Most Serene and Most Illustrious Nurturers, our Princes and Clement Lords, have not long since asserted most clearly in sundry of their most clement Mandates and Rescripts; declaring thereby their zeal for the fostering and promotion of these studies, and their grace and inestimable benignity toward the cultivators and professors of them, by living proofs. Wherefore, with God's help, I will leave no stone unturned, lest the hopes which You have conceived of me be deceived — that *sparta* which, by Your counsels and desires, I have obtained, I will adorn to the uttermost, so long as God and the Most Serene Dukes, the munificent Nurturers, shall will it so.

But — as Fabius narrates concerning the most excellent painter **Timanthes**, that at the sacrifice of Iphigenia, when he had painted a sad Calchas, a sadder Ulysses, and had set beside Menelaus the utmost grief that art could compass, finding no mode by which he could worthily express the countenance of the Father, he veiled his head, and left to each man's imagination to estimate what he could not depict — so I, conscious of my own slightness, do not undertake to paint the grief of Agamemnon, but rather the surpassing virtues of You my Patrons: Your signal and most eminent deserts of this illustrious *Salana* [the University of Jena], Your immense and incredible zeal for the advancement of letters, Your most flourishing and most noble bevy of the finest Students, Your singular prudence in the administration of academic business, Your goodness and equity toward the readers that come under You — all these I leave to be weighed by candid and equitable readers.

Meanwhile, for that benevolence with which hitherto You have embraced me, unworthy — I place before You, within Your most learned pulpits, an altar whose title is:

> *To the Magnificent Rector of the University*
> *and to the T. Senate,*
> *Alone and Necessary Patron,*
> *Most Equitable Estimator of Philosophy:*
> *Farewell and favour me.*

Of Your most celebrated names a cultivator, devoted to every service and office,

VALENTIN VELTHEIM

# Preface to the Reader

**I set before thee, benevolent reader, those fundamental precepts of First Philosophy which men now vulgarly call *Metaphysics* — not confected out of my own brain, nor born only in the last few days, but handed down long ago from the most approved and most celebrated Philosophers, received into use among the Doctors of every Faculty, and wont to be applied to the material of any discipline whatsoever.**

The whole learned world has long seen, and still sees, the signal deserts of my *predecessors* in this chair, especially the most celebrated **Daniel Stahl** of blessed memory and **Johann Andreas Slevogt** of blessed memory, formerly most illustrious Professors of Logic and Metaphysics in this our *Salana* — with what applause, with what fruit and praise they taught Logic and Metaphysics both within the public walls and within the private; how great a number of the best and most learned Students in this *Salana* they increased and preserved to the envy of others; and how, by their lectures, they trained up men not only for the cultivation of the other faculties, but also such as, with the highest praise and the ineffable increase of the public good, were advanced to so many and so weighty ecclesiastical and civil offices. Witness of this are their innumerable disciples and auditors, afterwards great Doctors both in universities and in other places, likewise men constituted in the highest dignities; they still bear witness, as often as, with grateful mind, they call again to memory the Stahlian and Slevogtian deserts.

That I might usefully step in the footprints of such men, the first help came from the *dexterity* of the Most Illustrious **Valentin Bergerus** of blessed memory, Rector of the Gymnasium in my fatherland and a man of signally honoured deserts, which deserts I shall never sufficiently preach forth. For during four years, after the necessary foundational studies, he instilled into me — a slow learner — and into my fellows, by teaching, by examining, by disputing, by most frequent repetition, the doctrine of Stahl and of Slevogt (whose assiduous auditor he had been in this our *Salana* through ten full years and more); so instilled it, I say, that even the dullest might profit and recognise Stahl in Stahl and Slevogt in Slevogt. His deserts toward my welfare I cannot sufficiently preach forth: he judged me, unworthy as I am, worthy to be his successor in the governance of the said Gymnasium or in its Rectorate, and shortly before his blessed death confirmed anew his wishes concerning this matter — wishes which the Fathers of my Fatherland (whom I shall venerate to the utmost) refused to be void; whereby the divine goodness, by this manifest calling of me as by the hand itself, abundantly declared to my grateful spirit that it was called forth to the offices of piety and of thankful observance.

The full weight that the Bergerian formation added was added by the most worthy *successor* of Stahl and Slevogt, a man most eminently deserving both of the public and of my own slender person, continually meriting more every hour: **Friedemann Bechmann**. He admitted me, equipped with so many public testimonies and letters of recommendation, with the utmost benignity as I came to the Jena Muses; he explored my progress, prescribed the method of continuing my studies, taught Logic and Metaphysics most faithfully, and by his paternal counsels blesses me to this very day. Rather than enumerate his deserts, I venerate them in a dutiful silence, because I know that the singular modesty of my venerable preceptor shrinks from my praises. I pass over now the names of my other most free-born preceptors, continuers of the Stahlian doctrine, highest men: among the Theologians, **Johann Musaeus**, **Christoph Chemnitz**, my own host **Johann Ernst Gerhard** of blessed memory, Preceptor equivalent to a Parent, and **Sebastian Niemann** likewise my host; among the Philosophers, **Zeisold** of blessed memory, my Promoter, **Posner**, and **Gottfried Posner**: I shall venerate for ever their benefits, by which the whole fruit of the Logical and Metaphysical doctrines was derived into my studies in Theology, Physics, and Practical Philosophy.

These things I write, benevolent Reader, that thou mayest know to which Doctors I owe the doctrines I have written. Nor let the *prolixity* of these Institutes deter thee: in the Precepts themselves, the fundamental, as they are called, I have so proceeded that neither can I be accused of over-abundance nor of defective brevity, having had the chief precepts printed in a larger type so that they might be committed to memory, repeated, explained — and these in truth do not take up so great a part of the book that I can be rightly accused of any prolixity on their account. Those matters, however, which stand under the title *Notes* and are set in smaller type have been drawn out at greater length by me, in order that many Students, more rightly than is commonly done, should estimate the metaphysical doctrines and their use in the (so-called) superior Faculties, and above all should have something which, after public or private lectures, they might read at home, ruminate, imitate, and learn to turn — as the saying is — into the *marrow and blood* of their principal studies, so to become more fully conversant with them as time goes on.

Of the praises of the metaphysical study I shall not here undertake to speak: I have attempted this in a separate little tract whose title is: ***Apparatus moralis***, that is, *a brief disquisition on the suitable and necessary means and aids by which those must be equipped who wish, in the University, to carry off the fruit of metaphysical studies*, where I have also treated the *origin and progress of the metaphysical study*, and *which writings are most apt for the obtaining of a metaphysical erudition*, together with the deserts of the most illustrious men in First Philosophy. (This *Apparatus Metaphysicus* will see the light, God propitious, shortly.) Much less do I now stop over to answer the *architectonical detractors* and corrupters of First Philosophy — those who dare to judge it, out of the number of the disciplines, as useless, trifling, injurious; who have essayed, with wholly rash attempt — nay, who even now essay — either to drive out the very memorials of this discipline and of the Analytic (whose very name scarce any of them has ever learnt), by rendering them odious to the Students and so by degrees snatching them away altogether; or else, posing themselves as the best doctors and leaders of these very disciplines (though they are rather *seducers* and *cheaters*), ask to be so believed and so honoured by all; a sin which, however much it has been more frequently committed, even by the most inept and the most lazy, against no discipline than against First Philosophy — commonly called Metaphysics — which it has now become fashionable to assail with abuses, though they understand it not a whit more than our *lame Ockershausen pedlar* understands of the Cabala.

Yet hope cherished us, that by the manifest utility of the same, especially in Theological controversies, those who sneered at the light with closed eyes — whether calumniators, or corrupters — would at last yield to the truth, and would be strangers and immune, henceforth, from all guilt of seducing or corrupting the Philosophical Youth. But it has fallen out otherwise: new frauds are daily being devised against the innocence of this discipline, new ambushes laid against the truth — even by those who in theological controversies clearly show that they have in no way been able to spare themselves the use and help of this discipline. And would that they had always used it with that *integrity* which was due; perhaps there would now be far fewer errors, and far fewer contentions, than we — not without groans — now experience. These are the words of the most celebrated Metaphysician **Jakob Reeb**, in his *Apology for the Truth of First Philosophy or Metaphysics against the Celebrated Theologian **William Ames**, Calvinist D. D., and against his sharp and railing-filled disputation entitled* **Disputatio Theologica adversus Metaphysicam**, *first printed at Leiden, afterwards reprinted at Hanau*. But concerning these matters I have treated more at length in the said *Apparatus Metaphysicus*, which, God propitious, will see the light shortly, and in particular capita *on the damages and detriments of Metaphysics, item on the haters and resisters of Metaphysics*. Examples meanwhile of such haters' *Practical Philosophy* occur throughout the Institutes themselves, where if any of them seem to me too sharply refuted and to besmirch the Institutes, Thy *equity* and Thy *prudence*, Reader (if indeed Thou art of prudent mind), will easily free me from all guilt, if Thou consider — as I write with God and a good conscience as my witnesses, as Thy own δύσεις [thine own judgment] can abundantly testify to Thee — that these very words (which do indeed seem sharp) are not mine but my adversaries', turned against himself by me (saving the laws divine and human): a man who did not blush to vomit forth the most dreadful atrocities (which, saving honesty, could scarcely, scarcely be repeated by me) against my innocence.

Three things more, benevolent Reader, it concerns Thee to know.

**First**, I offer Thee these Institutes *complete* — except that we treat God, angels, and the rational soul in so far as positively *separable* and *separate*, in a sequel. For since even today Metaphysical Doctors — not few of them, nor obscure — are found who treat of God, angels, and the separated soul in a *special* discipline which they call **Pneumatology** or **Pneumatics** — not without a sufficient or at least highly plausible reason, which we have inspected in our *Apparatus Metaphysicus* — we have decided that nevertheless the whole discipline of Metaphysics remains full and unimpaired, even if, in the given case, the very name of *theologia naturalis* could not so conveniently be defended as if it were denied. On this hypothesis, our *prior part* will be *abstract metaphysics* — in which, namely, we have treated all those things which, excepting indeed Pneumatics, are treated commonly, whether in the so-called general part or in the other, which men call *special*. And whoever considers this with himself will no longer wonder why we have included the *posterior part* of the Institutes in a thinner quaternion. In short, Pneumatics, in a separate little book included therein, shall see the light.

**Then**, since the cultivators of Theology, of Law, and of Morals promise themselves above all a *practical use* of Metaphysics from the questions touching upon these matters — by the method after which the *axioms* appended to the first chapter of the Institutes have been handed down — a work on *Metaphysical Axiomatics* (if we may so call it) will be treated in a separate opus. Meanwhile, benevolent Reader, if it so please Thee, make good use of these Institutes, written for the glory of God and to Thy own advantage; and if in this or that I have erred, pardon me. For — if, as becomes an honest and prudent man, Thou admonish me — I shall more promptly do it than Thou shalt put forth the monition.

**Lastly**, it is worth my pains to call back to memory, and publicly to proclaim, the singular diligence and the benevolence of my honoured AUDITORS, and their ardour toward these studies: for these are they who, by the most honest entreaties, obtained from me both that I should commit these Institutes to the public types, and that I should enter into private walls with them. Ungrateful I were, did I not honour, by the title and the honour of ***Benevolent, Studious, Attentive, and most Grateful*** both in reality and in disposition, those Auditors who with grateful mind have pursued my name, and have paid their didactic by thought and by hand. May God grant them all those things which must be the solemn wish of good academic Auditors and of a Professor. The memory of this matter I have willed to leave in these pages, out of my gratitude to Those Benevolent Auditors who shared this private Collegium with me — I omit all distinctions of dignity, of erudition, and of surname:

# Caput Praeliminare

### *On the Nature, Constitution, and Use of Metaphysics*

---

**§ 1.** For those about to enter upon the metaphysical study in the University with any fruit, there is as much need of a *propaedeutic* (παιδεία) to Metaphysics as there is, for those about to undertake a long journey, of travelling-provisions. By a *propaedeutic* (παιδεία) to Metaphysics we understand the whole of that doctrine with which anyone who would reach — with gain and without loss of time — the desired goal of metaphysical studies ought to be equipped, rather than seek it in the midst of the exercises of this doctrine itself.

**§ 2.** Most rightly says **Aristotle**: *Ἀεὶ δεῖ πεπαιδεῦσθαι ἔν τι τοῦ πρὸς τὴν ἔντευξιν ἔξεως φιλοσόφου* — *"The hearer or the teacher must always have been propaedeutically trained before he can rightly discharge his office."* And again: *Σπουδαιότατον ἔοικεν εἶναι τὸ ἅμα ζητεῖν τὸ ἐπιστήμην καὶ τὸν τρόπον τῆς ἐπιστήμης* — *"It is a wholly inept and absurd thing to seek simultaneously the science and the mode of the science"* (that is: the doctrine or discipline, and the mode of teaching and of learning it).

**§ 3.** Altogether the future apt auditors of Metaphysics must first equip themselves in these matters, since it will be *their proper office to judge* whether the Master is teaching Metaphysics rightly or ill, whether he keeps himself within the limits of his discipline, and whether in the treatment of things he proceeds *metaphysically* — that is, in such wise that he keeps his promises; that he uses defined terms and ones more known to the auditor; that his *principles* (certified to others also) and his vernacular glosses are fittingly accommodated to his science.

**§ 4.** For those auditors who do not have judgment enough to discern what has the force of *proving* and *demonstrating* and what has not — what produces science and accurate cognition, what passes off ignorance for science, what produces only a tenuous cognition or the mere shadow of a science; in what the Master accommodates himself still more to the auditor's capacity and uses *common* arguments, and in what the nature of the thing requires that he teach according to its own and *domestic* principles; how far the *necessary* differs from the *contingent*, the *true* from the *false*, the *certain* from the *doubtful*, the *common* from the *proper*, the *foreign* from the *domestic* and the *genuine*, the *solid* reasons from any mere declamation whatever, the learned Master from the less learned — such auditors are, as of no other science so least of all of Metaphysics (whose propaedeutic is the most difficult of all), *γνήσιοι* **genuine and apt** auditors.

**§ 5.** For the rest, we leave the *propaedeutic* in the present matter — as something elsewhere ascertained — in its own place; namely that the propaedeutic of Metaphysics, as of every other discipline, is partly *more common* and partly *more special*. The *more common* propaedeutic is plainly the same as the Analytical doctrine — Aristotelian, understand, not such as this or that *Modern* now feigns; for ignorance of the Analytic leaves both the auditors and the Master of Metaphysics groping, and they will feel nothing of the happy progress in solid solid Metaphysical doctrine. To hand down that *more common* propaedeutic now would be to reproduce the whole of the Analytic doctrine, which the reason of our present institute neither requires nor permits.

**§ 6.** This, however (namely the *more special* propaedeutic), cannot be more rightly or more conveniently handed down elsewhere than here; whence, before passing to the Institutes of Metaphysics themselves, we shall touch upon it very briefly: and this both because by other most illustrious Men and Preceptors, whose most honoured footprints to tread (so far as can be done by God's grace and the strength of our wit — let this be the sacred law of our enterprise!) we are vowed to tread, we have been so advised and have seen it so observed; and because this was advised and observed by Aristotle himself.

**§ 7.** And first, that there is a *Metaphysical Science* every man easily admits in whom the natural desire for science is felt; since Metaphysics is above all a science — whence by Aristotle and the Ancients it is called ἀκρότατον, the *topmost* science, the *first* science, the *most honourable* science, the *princess of the sciences*, to whom (as to a mistress) it is fit that the other sciences, as handmaids, not contradict.

**§ 8.** If we regard the *signification of the name* "Metaphysica", properly and according to the use of speech received among the Philosophers, Metaphysics denotes nothing other than a *habit by which it is given to us to know the most commonly-used notions, the most commonly-used axioms, and the most eminent things*. This signification *use* — which is the best master of speech — approves in the universities; although by metonymy also the *discipline* which supplies the most commonly-used notions, the most commonly-used axioms, and the most eminent things, is often called by the same name.

**§ 9.** The *definition* of Metaphysics which, in the schools of the Metaphysical Doctors accommodating themselves ever to the capacity of their auditors, we know to have obtained, is this:

> **Metaphysics is a demonstrative (theoretical) science of being and of the inferiors of being, in so far as they are *really abstract from sensible matter*, or have predicates *really abstracted* from sensible matter.**

**§ 10.** The *common concept* of Metaphysics, which answers to the genus, is expressed by the word *Science*. The *proper concept*, which exhausts the whole essential differentia, is given by the remaining words of the definition.

**§ 11.** For a right perception of that concept, cognition of two things is needed: first, that it be abundantly settled that Metaphysics is truly and properly so-called a *Science*; second, that there are *other* sciences, besides Metaphysics, truly and properly so-called. Both are easy to recognise, and will appear in what follows.

**§ 12.** For the exposition of this concept it is pertinent to know that Metaphysics is: (1) a science *demonstrative*, (2) *theoretical*, (3) *of being and of the inferiors of being, in so far as these are really abstract from sensible matter, and in so far as they have predicates really abstracted from sensible matter.*

**§ 13.** To prove most exactly that *Metaphysics is a science, and indeed a demonstrative one*, is to teach all and single the demonstrations by which the whole Metaphysical science is acquired. Let it suffice to have adduced one or another example of Metaphysical demonstrations in passing. Certainly, as it is established from the Analytic that of demonstrations some are called *ὅτι* [demonstrations *that*], some *διότι* [demonstrations *why*], some *per impossibile* — so it is most certain that *all* and *single* modes of demonstration have their place in Metaphysics.

**§ 14.** Is not that a *most excellent demonstration* by which the Metaphysician demonstrates the infallible truth of this proposition — ***GOD IS*** — in the following way? *Whatever is, either is from itself or from another. The whole universe is. Therefore, either it is from itself or from another. But it is not from itself, since these corruptible things cannot be the cause of themselves — indeed, nothing is the cause of its own prior being. Therefore the whole universe is from another. Further, there is no progress to infinity. Therefore it is necessary that we come to rest in some one First, which is the cause of all the rest and depends on no other. And that is **Being of Beings**, that is, **GOD**.* For all men, hearing the name of God, understand that infinite, most noble, and first of all beings, which depends on none; and that there should be two or more infinite beings implicates a manifest contradiction. All these things will be more accurately and more clearly treated in the *latter part* of these Institutes.

**§ 15.** Equally, *demonstrations διότι* ["demonstrations by cause"] have altogether their place in Metaphysics. Does not the Metaphysician, for example, demonstrate the *eternity* of God *through immutability*; immutability *through utter simplicity*; utter simplicity *through infinity*; infinity *through independence*, and so forth? For the demonstrations *of this kind* do not always require that what is demonstrated and the cause through which it is demonstrated really differ among themselves; it suffices that the reason be *domestic* and simply *proximate*, which can satisfy *per totum* a solid question put by an attentive auditor.

**§ 16.** And *demonstrations per impossibile* also have the amplest place in Metaphysics. For example: if the Metaphysician would demonstrate that *God is wholly ἀπαθής, bare of all passive potency*, he demonstrates it *through the absurd and the impossible* thus: ***If in God there were some passive potency, he could receive something through it — either a perfection, or something involving some imperfection. Neither can be said. Therefore in God there is no passive potency.*** For that He cannot receive any *perfection* through it is easily seen, since He would so not be *perfect* but could still receive another; which is absurd, foreign to all piety. That He cannot receive, through such passive potency, anything involving *imperfection* is likewise easily seen, since so there would be imperfection in God, which also is absurd and impious. — Likewise, if any should deny the truth of this proposition, *God can be*, he may easily be led into the absurd thus: *Whatever cannot be, is impossible to be. God cannot be. Therefore it is impossible that God be.* The conclusion is absurd and foolish — for *"the fool hath said in his heart, there is no God"* (**Ps. 14:1**). The form of the syllogism is best; whence, since from true premises in legitimate form a false conclusion cannot follow, it must be that either both premises or at least one of them is false. Of the *major* premise's truth no man of sane judgment doubts, since what cannot be is not possible to be, and what is not possible to be is impossible to be, as even a blind man sees. Therefore the *minor* is false. But the minor was, *God cannot be*; and since this minor is contradictorily opposed to the proposition laid down before, *God can be*, and two contradictorily opposed propositions cannot both be false, it is necessary — since this one, *God cannot be*, is false — that the former, *God can be*, is true, since whatever is or is not, it is impossible for the same to be and not to be at once. — As, then, from what has just been adduced it comes out of its own forehead that Metaphysics is a truly demonstrative habit, that is, a demonstrative science, and so truly and properly so-called a science: since *Physics*, *Mathematics*, and many practical and effective habits are likewise acquired through demonstrations proper to their kind, there is no doubt but that *science* may with saving truth be predicated of many habits — and is actually predicated — so that it comes very near to answering as a *genus*.

**§ 17.** It might indeed seem that *Metaphysics* cannot be defined through *science* as its *integral part* (as they say) — for by the common consent of Aristotle and all the Philosophers Metaphysics is **wisdom** (*σοφία*), and the received and popularly known definition of wisdom is this: ***Wisdom is a habit composed of understanding and science of the most eminent things.*** Hence he who should wish to call an *integral part* of Metaphysics its *genus* would seem as inept as he who should call the head the genus of the man. The whole difficulty, in truth, is bred by the too thin exposition of Aristotle's dictum, in which (at *Nic. Eth.* VI, c. 7) he had called wisdom *νοῦς καὶ ἐπιστήμη* — *understanding and science* — and in the same chapter had defined it in express words: *ἡ σοφία ἄρα ἔσται νοῦς καὶ ἐπιστήμη τῶν τιμιωτάτων τῇ φύσει* — *"wisdom therefore is both science and understanding, concerning the by-nature-most-eminent things."* For neither from these words of Aristotle nor from the context can it be gathered — nor can it ever be admitted without loss of truth — that wisdom is truly and really composed of understanding and science, however much the vulgar of philosophisers suffer themselves to be persuaded of this. For wisdom and understanding (ἐπιστήμη and σοφία) are adequately distinct habits, nowise regarding one another as *integral part* and *integral whole*. *Understanding* (νοῦς) is a habit *ἀναπόδεικτος* — one which cannot be acquired by any demonstration, or (what comes to the same) which can be neither taught nor learnt. *Wisdom*, by contrast, is a habit *οὐκ ἀναπόδεικτος* — truly demonstrative, that is, able to be taught and learnt. It does not *include* understanding but presupposes it; for *understanding* is the habit of the first principles, while *wisdom* is the habit of *confirming* those first principles (known certainly and easily by the mediation of understanding) for the benefit of the doubtful, and of *demonstrating* them, against deaf and stubborn adversaries, *per impossibile et absurdum*. Deliberately we say *per impossibile et absurdum*: for the first principles cannot be known by a demonstration *ὅτι*, since such a demonstration necessarily rests on priors, whereas the first principles — being first — admit nothing prior to themselves, since to the first there is no prior.

**§ 18.** Nor is there anything in the objection that at least *wisdom* and *science absolutely so-called* (or *theoretical science*) remain essentially distinct habits under one and the same proximate genus — namely, under the *theoretical intellectual habit* — and mutually opposed, so that it can never come about that two *disparate species* are predicated *in quid* of one and the same inferior, namely of Metaphysics, and therefore that Metaphysics must either not be a *science* or not be a *wisdom*. For either *demonstrative science* is taken for any demonstrative habit whatsoever, from first principles, without heed whether those principles be *simply first* or *first in a certain genus*; or it is taken for a demonstrative habit from principles that are first in a certain genus (which some call *secondary* principles). If science be taken in the former way, then any party-free man can easily see that *demonstrative science* so taken and *wisdom* are not disparate but *subordinate*, and that *science* can altogether be the *genus*, or at least answer to the character of a genus in order to *wisdom* (that is, to Metaphysics) and to *Physics* and many other habits: for *Metaphysics*, *Physics*, *Mathematics*, &c., are habits demonstrative from first principles, whatever may be in it that to these only *secondary* principles belong, while to those also *primary* principles — i.e. simply and absolutely first — do belong, since this does not in any way enter into the essential constitution of science so taken. If science be taken in the latter way, we readily grant that science in that rigour is neither the genus of Metaphysics nor answers to its genus — since Metaphysics is not a demonstrative habit that uses only secondary principles.

**§ 19.** It stands, then, hitherto, both from the definition of science and from the definition of wisdom, that Metaphysics is both science and wisdom; nor can it any longer be obscure *why only the former, and not the latter, can be the genus of Metaphysics*. For *science* extends more widely than *Metaphysics*; *wisdom* not at all — since all and only Metaphysics is wisdom, and conversely all and only wisdom is Metaphysics. Hence, since to every genus simply and necessarily it is required that it be able to extend more widely than its species — and since this can never truly be said of *wisdom* as against Metaphysics — the reason stands out clear why *wisdom* cannot be called the genus of Metaphysics.

**§ 20.** You say: *Wisdom can be divided into First and Second, so that wisdom extends more widely than Metaphysics, which alone is First Wisdom*; and you cite Aristotle as patron of this division. But a truer reason dictates that **Second Wisdom**, accurately and properly speaking, is not wisdom at all: for wisdom rigorously so called is conversant about *first principles*, since (as said above) it is the office of wisdom to unfold the objects of *understanding* itself — that is, to expound those notions out of which the first principles consist, to *confirm* the first principles themselves for the benefit of the doubting, and to *demonstrate* them *per impossibile* against those who deny. More clearly: *Understanding* is conversant about absolutely first principles as being *first and known per se*; *Wisdom* treats them as *conclusions*. Understanding gives the intellect readiness of assent to the first principles; Wisdom supplies evidence of them. — Nay, feign at length some other wisdom besides First Wisdom (i.e. Metaphysics): it will be conversant either about things *less first*, or *more first*, or *equally first*. Not the last: for if Metaphysics and your supposed wisdom were conversant about equally first principles, the principles of both would be comprehended under *earlier* and *more common* principles, and so neither of the two "wisdoms" would be first — which is absurd. Not the middle: that is, it cannot be that your supposed wisdom be conversant about *more first* things, because nothing more common and nothing prior than what is handed down in Metaphysics can you feign, however much you may wish to err; and if you do feign it, then Metaphysics against your own assertion will not remain *first*. Not the first alternative either: for if your feigned wisdom were conversant about *less* common and first things, and Metaphysics about *more* common, then your feigned wisdom would be demonstrative in such wise that in all its demonstrations it would depend on Metaphysics — which plainly repugns to wisdom rightly so called. The matter is at a stand, unless we would play with terms, so that *science* and *wisdom* be taken for the same and signify entirely the same thing: and this play — to what end? Nor is it ever to be deduced from Aristotle's Philosophy that he divided *wisdom*, in opposition to *science*, into First and Second. *φιλοσοφία πρώτη καὶ δευτέρα* — "First and Second Philosophy" — in the Philosopher's mind are the First and Second Sciences; for now *σοφία* denotes the same as *science*, now the same as *wisdom*; which no man denies, unless he would call the Seven Sages of Greece the Seven Metaphysicians of Greece. Each signification is proper and received in use. Let the like judgment be made of the word *φιλοσοφία*, if you consider the usage of speech.

**§ 21.** Not without good reason have we assigned a *twofold essential differentia* to Metaphysics in calling it a *science* and *demonstrative*; because *science without demonstration* can also be given — such namely as is *understanding* (νοῦς) and *theorematic* (θεωρηματική), that is, such as cannot be acquired except by acts of the intellect purely *theoretic* — acts, that is, that are directive of no *actio in the strict sense* nor of any *effectio*, as the Schools speak. For such are called those acts by which the intellect judges whether or how an *external action strictly so-called* (ἔνδοθεν or ἔξωθεν) or an *effection* ought to be committed or omitted. — The apt auditors or cultivators of Metaphysics must have very clearly perceived that no more accurate division of demonstrative science is given, nor can be given, than if it be divided into *contemplative, active, and effective* (θεωρητική, πρακτική, καὶ ποιητική).

**§ 22.** Lastly, the *essential differentia* of Metaphysics was sought from its *total or adequate object*, which, as in other sciences, is wont commonly to be stated as twofold in Metaphysics: the *material*, that is, the thing about which the science is conversant — here, *being with its inferiors*; and the *formal*, that is, the character under which the science is conversant about the thing (its material object) — which, so far as this present matter is concerned, is *real abstraction from sensible matter*.

**§ 23.** Therefore the ***total and adequate object*** of Metaphysics will be: *being and the inferiors of being*, in so far as they are *really abstract from sensible matter* and have *predicates really abstracted from sensible matter*.

**§ 24.** Something different in the meantime (if we would philosophise more accurately) is the *object*, and something different the *formal objective reason* (*ratio formalis objectiva*, as the Schools speak). It can happen — nay, in all sciences and disciplines it actually does happen — that many objects are given of one and the same science or discipline, yet so that there remains *one and the same first ratio of knowing*, into which all other cognitions of all and single objects at last run and tend. For example: many objects of the physical science are given — stone, lion, man, tree, sky, earth, water, &c.; but these and far more others have *one formal ratio* under which they are known in Physics, namely, in so far as they are *natural*. So if we take, by way of example, a *faculty*, as *sight*: there again many objects are given — wood, stone, lion, man, rose, cherry-tree, &c.; but these and innumerable others have *one ratio* under which they are known, and by which they have it that they are objects of sight, namely *the ratio of the coloured*: for they are not visible except in so far as they are coloured.

**§ 25.** And this is the cause why the Schoolmen Doctors distinguish *formal objective ratio* from the *objects themselves*: for just as elsewhere whatever is, is by its *form*, and *form gives being to the thing*, and the thing itself is said to receive its *denomination* and its *being* from the form — so many things have it, that they are objects of one science or discipline, from *one ratio of knowing*, which was for that reason called *ratio formalis objectiva* by the Scholastics. For unless there were one genuine and formal ratio from which many things have it that they are objects of one science or discipline, one science or discipline would not be *one*, which implicates a contradiction.

**§ 26.** As, therefore, there is said to be *one formal object of sight*, because all visible things name *one and the same ratio*, namely the ratio of *colouredness*, as just noted — so it cannot be denied that there are *innumerable objects* of the Metaphysical science (since Metaphysics is conversant not only about *being*, but also about *the inferiors of being*), while all the objects agree in *one and the same formal ratio*, which is and is called *real abstraction from sensible matter*.

**§ 27.** Here comes to be said what is called *abstraction*, what *real abstraction from sensible matter*, what *sensible matter*. The notion of abstraction in common cannot be defined; and at once there is need of the distinction between *abstraction of reason* (*abstractio rationis*) and *real abstraction* (*abstractio realis*). From the former something is denominated *ratione abstractum* (abstract by reason); from the latter, *realiter abstractum* (really abstract). Two things, therefore, are required to any abstraction whatsoever: one, *what is abstracted*; another, *that from which it is abstracted*.

**§ 28.** ***Abstract by reason*** is said to be ***that which, apart from another, is known by the intellect or through the mediation of the intellect***, whether it agree or not with that other, and whether it be really one and the same with it or not. *Man denckt an eins, und an das andere nicht; das ist, man denckt an eins ohne das andere.* "One thinks of the one, and not of the other; that is to say, one thinks of the one without the other." For example: when, by my intellect, I know or think that *man is an animal*, yet do not at the same time know or think that *he is partaker of reason*, I abstract by my reason the *animality* of man from his *rationality*, since I know the animality of man without distinctly knowing man's rationality at the same time. Hence it is easily recognised what is *abstraction of reason*.

**§ 29.** The Scholastics make the *abstraction of reason* twofold: one *negative*, the other *praecisive*. The *negative abstraction* of reason they call that by which reason or the intellect *divides one thing from another*, to which division a negation corresponds: e.g. when my intellect knows that *man is not stone*, or thinks that *man is not rational*, this thought the Scholastics call *negative* abstraction, also *divisive*, since the intellect *divides* man from stone, or man from the rational, to which act these negative propositions correspond: *man is not stone, man is not rational*. ***Praecisive*** abstraction is the abstraction of reason by which one is known without the other, yet without any intervening *division* or *negation*: as, when I know what an *animal* is — namely, a mixed, animated, sensing natural body — yet do not at the same time know that it is a *man*, nor divide animal from man, nor (which comes to the same thing) deny man of animal: this manner of cognition is called *praecisive* abstraction of reason. For us, whenever in Metaphysics we speak of *abstraction of reason*, the speech is of *praecisive* abstraction — which is also called *praecisio* and, by excellence (*κατ' ἐξοχήν*), *abstractio rationis*.

**§ 30.** And of this (namely, of *praecisive* abstraction of reason) is to be understood that renowned saying — ***Solus est philosophus, qui bene abstrahit*** — "He alone is a Philosopher who abstracts well": that is, that man is the accurate Philosopher who knows how to recognise and weigh the objects of his science not confusedly, but becomingly and distinctly. To which fits well the common saying, *Qui bene distinguit, bene docet* — "He who distinguishes well, teaches well." Of *praecisive abstraction* we also accept this other dictum: ***Abstrahentium non est mendacium*** — "There is no lying in abstracters." For he who knows the one without knowing the other at the same time does not on that account at once affirm the one of the other, or deny the other; and so he cannot be convicted of lying. The contrary is the case with *negative abstraction*, by whose users one often lies. So much for *abstraction of reason*.

**§ 31.** ***Really abstract*** (*realiter abstractum*) is said to be ***that which, with no one thinking of it, can be without the other***. — *Es kan wahrhafftig eins seyn ohne das ander, wenn auch gleich niemand dran dencken solte, dass es ohne das andere seyn könne.* "It can truly be one thing without the other, even if nobody at all should think about it that it can be without the other." For example: with no one thinking of it, an *animal* can be without a *brute*, because though no brute at all were given, still an animal could be given with no one thinking of it, since a *man* can still be given; whence an animal is said to be really abstract from a brute. So *spirit* can be, with no one thinking of it, without matter — indeed is without matter, because to the essence of spirit *immateriality* belongs, that is, out of its own essence or by its own strength of being spirit is immaterial; whence spirit is denominated as really abstracted from matter.

**§ 32.** For the rest, something can be said *really abstract* from another whether it be in that other or belong to it or not — provided only, with no one thinking of it, it can be without that other. For it is not required of a *really abstract* that it always *actually be* without that from which it is really abstract. And here we especially note, lest we confound (as many are wont) *real abstraction* with *real separation*, that of a *really separated* it is certain that it is actually *without* that from which it is really separated; but not every *really abstract* is also *really separated*. For example: *animal* is really abstract from *man* and from *brute*, yet it is not really separated from man and brute. So the Philosophers rightly say that *genus* is really abstract from its species, though it can never come about that *genus* is really separated from all its species. — Real *separation* is twofold: one *positive*, by whose force that which is really separated exists separately on its own after separation (such is the real positive separation of nail from wall — for after it the nail exists apart, perhaps in your hand, the wall exists apart from it). The other *privative*, by whose force that which is really separated cannot after the completed separation exist any longer: as when *whiteness* is separated from a wall, since once the real separation of whiteness from the wall is made, the wall remains, the whiteness must perish — it could not remain. Now if *animal* were really separated from *man* and *brute* with a *positive* separation, then it could happen that an animal would be given which is neither man nor brute, or again that a man or a brute would be given which is not an animal — which is absurd. If *animal* were really separated or really separable from man and brute with a *privative* separation, then again it could happen that no animal would be, yet man or brute would be — and could be without animal — which blows forth a great absurdity.

**§ 33.** ***Real abstraction*** is in turn twofold: one is called by the Scholastics *absolute*, also *abstraction according to essence*, also *abstraction according to excellence*; the other is called *permissive* abstraction, also *abstraction according to indifference*. The *former* is the real abstraction by which *the one which is said to be really abstract from the other* ***cannot not be without the other*** — that is, cannot be *in* or *with* that other, or be that other: as *God* is said to be abstract from sensible matter *according to essence and absolutely*, since he cannot be sensible matter nor material. The *latter* is the real abstraction by which the thing (namely, what is held as really abstract from another) **can also be in that other, or with it, or itself be it**: as *being* is really abstract from sensible matter *permissively, i.e. according to indifference*, since being is given without any matter, being is given which is itself matter (because matter is being), being is given material, such as is every being that depends in its being on matter.

**§ 34.** ***Sensible matter*** is called *physical matter*, in so far as it can fall under the external senses or be known by the senses. Thus semen, menstrual blood, and the like are *sensible matter*, since they can be tasted, touched, &c. So too metal, wood, are sensible matter, since they can be seen, touched.

**§ 35.** As, therefore, every man will now with easy effort attain *what it is to say*: *being and the inferiors of being are really abstract from sensible matter* — namely, this: *being and the inferiors of being so stand, that with no one thinking of them they can be without sensible matter* — so one easily perceives also that things *really abstract from sensible matter* are of two kinds. First, there are given things abstract by the *real absolute* abstraction, or *abstraction according to essence*; that is, such that they *cannot be sensible matter itself, nor in sensible matter nor with sensible matter*. Thus, e.g., really abstract from sensible matter by *abstraction according to essence* or *absolute* abstraction, are these inferiors of being: *spirit, God, angels, the soul positively separated*. Next, there are given things really abstract by *the real permissive* abstraction, or *abstraction according to indifference*: namely such that they can also be sensible matter itself, in or with sensible matter: in this mode really abstract are these inferiors of being: *substance, accident*, and the like.

**§ 36.** ***The Parts of Metaphysics are twofold:***

> **Prior** — the part that treats of being and the inferiors of being, in so far as they are really abstract from sensible matter *according to indifference* or by permissive abstraction, not by absolute abstraction. — **Posterior** — the part that treats of those inferiors of being which are really abstract from sensible matter *according to excellence* or by absolute abstraction.

**§ 37.** The common division of Metaphysics is thus ordinarily set up: that one part be called *common* or quasi *general*, which treats of being in common and of the most common affections of being; the other, quasi *special* and *proper*, which treats of the inferiors of being, in so far as they are really abstract from sensible matter, or have *less common predicates* really abstracted from sensible matter. (For in so far as the inferiors of being have the most common predicates really abstracted from sensible matter, so far they are wont to be referred to the *common* part.) In the meantime, for the sake of a better order and more fittingly to our purpose, it pleases us — saving a better judgment and with the good leave of any — to hand down the division of Metaphysics differently. Far be it, meanwhile, that we reject the common and received division: nor yet is there reason for anyone to wonder if, by the diversity of division, we respond diversely to the same question. For example: if it be asked, *to which part of Metaphysics does the natural body belong, if we take it precisely as it has predicates really abstract from sensible matter* — the answer is: if you take up the vulgarly-received division of Metaphysics into *common* and *proper* parts, it pertains to *both* — to the *common* indeed in so far as the natural body has predicates really abstracted from sensible matter which are *most common* to the natural body in community with other inferiors; to the *proper*, if the natural body's predicates really abstracted from sensible matter be considered which are *not common to all inferiors of being*: as, when the natural body is considered as *being*, as *one*, as *true being*, &c., the most common predicates are considered, whose treatment altogether pertains to the common part. But if the natural body be considered as *substance*, as *second substance*, &c., those predicates are attended to whose consideration is wont to be referred to the *special* part. — By our division, however, the whole consideration of the predicates really abstracted from sensible matter — which belong to the natural body — is rightly referred to one and the same part, since all and single those predicates are really abstract from sensible matter, not by absolute but by permissive abstraction.

**§ 38.** But this we especially note concerning our proposed division: that **to Metaphysics there also belong those inferiors of being which, though not themselves really abstract from sensible matter, nevertheless have predicates really abstracted from sensible matter**: e.g. natural body, sky, tree, stone, animal, &c., are inferiors of being and are not really abstract from sensible matter (since they cannot be without matter, and matter itself is their essential part); yet they do have predicates abstracted from sensible matter — being, substance, &c.

**§ 39.** ***The use of Metaphysics is nothing other than the fitting, decent, and genuine application of the metaphysical doctrines to the other disciplines and faculties.***

**§ 40.** ***And the use of Metaphysics is twofold: general and special.*** The *general* extends to all disciplines and faculties. The *special* refers to one precise discipline — e.g. Theology, forensic Jurisprudence, Moral Philosophy, &c.

**§ 41.** And indeed as to the *general use* of Metaphysics — that which Metaphysics itself furnishes rather than promises to the cultivators and doctors of all the Faculties and disciplines — no man will deny it who rightly attends to the office of the Professor and Master of Metaphysics; which is comprehended in these two: (1) that he hand down **the most common notions**, most familiar to every kind of discipline and faculty, so that certainly no discipline can excuse itself from them, and so that the cultivators and doctors of every faculty — nill they, will they — must own the eminent use of these notions. Of this sort are, for example, the notions of *formal reason*, *essence*, of *adequate* and *inadequate concept*, of *primary* and *secondary* concept, of *principle*, of *cause*, of *ratio of causing*, of *causality*, of *necessary conditions*, of *order*, of *formal effect*, of *form*, of *subject*, of *object*, of *end*, of *efficient*, of *impulsive cause*, of *foundation*, of *sign*, of *norm*, of *actus primus*, *actus secundus*, *actus signatus*, *actus exercitus*, of *complete cause*, of *subordinated causes*, of *coordinated causes*, &c. Here also belong the *highest genera* of all things. Certainly no discipline can be learned or taught with any happy accuracy unless the use of these notions is admitted. (2) That he open up ***the most common and the first fountains of knowing*** — that is, exhibit the most common dicta and axioms upon which all doctrines and disciplines whatsoever rely, into which all the rest of Philosophy at last runs and resolves, and which are most common to Theology, to Jurisprudence, and to the science of the art of medicine.

**§ 42.** Deliberately we say *the axioms and first principles of Theology and Jurisprudence are most common.* For what is vulgarly said — that *Philosophy relies on rational principles, Theology and Jurisprudence on bare authority* — is said indeed most truly, but nowise to the prejudice of our assertion. It is said most truly, because a great difference does intercede between theological and juridical argumentations on the one side and philosophical ones on the other. In philosophical argumentations, when the *principle of knowing* is at once the *cause*, it is either connected immediately with the extremes of the conclusion, or mediately: if the former, it is a first principle, known *per se*, and needs no proof from elsewhere; if the latter, it must be proved from some other prior principle, yet that very principle from which the connection has to be demonstrated is drawn from the nature of the thing itself. But in Theology and in forensic Jurisprudence the matter stands otherwise. For though Theology, in matters to be told, teaches that one is the cause of another — e.g. that faith is the cause of justification, and justification the cause of grace with God, of tranquillity of a good conscience, and of hope of eternal life — yet since this is not known to us except out of **Sacred Scripture** and divine revelation, we must at last recur to divine revelation as the ultimate principle: **Whatsoever the Canonical Sacred Scripture teaches is infallibly true** — whose certainty cannot escape anyone, nor be called into doubt by any, who has the nature and essence of Sacred Scripture rightly before his eyes. Similarly, though forensic Jurisprudence treats of things through their causes — as e.g. if any would fully *prove* a testament having full juridical effect, he must prove that all and single causes, and all and single necessary requisites of making a will (as they say) on the side of the causes, were present: on the side of the **efficient cause**, that the testator (that is, he whose testament is now fully to be proved) is *paterfamilias*, of sound mind, not a minor, &c.; on the side of **matter** or **object**, that he has free administration of his goods; and, if the testator is a *filiusfamilias*, that he has made testament only about *peculium castrense* or *quasi-castrense*, &c.; on the side of **form** or **mode**, that he employed and specifically called the witnesses — and, since he was blind, employed besides the seven witnesses also a *tabularius*, or an eighth witness, &c.; on the side of **end**, that he has not been deprived of testamentary faculty for a crime or for some other public cause, &c. — yet because of these matters nothing can be clear to anyone except out of the **Civil Law** and the decrees of the legislator, we must at last take refuge in the Civil Law; and the so-called Corpus of Civil Law is itself the seat and quasi-domicile of the principles of Law, to which the Jurisconsult must equally recur, and from which his proofs must be sought — just as the Theologian is bound to flee to Sacred Scripture, if any dogma of the faith among Christians be called into doubt and must be confirmed by solid arguments. There remains therefore the ***first principle*** into which all juridical proofs are at last resolved: **the authority of Civil Law**; so that whatever Civil Law establishes, ratifies, approves, is *civilly just*, that is, has the effect of civil law.

**§ 43.** Rightly, therefore, is to be understood what the Theologians and Jurisconsults commonly teach, when they say of themselves: *We argue only from authority; the Philosophers argue only from reason.* Namely, there must not be confounded the ***first principle proper*** of Theology or of Jurisprudence, and the ***first principles of Theology and Jurisprudence common in use with other disciplines*** (deliberately we say *common in use*, since the same principles remain partly Metaphysical, partly Analytical by their own proper consideration). Most true indeed is this of the first principle *proper* to Theology or to Civil Jurisprudence, into which (as we said) all theological and juridical proofs run and are at last resolved — for whatever has to be proved by Theologians or by Jurisconsults, these men prove not from anywhere else than, the one from Sacred Scripture, the other from Civil Law. But the students of Theology and of Civil Jurisprudence certainly deceive themselves — as many as persuade themselves in vain that they can therefore do without the study of Metaphysics and of Logic; they deceive themselves — as many as are haters and gnawers of these studies — since Theologians and Jurisconsults, just as the Philosophers, argue from cause, from end, from efficient, from object, from form, from subject, from effect, from property, &c. And to say it briefly: all those modes of arguing which are prescribed in Logic have their place also in Theology and in Jurisprudence, if the speech be of *proximate principles* from which theological and juridical conclusions are proximately deduced. Since these things are true and will easily, without further demonstration, obtain assent from all who are endowed with sound judgment, who, I ask — provided he be free of preconceived opinions — would doubt of the manifest use of the metaphysical study in Theology and Jurisprudence as in the other disciplines?

**§ 44.** For it cannot be that any cultivator of Theology or Jurisprudence should rightly use the *loci* of argumentations unless he has previously known and intimately seen through the metaphysical doctrine, since there is no argumentation from a logical *locus* that cannot and must not be referred to some chapter of the Metaphysical doctrine. For example: how can theological or juridical questions concerning the *causes* of spiritual and theological or forensic things and businesses be answered unless you have thoroughly held the Metaphysical doctrine of *causes*? All questions, if we except the question *an sit*? "*is it*?", are — as in the other disciplines, so also in Theology and Jurisprudence (except where there is discussion of the sense of a dictum of Sacred Scripture or of civil law) — about *one or some or many or all causes*. In Theology and in forensic Jurisprudence it is only when the cause of each theological or forensic thing is fully known that something is truly known.

**§ 45.** Let us resume the examples cited above at § 42. Though it be clear only from Sacred Scripture that man is justified by faith, that he who is justified by faith has God propitious to him, that is, grace with God, tranquillity of conscience (i.e. a glad and tranquil conscience), and the hope of eternal life — yet it cannot be denied that in this theological argumentation — *Whoever is justified has peace of conscience, grace with God, hope of eternal life. But whosoever believes in Christ is justified. Therefore...* (whose Major is proved from **Rom. 5:1–2**, whose Minor from **Rom. 3:28**) — ***justification*** stands as cause, and ***peace of conscience, grace with God, hope of eternal life*** stand as effects of justification; and this whole argumentation is drawn from the *locus of cause*, and rests on this double axiom: ***Posita causa ponitur effectus*** — "Given the cause, the effect is given"; and ***Causa causae est causa causati*** — "The cause of a cause is the cause of what is caused" (for *faith* is the cause of justification, and justification itself is the cause of peace of conscience, of grace with God, of hope of eternal life; and so faith is the cause of peace of conscience, of grace with God, of hope of eternal life). For these two do not fight with one another: *The premisses in this theological argumentation contain the principle of being, i.e. the cause why the predicate belongs to the subject*; and, *These premisses are at last resolved into the principle of knowing, which is not at once the principle of being*. Both remain most true. For since neither premiss can be proved except out of Sacred Scripture, the whole argumentation is at last resolved into this common principle: ***Whatsoever Sacred Scripture says is infallibly true*** — which principle is not a principle *of being*, but only a principle *of knowing*. **Behold, then, the use of Metaphysics in Sacred Scripture!**

**§ 46.** Let the same judgment be made of the other example cited (§ 42). For although it be known to us only from Civil Law that the faculty of testating is granted by Civil Law only to *patresfamilias* (*paterfamilias uti legassit*, &c.), and that *filiifamilias* cannot make a testament (**Institutes**, title *Quibus non est permissum facere testamentum*; **Dig.** 28.1.6; **C.** eod. tit., §penult.) — and formerly indeed could not, because they had nothing of their own (**Ulp. tit. 20**, §9); nor to-day can, since they do not yet have anything by full right as *patresfamilias* save for *peculium castrense* or *quasi* (princip. *Institutes*, ead. tit.); likewise that those who are destitute of mind cannot make a testament (**Inst.** eod. loc.; **Dig.** 28.1.2 §13 *in fine*, *de inoff. test.*); likewise that even the testament of minors is void, though they left something in time of plague, or with authority of guardians, or for pious causes (**Carpzov, Pars III, Const. 6, Def. 8**); likewise that the blind cannot make a testament except by the observance which the law of divine Justinian (namely **C.** 6.22.8, *Quibus non est permissum facere testamentum*) introduced — by which it is provided that the blind, about to name an heir, shall employ besides the seven witnesses also a *tabularius*, who shall receive the will of the one declaring into writing; or, if there is no copy of a *tabularius* at hand, an eighth witness; and all shall subscribe and seal. — Though, I say, these and far more things, which we here pass over with dry foot, be known to someone from Civil Law alone, yet it remains most true that these matters either are themselves causes or arguments on the side of necessary requisite causes, which cannot be felicitously and accurately understood by one who has not — or has not well — ascertained *what and how many causes* obtain in forensic matters, and hence also in testaments. For as we said: the full science of any forensic thing consists in the fitting cognition of all its causes. How, then, could one destitute of the Metaphysical and Analytic doctrine (whose chief and most eminent part is that which treats of *causes*) discern the true and full science of any forensic thing from a proletarian or rude or imperfect notice of the same? — I rejoice that men of the greatest Judgment fully agree in this.

**§ 47.** For the rest, we must not entirely pass over this: that the very ***first principle proper to Theology, absolutely so-called***, can itself be resolved into another ***first principle common to Theology with the other sciences***. For *the principle absolutely first proper to Theology* was: **Whatsoever Canonical Sacred Scripture teaches is infallibly true.** Now there is no doubt but that this can be resolved into that axiom which, considered in its proper subject, is Metaphysical, but in use is common to all disciplines and faculties: ***Impossibile est idem simul esse et non esse*** — "It is impossible that the same thing simultaneously be and not be" — thus: *Unless what Canonical Sacred Scripture teaches were infallibly true, God, who is immutable Truth itself, would simultaneously be and not be. He is — as is manifest not only from Sacred Scripture but is also solidly demonstrated in Metaphysics, both from His infinite science (for the omniscient cannot be deceived) and from His infinite goodness (for He who is of infinite goodness neither wills to deceive, nor can, nor can will to deceive). He would not be, since by His word He would deceive or could deceive — for Sacred Scripture is the word of God; and how would God, who is immutable Truth itself, remain immutable Truth, unless His word were infallibly true?* The whole discourse, as every man easily sees, rests on this axiom: *It is impossible for the same thing simultaneously to be and not to be.* For it is one thing to ask whether Sacred Scripture is canonical and of divine authority; another, whether what Sacred Scripture teaches is infallibly true. The decision of the latter question, dependent on the decision of the former, at last runs into that axiom and is ultimately resolved. — And what need is there of words? Does not the Author of Sacred Scripture Himself, that is, God Himself, make the same analysis and assert the same thing? Let us look at the words of the Epistle to the Romans, c. 11, v. 6: ***If by grace, then is it no more of works: otherwise grace is no more grace. But if it be of works, then is it no more grace: otherwise work is no more work.*** Does not the Holy Spirit assert this truth — *If by grace, not of works? If of works, not of grace?* — and resolve it into this axiom: *It is absolutely absurd and simply impossible that the same thing simultaneously be and not be*: e.g. that it be *grace* and *not be grace*, that it be *work* and *not be work*. — Therefore these two do not fight with one another: *That there is a principle absolutely first of Theology, and that it can be resolved into another principle*. For that is called the *absolutely-first principle of some discipline* which is the proper principle of that discipline; and absolutely remains Sacred Scripture alone the principle of Theology — not those philosophical principles that are *common* to Theology with Philosophy. But those which Sacred Scripture furnishes are the proper and strictly-so-called principles of Theology.

Entirely the same, *mutatis mutandis*, must be said concerning the simply first principle of forensic Jurisprudence. It is beyond doubt that forensic Jurisprudence argues *only from authority*, if this be understood of the absolutely first principle of civil Jurisprudence — which at the same time, in another sense, is most rightly called the *ultimately simplest* principle: yet nothing hinders that this ultimate principle be rightly resolved into the most well-known axiom: ***It is impossible for the same thing simultaneously to be and not to be***. For are not these contradictory — *to be sanctioned and approved by civil law*, and *yet not to have the effect of civil law*? To be at once *sanctioned and approved by civil law*, and *not to be so*, is contradictory.

**§ 48.** Let them, then, who can and will judge, judge whether a man can pronounce — saving truth, and freed from either affected and gross ignorance or malice — who did not blush to write: *"That all metaphysical doctrine, all distinctions and terms metaphysical, and above all those distinctions and terms as they are handed down from Stahl, the Professor of Morals is ignorant of — is no more reason to blush than it is for a courtier not to smell of a goat." Item: "In the whole metaphysical science the chief thing is that he who knows that science alone knows nothing."* As to the first: all metaphysical propositions which enunciate something of every being, if they be restricted to moral persons, actions, and things — likewise to persons, actions, and things that are of civil society — in a way become and remain proper to Moral and Civil Philosophy, and to the discipline of the Law of Nature and of Nations; since the Professor of Ethics and Politics, of Natural Law and the Law of Nations (if he wish to discharge his office rightly, as he needs must) uses in this way the *κοινὰς ἀρχάς* — *common principles* — as Aristotle is wont to speak. Nay, all those things which we have just set out concerning the use of Metaphysics in Theology and Jurisprudence are to be drawn with equal, if not greater, right to Ethics, Politics, and the doctrine of Natural Law and the Law of Nations. For *far more easily* will the use of the metaphysical study be persuaded to students of Philosophy in Morals and Politics (above all in Morals, since this is the doctrine of law absolutely immutable) than the use of Metaphysics in Theology and Jurisprudence will be persuaded to the cultivators of Theology and Jurisprudence. — As to what was asserted in the last place, *that the chief thing in the whole metaphysical science is that he who knows it alone knows nothing* — let others see whether here imprudence rather than impudence must be charged. For here is confounded, in a shameful way, the office of the Professor of Metaphysics (which was indicated above at § 41, p. 27, &c., and which belongs to him in so far as he is a *sapiens* — for in what sense Metaphysics, and only Metaphysics, is wisdom, is clear from §§ 19 and following; in this sense also the Metaphysician, and only he, is a *sapiens*) with the office which he discharges in so far as he is *sciens*, precisely considered — as he is, no less than the Doctors of the other theoretical disciplines. For we have said that Metaphysics is both wisdom and science. By the force of this latter office it belongs to the Doctor of Metaphysics to *demonstrate*, that is — as we define it — syllogistically to deduce the necessary certitude of the propositions set forth, from certain principles as from their causes, to be known with no doubt. Of what kind, moreover, those propositions must be through which Metaphysical demonstrations are made, can and ought to be clear from the Analytic. For what is sought by demonstrations is a *certain, limpid cognition, immune from the fear of error*. Whence, if to a suitable Doctor of Metaphysics there are at hand propositions *truly* truly so, not out of mere concession or hypothesis, but *first* also — such namely that they need no further proof, but by their own evidence merit assent; or at least such that at last they can be reduced to some *first truth*, if the Metaphysician continues the argumentations from a demonstrable proposition up to that point until some first principle is reached; and *immediate*, that is, without interruption or hiatus flowing continuously from themselves; and finally *causes of the conclusion*, so that they contain the reason why in the demonstrable proposition the predicate necessarily belongs to the subject — if, I say, propositions of this kind, *true, first, immediate, and causes of the conclusion*, are at hand to the Doctor of Metaphysics in sufficient number, through which first in his own and afterwards in the minds of others he has been able and still is able to generate the certain, limpid cognition immune from the fear of error — by what colour or pretext can it be said that *he who knows Metaphysics, in so far as he knows it, knows absolutely nothing*? — Next, by the consent of all the most learned Philosophers, whatsoever belongs to the theoretical sciences and disciplines has this in common, that it is to be sought for its own sake and so for the sake of the very knowing — why then would the same not have to be said of the metaphysical science and discipline? — Thirdly, so far is it that the metaphysical science is not to be sought for its own sake and so for the knowing's sake, that rather it stands most certainly, which every illustrious Philosopher has pronounced of Metaphysics alone (and which in Aristotle's judgment is one of the most accurate specific marks of Wisdom or of First Philosophy, which men now call Metaphysics), namely that *it is to be sought maximally for its own sake and so for the sake of the very knowing*. This genuine condition of Metaphysics is more at length explained and demonstrated by Aristotle at *Metaphysics* Book I, c. 2 (which whole chapter, together with the preceding, deserves earnest inspection). The words of the Philosopher are these: *"To know and to know for the sake of knowing itself is most seen in that science which is conversant about the most knowable. He who chooses science for the sake of knowing will embrace most of all that which is most worthy of the name of science; and such is that which is conversant about that thing which is most apt to be known. And most apt to be known are the things which are first and the causes, since by these and from these the rest are known..."* Most truly spoken: for does not Metaphysics expressly claim for herself the whole of *Pneumatics*, as it is called — that is, the doctrine of Spirit, God, angels, and souls, which are of spiritual essence, most of all as these are considered in the separated state? But what nobler or equally noble objects, whose science is to be sought equally or more for its own sake, could be handed down or imagined? — Fourthly, even if (which however we believe to be asserted not only contrary to Aristotle's Philosophy but to truth itself) *Pneumatics* did not pertain directly and *per se* to Metaphysics, yet many other and more things occur in the Metaphysical discipline rightly treated which can be derived from certain principles so that no further room for doubt remains, and the whole Metaphysical science — even setting apart its most noble and most eminent part, which treats of Spirit, God, angels, and the separated soul — rests on such foundations that from them genuine demonstrations, apt to beget solid science, can be deduced. If therefore the chief thing in the Metaphysical science were that *he who knows it alone knows nothing*, this must be said equally, nay far more, of all other theoretical sciences, Physics and the Mathematical Sciences: for Metaphysics is the most exact and the most accurate of all the natural sciences, since it is maximally the science of the *first things*. Rightly says Aristotle (to demonstrate this of Metaphysics, *loc. cit.*): *"Of the sciences, those are the most accurate which are conversant with first things. For those which conclude the thing from fewer are more accurate than those which conclude it with addition — as Arithmetic than Geometry. And Metaphysics is also more apt for teaching, which is more occupied in the consideration of causes. For those teach, who adduce the cause of each thing."* Lastly, we proceed thus: Either Metaphysics as a whole is uniquely and solely for the sake of the other sciences and disciplines, or not at all. If not at all, it follows of itself that he who knows that science alone does know something. If so, then by the greatest ignorance — if not by malice — Metaphysics would be exposed to the mockeries of ignoramuses. For otherwise the same fate would await Analytic, concerning which it has been sufficiently established that she is nothing with all the sciences and disciplines abolished, nor could she be anything if there were no place for sciences and disciplines. But for all that, must we proceed with level foot to the sciences and disciplines themselves without the help and benefit of Analytic — because, with the sciences and disciplines abolished, to wish to know her alone (which indeed we grant) is to wish to know nothing? We must learn Analytic, both because it is the *common propaedeutic* (as noted at § 5, p. 3) without which it is impossible, or certainly most difficult, to get sciences, and because it makes us apt for dealing with auditors, so that what we ourselves have solidly, limpidly, and without any fear of error come to know, we may (let it be said so) *scientifically* transfuse into the auditor also, recalling everything to the Analytic rules. The same would be the judgment of Metaphysics, if we said that a good and most ample part of her — like Analytic — passes over so many times into another science or discipline, as often as to that other it is fitly, learnedly, and becomingly applied; perhaps we would have sinned in nothing. For this sentiment the Greeks most keenly championed; and the same, as being most congruent to truth itself and to the mind of Aristotle, has been embraced also by the most accurate Philosopher of the University of Altdorf, **Philipp Scherb**, and by those who succeeded him in order — the great-named Philosophers **Ernst Soner**, **Michael Piccart**, and **Johannes Kobius**. — But these things in passing, and *ἐκ παρόδῳ* [by way of a parenthesis], let be said. Rather we press on to the matter itself, since we are now setting about to hand down, with the good God's help, the very foundations of this discipline — to which when it has been happily completed we have resolved to add our ***Apparatus Metaphysicus***, that is, a brief disquisition on the suitable and necessary means and aids by which those students must be equipped who in the University would carry off the fruit of metaphysical studies; where there will likewise be opportunity of inspecting *which Commenders, which Momi and Detractors Wisdom (i.e. Metaphysics) has had and now has*. Meantime we leave that man to his own insipid wisdom — a man who thinks so abjectly of wisdom.

**§ 49.** Only this remains to be said concerning the **general use of Metaphysics**. The **special use** we are ready to show in the very progress of this our labour. One thing only may it please me to note in advance: namely, we readily grant that the *posterior part* of Metaphysics, as we defined it in § 36, p. 23, does not promise an equally great use to the cultivators of the other disciplines; yet of great moment is its use in *revealed Theology* and in *Morals*. For to the right perception and demonstration of the duty of *piety* and of the whole *divine cult*, *Natural Theology* contributes much — Natural Theology being an eminent and most noble part, into which, along with others, the *posterior part* is subdivided. For how could it be sufficiently taught or learnt in Morals that *God is to be worshipped above all things*, unless it stood most solidly from Metaphysics that *God is Being of beings, highest and most perfect being, from no other being, but the cause of all other beings*? How could it be demonstrated in Morals that *God alone is to be worshipped above all things*, unless by Metaphysical reason it could be most certainly demonstrated that *God is one in essence*? How could it be inferred from a solid Moral principle that *God is to be worshipped with spiritual worship, and the best mind is the fairest worship of God*, unless it were clear from Metaphysics that *God is spirit*? How could it be deduced by genuine Moral reasons that *God is always and everywhere to be worshipped*, unless from Metaphysics the *eternity* and *omnipresence* of God were demonstrable? And so forth. For altogether twofold is Natural Theology: one *theoretic*, which is the part of First Philosophy or Metaphysics; the other *practical*, which is the part of Moral Philosophy. Of which matter it will be treated *ex professo* in its own place, namely in the posterior part of these Institutes. Meanwhile every man can easily reach the special use which the *posterior part* furnishes in *revealed Theology*, if we would avoid the *faulty leap from genus to genus*, and so take care lest we confound *Natural Theology* with *Revealed Theology* — which confusion is wholly at war with the office of a good cultivator or master of Metaphysics.

**§ 50.** These things, then, be said of the nature, constitution, and use of Metaphysics. More things which could be said of these matters — likewise, *which published writings are most apt for the obtaining of a metaphysical erudition* — the promised ***APPARATUS METAPHYSICUS***, at the close of these Institutes, will perhaps indicate then with better fruit.

# Caput I. Of Being and Non-Being, with the various distinctions of each, and of Being by Accident

### *De Ente et Non Ente, variisque utriusque distinctionibus. Item De Ente per Accidens.*

---

**§ 1.** ***Being*** (*Ens*) is said to be ***that which truly exists***. It is also wont to be delineated thus: *that which, with no one thinking of it, exists* — i.e. *that which exists, even though no one think that it exist*.

> **Notes.**
>
> **I.** In vain do you ask for a *definition* of being, since being, by reason of its utmost commonality, cannot be defined; but what has just now been said can suffice in the place of a delineation.
>
> **II.** When it was said *with no one thinking of it*, this must be understood of the thinking of a *created intellect*: for otherwise it cannot be denied that *nothing exists unless God know that it exists*; yet in our mode of knowing it is first that something — e.g. God, sky, earth — exist, and secondly that it is known by God to exist.
>
> **III.** If *being* is taken as it was just now delineated, then it is common to one — nay, *most common to one*, that is, must be referred to the first class, viz. of those which closely approach the univocals; and yet there is no doubt but that in another sense *being* can, with saving truth, be referred to the second class, viz. of those which more closely approach the equivocals: both are agreeable to Aristotle's doctrine.

**§ 2.** ***The opposite of being is Non-being*** (*Non ens*); and that is called *non-being* which truly does not exist.

> **Notes.**
>
> **I.** If that is *being* which truly exists, then by force of opposition that will be *non-being* which actually does not exist.
>
> **II.** An *immediate* and the *greatest of all* opposition is given between *being* and *non-being*.
>
> **III.** Not a few call the *being of reason* (*ens rationis*) the opposite of being; but it must be noted (1) that *being of reason* is not opposed to being *immediately*, but *mediately* — namely by the mediation of non-being, since *being of reason* is contained under *non-being* as the narrower under the wider; (2) that many things are neither *real beings* nor *beings of reason*: e.g. the *rose in winter* is not a being (since it truly does not exist), yet neither is it a being of reason, since (as will be noted hereafter) a being of reason is *an impossible something*, which cannot be said of the rose in winter, since it is contained in the number of *possibles* — for it is a *being in potency*, and every being in potency is a *possible being*.

**§ 3.** Without any delay we proceed to the chief distinctions of *being* and *non-being*. In the Schools of the Metaphysicians distinction is made between ***possible being*** and ***actual being***: the former by the Scholastics was wont to be called *Nominal* being (*ens Nominale*), the latter *Participial* being (*ens Participiale*). ***Possible being*** is called *that which can exist*; ***actual being*** is called *that which exists*. But let it be well noted that this distinction cannot be held by the accurate Philosopher as a true *division of being*: the reason is that *possible being as such is not true being*.

> **Notes.**
>
> **I.** Deliberately it is said *possible being as such is not true being*: for we do not deny that a possible being *can be* a true being — but not *as such*. For every true being is a possible being, but not every possible being is a true being; for by this that something is a true being, namely that it exists: it is not however that all that can exist, forthwith exists.
>
> **II.** So, if any should argue thus: *The sky is a true being. The sky is a possible being. Therefore, some possible being is a true being* — we grant the whole argument, if the conclusion be taken in the material or divided sense; but if it be understood in the formal or composite sense, then the conclusion comes out most false. Briefly: the *fallacia compositionis* is committed.
>
> **III.** Hence the distinction just adduced must by no means be confounded with the distinction immediately following, as will appear from the reasons just about to be given.

**§ 4.** It is also commonly wont (2) to be distinguished between ***being in act*** (*ens actu*) and ***being in potency*** (*ens potentia*); but any estimator of the more accurate Philosophy will never sufficiently wonder why so many pass this distinction off as a true *division*. For *being in potency* is called *that which in act is not a being, yet can be*: as, *the rose in winter*. ***Being in act*** is called *that which actually exists*, or *that which is in act*.

> **Notes.**
>
> **I.** *To be in act* denotes the same as *to exist*.
>
> **II.** Thus *being in potency* and *possible being* differ: (1) *being in potency* does not extend so widely as *possible being*, since every being in potency is a possible being, but not every possible being is a being in potency: e.g. the *rose in winter*, because it is a *being in potency*, is also called a *possible being*; but the *rose in spring*, if it be in act, is a *possible being* — it can be — yet the rose in spring, when it is, is not a *being in potency*. (2) In the *divided* sense this proposition can be true: *a possible being is a true being*; but this proposition — *a being in potency is a true being* — can never be true, neither in composite nor in divided sense: for as long as something is a *being in potency*, so long it is in no way a true being. (3) As *animal* stands to *man* and *brute*, so in its own manner *possible being* stands to *being in act* and *being in potency*: for as *animal as such* notes neither *man* nor *brute*, so *possible being as such* notes neither *being in act* nor argues *being in potency*, but is abstract from both.
>
> **III.** Therefore these two are most distinct: *to be in potency* (*esse potentia*) and *to be able to be* (*esse posse*): for *to be in potency* cannot stand with *to be in act* — that is, *what is in potency, is not in act*; on the contrary, *to be able to be* and *to be in act* can stand together — that is, something can *be* and yet actually *is*; nay, every thing which is in act, *can be*: for unless it could be, it would be impossible, and if it were an impossible being, how would it be?

**§ 5.** Though the distinctions just adduced cannot be held as true divisions of being, there is no doubt but that there is place for true divisions of being also. And to note this in passing, as it is the most common, so by us also it is held as the first and immediate division of being into ***substance and accident***. There are meanwhile other and far more divisions of being, and any *disjunctive attribute* of being, as they call it, is in the judgment of the more accurate Metaphysicians nothing other than a true division of being. But of these solidly, and so far as the reason of our institute requires, it will be treated in its own place.

**§ 6.** It does our matter not a little good if we also elicit the ***division of non-being***. The most commodious division of non-being is this: *non-being is either possible or impossible*. ***Possible non-being*** we call *that which in act is not — i.e. does not exist — yet can exist*: e.g. the *rose in winter*, a *mountain of gold* (for why by the absolute power of God could a mountain of gold not exist?). ***Impossible non-being*** is called *that which in act is not, that is, does not exist, so that neither can it be or exist*: e.g. *blindness* (for neither does blindness exist, nor can it exist), a *square circle*.

> **Notes.**
>
> **I.** Sometimes *possible non-being* is wont to be called absolutely *non-being*; *impossible non-being* is also called *impossibile in essendo*.
>
> **II.** *Possible non-being* with the more accurate Philosophers is the same as *being in potency*. For these do not hold *being in potency* as a true being: *it is not a being; therefore it is a non-being*, since from a finite negative proposition the consequence always holds to the infinite affirmative on the side of the predicate, provided the predicate consists of a single word.

**§ 7.** To ***non-beings*** belong *beings in potency*, *negations* (and *pure privations*), *impossibles*, *beings of reason*. In some manner also *beings by accident* (*entia per accidens*) can be referred hither, and in some sense they are so referred by the more accurate Metaphysicians.

> **Notes.**
>
> **I.** It becomes the cultivator of First Philosophy altogether to know these falsities, lest he at once stumble on the threshold of metaphysical erudition. And truly, whoever rightly attends to this paragraph will — without further leading by the hand — easily grasp that *the distinction of being into real being and being of reason can by no means be called immediate*: for many things are given as *middle* between real being and being of reason — namely, all those non-beings which, besides *beings of reason*, were by this very fact enumerated.
>
> **II.** On the less experienced sometimes this very dictum has imposed, which is commonly said: *Real being exists apart from the operation of the mind, i.e. apart from being objectively in the intellect. Being of reason does not exist apart from the operation of the mind, i.e. apart from being objectively in the intellect.* But these propositions, if the terms be taken as they lie, do not yet certainly suffice to understand rightly the difference of being of reason from real being: for of every non-being whatsoever it must be said that it does not exist apart from the operation of the mind.

**§ 8.** That ***beings in potency are non-beings*** follows from this, that in act they are not beings. Rightly and finely says **Aristotle**: *τὰ δυνάμει ὄντα, οὐκ ἔστιν, ἐπεὶ ἐνεργείᾳ οὐκ ἔστιν* — *"beings in potency are not, since in act they are not."* To this point tends Aristotle's mind: that *to be in act* is the same as *to exist*, and that nothing is a true being unless it exist: e.g. non-beings are *the rose in winter*, *snow in summer*, since they do not exist. For as *I am* signifies the same as *I exist*, not the same as *I can*, so *being* is the same as *existing*.

> **Notes.**
>
> **I.** The matter can be most easily perceived in this way: As he who *can be* learned is not forthwith learned, and he who *can have* a wife does not on that account forthwith have one, so what *can be* a being is not forthwith a being. In each case the reason is equal, as any man sees who is yet free from preconceived or vulgar opinion.
>
> **II.** This indeed we do not deny: that **Aristotle** in various places calls *being in potency* a *true being*. But there he does not treat of being in potency simply so-called — i.e. of that which plainly is not, though it could be — but of *being in potency secundum quid*, that is, of that which absolutely speaking is a being, yet in a certain mode is a non-being, since it is not such a being as from it can be made. For example: Aristotle calls bronze a *being in potency* — not because bronze plainly is not, but on this ground, that it is a *statue in potency*, since out of bronze a statue can be made.
>
> **III.** We could also have said of *the possible as such* that it is not a being: the reason is easily learned from what precedes — for the possible as such is not an *existent*, but *in so far as it is at the same time said* (i.e. in act). Meanwhile, since the possible can be a true being, though not as such, we therefore leave *the possible* as really abstract from being and non-being.

**§ 9.** That ***negations are non-beings***, no man of sound judgment will doubt, so that no proof whatever is needed: for a *pure negation* speaks no true existence. The like judgment be made of ***privations***, if they be considered *formally* — that is, on the side of that which they signify in the straight case: e.g. when it is asked whether *blindness is given in that man who, saving truth, is called blind*, the answer must altogether be that *blindness does not exist*; yet, without loss of truth, it is said that *blindness is given in that man*, and this through the fact that *the opposite of that whose absence is called blindness is not in the man*. For whether you say *sight is not in the man*, or *sight is a non-being in the man*, or *blindness is given in the man*, you pronounce entirely the same. So through this: *darkness is given in the air, because light does not exist in the air*. The same judgment must be made of all privations.

> **Notes.**
>
> **I.** They philosophise rightly who say that *by privation a non-being is noted, yet a being is connoted*: e.g. by *blindness* non-being — the absence of sight — is noted, but a being is connoted, namely an animal; from the fact that when sight is absent from the animal, we call that absence *blindness*. Note, however, that *blindness remains a non-being, however much it connote a being*.
>
> **II.** Yet *privation* and *the privative* must not be confounded between themselves. For just as *whiteness is not white*, but whiteness differs from the white as *abstract from concrete*, so *privation is not a privative something*; whence although *the privative* is a true being, yet it is not a true being if it be considered as speaking the privation; but if you consider the *subject* to which privation is attributed, e.g. the *man* to whom blindness, saving truth, is attributed.
>
> **III.** Lest the Scholastics confound privation with true being, they willed to call no privation *inherent* in a subject, but *adherent*: hence it came about that *inhaesio* was attributed to a real being, namely to a predicamental accident, but *adhaesio* to a privation.
>
> **IV.** *Privations are neither real beings nor beings of reason*, if they be considered in themselves and precisively, as will be seen in what follows.

**§ 10.** That the ***impossible is not a being*** is clear from this: *because it cannot be*. But what cannot be, this cannot be a being, and so is a non-being. Certainly every true being is a possible being; therefore the impossible is not a true being, and so is a non-being.

**§ 11.** ***A being of reason is a non-being***, which, by the use of speech received among the more accurate Metaphysicians, is nothing other than *an impossible something which is nevertheless held for a possible*.

> **Notes.**
>
> **I.** *Being of reason* and *the impossible* differ as narrower and wider: for every being of reason is an impossible, but not every impossible is a being of reason. For the *impossible* requires at least two things: (1) the negation of true and real existence; (2) the negation of possible existence. More clearly: two things are required to the impossible (as it is here taken): (1) that it *not be*; (2) that it *not be able to be*. But these two do not suffice to a *being of reason*. For to that one further requisite is added: that it (that is, what is not and cannot be) be held, through error, for a true being, or at least for a possible being.
>
> **II.** That this may be better perceived, we can distinguish between *the quasi-material of a being of reason* and *the quasi-formal of a being of reason*. The *material* is *the impossible*, since every being of reason is impossible. The *formal* is contained in these words: *that which is held for a true possible being*: so, e.g., when any one considers that *blindness never exists in an animal, nor can exist in an animal*, he certainly does not form any being of reason. But let us suppose someone to be stuck in such a little error, that he supposes blindness to be some little worm actually existing in the eye of his rustic wife, and therefore seeks from a quack a medicament by which that little worm may be smothered — he at last *feigns* a being of reason.
>
> **III.** From what has been said we may at once gather that privations are then to be held for *beings of reason* when they are held for *true possible beings*: but this happens to privations. For in order rightly to conceive privation, there is no reason why you should hold it for a true being; nay, when you hold it for a true being, you falsely conceive privation — meanwhile you feign a being of reason.

**§ 12.** Of ***Being by Accident*** (*Ens per Accidens*) it remains now to speak, since this also we have reckoned (together with the best Metaphysical Doctors) can in some manner be called a *non-being*. Namely, *being by accident* is called *in quantum*, non-being — in so far as it says *more existences* than were required for *being absolutely so-called*. For example: *this white thing*, if I point with my finger to a white wall, is called *being by accident*, since besides the substance in which the whiteness inheres, it says the very *existence of the whiteness*: and the whiteness per se was not required for the substance of the wall, and the wall could be without all whiteness. So the *physical man*, i.e. man endowed with physical science, is called *being by accident*, since the *man in himself* and *per se* remains a complete being, though no physical science were in his intellect. In this sense, then, *being by accident* is called *non-being*, in so far as it says *more* than *being* — namely, it says *beings*, of which the one was not of itself ordered to the other, nor the two to the constitution of a third being as to the being *per se*, out of their own nature.

> **Notes.**
>
> **I.** *Being by accident as such* says *beings* (plural). As the singular is not the plural nor the plural the singular, so *ens* and *entia* also differ; and *entia* are not *ens*, since they are not *one* but *many*. If they are not *ens*, therefore in a sound sense they are *non-ens*, however many beings they remain.
>
> **II.** You will say: *Man says two beings — body and soul; therefore in a certain manner he is non-being — which is absurd.* It is most easily answered: body and soul are *two beings really distinct in man*, but they are ordered *per se* and out of their own nature to constitute *one being*, namely the man. You will urge back: *wall and whiteness also constitute one being, namely one white thing*. It is answered: the wall can be without whiteness, nor is whiteness ordered *per se* to constituting a being; whereas soul and body are *per se* ordered to each other — which cannot be said of wall and whiteness, since they are not ordered *per se* to each other.

**§ 13.** Of ***beings by accident*** there are, so to speak, ***three classes***. For (1) *being by accident* is given when several beings, without all order and union, are called *being by accident* on account of mere *aggregation*: e.g. several stones lying without order and union are called *one heap*; so many grains of wheat are *one pile*; so a wholly inordinate multitude of men is called *one assembly*. These beings by accident are called by the name of **beings by aggregation** (*entia per aggregationem*), when nothing is regarded except the aggregation. (2) Several beings are called *being by accident* when they are united among themselves by the mediation of an *accidental* union, as *wall and whiteness*, if they be considered with their union, are called *one white thing*, and so a *being by accident*: so a *believer in Christ* is a being by accident, by the union which comes between the man who believes in Christ and the faith itself. (3) Several beings are called *being by accident* on account of a certain ***order*** which they keep among themselves: e.g. several men keeping a certain order among themselves in a certain polity are called *one society*; so *city* and *church* are beings by accident.

> **Notes.**
>
> **I.** The *first class* of beings by accident — i.e. by mere aggregation — do not deserve more prolix consideration.
>
> **II.** Of beings by accident of the second class we shall treat *ex professo* in the doctrine of *one and union*.
>
> **III.** Of beings by accident of the third class the treatment likewise will be instituted in its own place, namely where we shall treat of *order*.

**§ 14.** The ***Synonyms*** of *being* are commonly called: *Real* (*Reale*) and *Positive* (*Positivum*).

> **Notes.**
>
> **I.** It is most true, we believe, that *Real* and *Positive* in a rigorous signification mean plainly the same as *Being*; which however notwithstanding, sometimes *real* and *positive* admit a laxer signification, so as to mean the same as *not depending on mere thinking*, though it do not exist: e.g. a *possible being* is called a *real something*, though it is not a being, in this sense: just as, e.g., an *angel* who in act is or exists has this — that he is in act or exists — not from the operation of our intellect (for the angel exists even though no one think that he exists); so also such an angel who does not yet exist is called *possible* apart from the operation of our intellect — for no one would say that an angel not yet produced has this, that he is possible, that he can be produced, from our mere thinking. Hence it is that an angel not yet produced, since he can be produced by God with no one thinking of it, is in the Schools called *really possible* and so *real possible*. So *the rose in winter* can in a sound sense be called a *real and positive something*, so as yet to not be a *real being*: for just as it does not depend on any created intellect that the rose is in act, so also it does not depend thereon that the rose can be, or that it is in potency. — So we say: *this man is really blind*, and so blindness we call a *real something* — yet not in this sense as if blindness were a real being, or as if it spoke a real essence or a part of a real essence. The sense therefore is this: *with no one thinking it that this man is blind, yet he is blind — i.e. he lacks sight, of which he would have been capable, had not something of his nature stood as an obstacle*. So the more accurate Philosophers call many *relations real*, yet those which come under the name of real relations are not therefore to be called real *beings*: e.g. between two blind men a *real relation* is given, a *real similitude* — i.e. they are like one another, with no one thinking of it — because, with no one thinking of it, each of them is blind; yet that *real similitude* has not for its foundation a real being, but a mere non-being, namely blindness. Likewise we say that many *attributes of being* are given, and single attributes of being we call *real attributes*, *item positive attributes*; yet they are not many *real and positive beings*. The same is the judgment of *the attributes of God*.
>
> **II.** By innumerable other examples this could be declared. So, those who deny the faculties of the soul to be *really* distinct among themselves call those faculties *real*, yet not real *beings*: e.g. *intellect* and *will* are real faculties of the rational soul, with this standing, that they do not differ really among themselves, nor from the rational soul; with this also standing, that the rational soul is *one per se* being, intellect and will will be *one per se* being. Let every man therefore beware lest he suffer himself to be imposed upon by these ambiguities. This is certain: *real* and *positive*, if they be taken absolutely and in the rigour, can be — nay, must be — held as synonyms of *being*.

## Axiomata

### Axioma I

> ***Impossibile est idem simul esse et non esse.***
>
> **It is impossible that the same thing simultaneously be and not be.**

#### Sense of the Axiom

The axiom has this sense: ***Between true contradictories there is given a perpetual and immutable repugnance.*** For example: *the same thing cannot simultaneously be an animal and not be an animal; simultaneously be such-and-such an animal and not be such-and-such an animal.*

#### Confirmation

Certainly, that *most absurd and first impossible* is what is expressed by the adduced axiom, since no impossible that argues a greater absurdity can be feigned or thought up by anyone, however much he wished to err; whence there will be no need to confirm this dictum. Meanwhile, in the place of confirmation, the notions by which the given axiom is made up are to be weighed, which notions once perceived it cannot come to pass that the truth of the axiom hold anyone in doubt.

***Impossible*** is called that which cannot so behave. ***The same*** is here called every *undivided* — whether, as to existence, there be an *undivided* with respect to existence (and this again is either *indivisible* or *divisible*), or whether it have no existence (and this again either can have existence or cannot). The notion of *simultaneously* respects time or duration of whatever sort, and is opposed to what is not at one and the same time, or what denies the unity of time or duration of whatever sort. ***To be*** here is the same as *to exist*, ***not to be*** the same as *not to exist*: *now to be* is the same as *to so behave*, *not to be* the same as *not to so behave*.

#### Demonstration per Absurdum

In this axiom is contained the *absurdum absurdorum absurdissimum* — that is, the *first and greatest absurd*, the greatest impossible, so that it can hardly be demonstrated *per absurdum* in words, since no more absurd proposition can be given than the one which is contradictorily opposed to the given axiom, i.e. the proposition which has itself thus: *It is not impossible that the same thing simultaneously be and not be.*

And this perhaps moved **Petrus Hurtadus de Mendoza** to judge in his *Disputationes Metaphysicae* III, sect. 21, p. 311, that *one must proceed from words to blows*. "If any shall have *denied* or shall have *distinguished* this principle," he says, "he must rather be instructed with a club than with a reason. If he complain that he is being struck, you shall answer that *not any thing is or is not*, and so, while he himself is being struck, that he is not being struck, since in his sentence *the same can simultaneously be and not be*." Others, far more rigid censors — and perhaps more detestable than the hangman — pronounce thus: "Since this wretch is most miserably beaten, and since the certitude of the given axiom is not yet established, let him therefore be placed upon the pyre, until he confess that he feels the pains and flames, and that it is impossible that he at once grow hot and not grow hot, that while he is burned he is not burned. And if he deafly concede this, he has conceded the universal truth of the given proposition: *It is impossible for the same to be and not to be at once*. For if he answer that *in the given cases*, not in others whatsoever, the truth of the axiom holds, it will be easy to place before his eyes that of all contradictories there is plainly one and the same reason — namely, that it consists in repugnance; and that as to be beaten and not to be beaten, to grow hot and not to grow hot, to be burned and not to be burned repugn to one another in turn, so also whatsoever contradictories do repugn to one another: if the same could at once be and not be, then at once the thing and its privation would be; therefore man at once dies and lives, is simultaneously generated and not generated, we are simultaneously here and not here; and this book, before it was today set in type and read, had already been set in type and read; what we are ignorant of, we know; and when we sleep, we wake; and when we labour, we rest." — But such things are plainly unknown to the *Philosopher*, who takes pains to persuade the truth to others not by blows but by words, not by the pyre but by reason as far as he may, and from the opponent's own concessions to confute those who contradict him — even those who out of *obstinacy* deny what is otherwise most evident; although this is not to be presumed concerning all who deny first principles.

**Aristotle**, in *Metaphysics* IV, c. 4, hands down ***three sorts of men*** who deny the present axiom (which is the *first* of all principles): some, he says, have done this out of *affected malice*; some *deceived by some fallacious appearance of reason*; some *through δυσμάθεια*, that is, through ignorance of the Analytic — because they presumed to demonstrate it, but could not find the demonstration, not knowing of what matter the demonstration had to be sought, and of what not. Must all these be coerced by blows, or placed upon the pyre? Not so did it seem to Aristotle, who has handed to us an excellent method of proving the simply-first principle, by which we can prove it from the very assertion of the one who contradicts, drawing our argument from his own words. Let Aristotle be looked at in the cited place, πόλλῳ (if it please). Some think this *refutation* can take place thus: *Whosoever disputes with me either admits something or nothing. If nothing, in vain is he disputed with. If something, I shall argue from the thing conceded.* Others set up rather thus: *Whether he admit something or nothing, still against him it may be disputed. For since he admits nothing, he will admit that nothing is not something (otherwise he would admit something); and the one refuting will have what he willed to evince against the one contradicting.* Yet to others this manner of proceeding pleases: *Either while the contradictor disputes with me he denies my principle, or he does not. If the latter, I have what I intended. If the former, he denies it because to him this principle is false. Therefore what he says is false, he denies to be true. Therefore he himself admits that the false is not true — which is the very exposition of the present principle. And if he says that this principle can be false, it will come to the same. For when he says it can be false, he says so because it would not always be true; therefore to-be-able-to-be-false would mean not-always-to-be-true; therefore he would differ anew from me, who was saying that it cannot be false; and since his "it can be false" is not my "it cannot be false," he will observe that the same is not simultaneously and not simultaneously.* To others still, another method of refuting is pleasing.

Most true, we believe, is this: unless this axiom were of perpetual, infallible, and immutable truth, in vain would Theologians and Jurisconsults sweat in investigating the sense of Sacred Scripture and of the civil laws, in reconciling texts; in vain would any disputation be instituted — since to dispute is (as commonly said) to be engaged in contradictory terms, and to presuppose the immutable truth of the given axiom. In vain would any man learn anything he learned, in vain teach anything he taught: any heresies, any absurdities, any trifles, dullnesses, dreams, deliria would have to be admitted without discrimination.

#### Practical Use

The *amplest* use of this axiom is in all disciplines and faculties whatsoever, and in all their parts. For, as was just now said, every solid doctrine can and ought at last to be resolved into this axiom. All axioms also rely on its truth. Two illustrious examples we have adduced in the ***Caput Praeliminare*** itself, § 47 and following (from p. 35 to p. 39), which we are unwilling to retranscribe here anew; yet it will be easy for anyone, from their inspection, to gather the most present use of the axiom.

We believe no one lives so without eyes, so without mind, that, looking at and understanding the passage of Sacred Scripture cited above at p. 37, he will not openly see and abundantly recognise that *the Holy Spirit Himself willed to found, and in fact founded, His own assertions on this our axiom*: ***It is impossible for the same thing simultaneously to be and not to be.***

Nor is there anything to move any one in that doubt — as if, with our assertion standing, *revealed Theology* were not a supernatural habit, if of its *first principle*, into which all theological doctrine at last can be resolved, it be lawful to say that it is a *natural* principle, namely this ours: *It is impossible for the same thing simultaneously to be and not to be*. For the single distinction given above, and here to be repeated, most easily removes every difficulty: namely, there must be distinguished between the ***first principle proper to Theology*** and the ***first principle common to Theology with the other sciences and disciplines***. The same we have asserted of Jurisprudence, and of all disciplines, chiefly the practical. Let what was noted in *Caput Praeliminare* §§ 41–50 be repeated.

#### Practical Abuse

Here, in the first place, are to be noted the modern ***Fanatici*** — those who judge that no man can congratulate himself upon a solid science of any thing whatsoever, *unless he first doubt concerning all things which can fall under knowledge, and strive to consign to oblivion all those things which through so many years of academic life he has learned; and that this must be done because any one who is born an infant, and has pronounced various judgments concerning sensible things before he had the whole use of reason, so long as the mind is not freed from so many and so great prejudices by which it is turned away from the knowledge of truth, must despair of solid erudition — since, however, he cannot evade free of them, unless once in life he doubt of all things, and hold as false even those things of which he doubts, so that so much more clearly whatever is most certain and easiest to know, he may discover.*

The same men bid us doubt also of those principles *which hitherto have been held by all men of sounder judgment as naturally first and most certain, and which hitherto we have reckoned to be known per se — the first of which is this dictum:* ***It is impossible for the same thing simultaneously to be and not to be***; *because we have at some time seen that some have erred in such matters, and have admitted certain things as most certain and known per se which to us seemed false; but above all because we have heard that there is a God who can all things, by whom we have been created — and yet we are ignorant whether perhaps God may have willed to create us such that we are always deceived even in those things which seem to us most known; because it does not less seem possible to have happened than that we are sometimes deceived; and so much the more, if we feign ourselves to have arisen out of the earth like mushrooms — or at length to have fallen into this world from whencesoever.*

And truly this ***Sceptical reason of doubting*** has not stopped in Theoretical Philosophy, but to Moral Philosophy also, and — what is most to be grieved — to Theology itself have the patrons of so many and such great trifles translated it: men to whom all things of old are foul, and who do not blush to give counsel, by public and written word, to students, *not to read any writings of learned men hitherto held as approved, nor to hear such men commending or explaining them; but to live most certain that those who learn as little as possible of all those things which hitherto (that is, before these new Philosophers had come forth and communicated their wisdom to the literary world) have been wont to be signified by the name of Philosophy, are most apt for the true perception of truth — especially if they hold Aristotle's Philosophy as such; whereas it is likely that not even Aristotle himself held it as true, and that he obtruded his principles as true and right, however much he himself had never held them as such; whereas they count the Scholastic disputations as such from which no truth hitherto unknown has ever been brought forth into the light.*

Certainly, of ***unteachability*** (ἀμαθία) there are some degrees — this one is more harmful than that; yet no other more pestilential than this new and perverse method of teaching and learning can be found, as all those who have the studies and emoluments of Youth at heart most certainly believe. For learners who are not yet strong in either talent or erudition, when the common principles are *called into doubt*, indulging in these thoughts, and taking the censor's rod against the received sentences of most learned men and the common manner and custom of teaching, cannot but fall into dull and absurd dogmas, bring new opinions resting on no foundations into the literate world — and, while they stretch them even to ***Theology*** itself, this new method cannot but open gates wide ***to various fictions, and to the overthrow of what they call Christianity itself***. For if once it has been persuaded to the minds of mankind: *It is uncertain whether those things which hitherto have been held by all men of sounder judgment for most common and most certain principles are true; it is uncertain whether God is; and if He is, whether perhaps He has so made us that in those things which we have believed most certain, we are always deceived* — nothing can befall any estimator of true erudition more pleasing or more healthful than that he consign to oblivion all those things which through twenty and more years, according to the received and common method, he has learned and taught: so that, with nature in no way prone to the evil, they will reckon they can defend whatever comes into their mouths; and dwelling and dying in their vain speculations and in futile novelties, they will — by understanding — make themselves understand nothing. For neither will they themselves understand others, nor will they be understood by others.

And let so much have been said of the practical abuse of this axiom in common. The occasion of this matter was supplied to us by the inspection of several Metaphysicians, who judge that there is no need at all for the confirmation of this axiom, resting on the persuasion that *no one can be found who has ever doubted or will doubt of the truth of this axiom*. Thus **Mendoza**, toward the end of his cited disputation, writes: *"Aristotle in the fourth book of the Metaphysics ex instituto proves this principle, which however I judge has never been denied by any human. But Aristotle in his usual manner has cast this from his own brain, so that he might take occasion to confirm it."* But it would be easy to demonstrate from the history of Philosophy that even in Aristotle's time, and before, ***Sceptics*** existed, from whose trifles — as from ashes — the said modern innovators resuscitate their novelties.

#### The First Worked Example — The Opponent on Moral Acts

For the rest, a shameful abuse of this axiom is also committed by those disputants who feign contradictions in others where there are none. Chief among such are those who declare common and received opinions to be of such sort *than which nothing more foolish could be feigned, and from which horrible absurdities follow* — so that they afterwards have need to subscribe to those very opinions which they had so eloquently derided. That those to whom the faculty or the time for a diligent and attentive inspection of their writings is lacking may not notice this, they contrive vain refuges in the acceptations of terms, and impose their colouring upon incautious readers, or upon admirers of them who are ignorant of the very *status controversiae*. Let us take one or two examples of this matter.

**The common view.** So common — and received not only by the Philosophers of every age (if we except the Sceptics) but by the Theologians and Jurisconsults also, by unanimous consent — is this sentence: *that voluntary acts are given which can be regarded as moral (that is, honest or base) apart from reference to law* (law being here taken, as it ought to be, for the *decree of a superior, by which he binds the subject to form his actions after that superior's prescript*).

**The opponent's syllogism.** This very sentence a certain one, who credits himself alone with wisdom, wishing to attack, makes use of the following argument:

> **Major.** Whatever is not morally indifferent, this speaks a reference to the law.
> **Minor.** But *some physical motion in human action, considered apart from all reference to law, is not morally indifferent*.
> **Conclusion.** Therefore some physical motion in human action, considered apart from all reference to law, speaks a reference to the law.

**The alleged contradiction.** The conclusion, says the opponent, manifestly fights with our given axiom, *It is impossible for the same thing simultaneously to be and not to be*: for a most manifest contradiction is implicated when it is said *physical motion in human action considered apart from all reference to law speaks a reference to the law*; for it implicates, *apart from reference to law, to agree with law, or not to agree*. Can anything, apart from reference to law, agree with the law?

**Where the falsity lies.** Since the form of the adduced syllogism is good, it must be that at least one of the premises is false. Now the ***Major*** seems manifest and set beyond doubt — *for honesty and baseness are moral affections or qualities of human actions arising from agreement or disagreement with law; and if any shall attack this thesis, the soothsayers will announce a prodigy, that an ox has spoken* — therefore morality says nothing other than reference to law. Rather, so long as the human race has been, bare physical motion has not been a human and moral action, but only its material: and to a *moral* action, besides the physical motion, there is required also an *aptitude for imputation* and *reference to law*. Therefore the ***Minor*** will be false — the minor which has itself thus: *some physical motion in human action considered apart from all reference to law is not morally indifferent*. The contradictorily-opposed thesis to this minor will be true, and so most true will remain this proposition: *every physical motion in human action considered apart from all reference to law is morally indifferent*. And with this proposition standing, falls the common sentence concerning voluntary acts, which — apart from reference to law — can be regarded as moral, honest or base.

**Three responses.** But that it may appear to every man of sounder judgment how far it is from being the case that by this argument the received sentence is reduced to a first and greatest absurd — rather, that from it the greatest absurdity of the adduced argumentation itself shines forth — we shall note three things by way of reply:

**First.** Those who teach that voluntary acts, which apart from reference to law can be regarded as moral — honest or base — speak not merely of the voluntary acts, honest or base, of ***the rational creature***, but also of the voluntary acts, honest or base, of ***God***. More clearly: those who defend this thesis — *there are given voluntary acts which, apart from reference to law* (law being taken for the decree of a superior by which he binds a subject) *can be regarded as moral, that is, honest or base*, with no mention of God or of a rational creature being made — these men abstract their actions from the divine and from the human, and morality itself from the divine and from the human.

Now any man, free from passions, however blind, sees that *voluntary acts, as at once honest or base*, are to God in common with rational creatures — as also as to God proper — and can be regarded as moral (that is, honest or base) apart from reference to law, so far as law notes only *the decree by which a superior binds a subject to form his actions after that superior's prescript*: for so law in general (N.B. *in general*) does the patron of the contrary sentence himself define, and judges that it cannot be more commodiously defined. The *Major* of the adduced argumentation therefore falls — the Major that had itself thus: *Whatever is not morally indifferent, this speaks a reference to law* — especially since the very patron of the adverse part says: *"We admit that there are given acts per se and by their nature honest or base, if mention be added of the subject to which these are said to be in their own nature honest: e.g. acts are given which per se do not agree with God — that is, which, saving His perfection, cannot without contradiction fall into Him."* For how can acts which per se agree or disagree with God — and in this sense are called just or unjust, in one word *moral*, and can by no means be called morally indifferent — speak a reference *to the decree of a superior, by which that superior binds a subject to form his actions after His prescript*? Shall God have Himself a superior to whose prescript God, as a subject, must form His actions? Or shall God be called superior to Himself, or subject to Himself? Absurd, most absurd absurdities.

Let the same judgment be made of acts which per se agree or disagree with any rational agent (not regarding whether that agent be increated — that is, God — or created). These acts certainly cannot be reckoned morally indifferent; yet neither as such, considered as abstract from the actions of God and of man, can they speak a *real reference* to the decree of a superior who binds a subject, unless you would feign some one superior equally to God and to men. — To render this response void, in vain the contradictor shall say: (1) *"He who so philosophises places the actions of God and of men in the same class, and so is injurious to the divine Majesty."* Rather, those are injurious to Majesty who distinguish between independent being and dependent, immaterial and material substance, increated and created spirit, divine and human wisdom; and why between the *perfection* of God and of men, the *justice* of God and of men, acts *per se and by their nature agreeing or disagreeing with God*, and acts *per se and by their nature agreeing or disagreeing with man*, is distinction *not* made? (2) *"Actions per se and by their nature agreeing with God are to be defined by those sages of Atlantis who hide their head among the clouds."* But this is to pour forth insults on *Natural Theology*, both theoretical and most of all practical. — For the rest, to be noted is this: that *acts per se and by their nature agreeing with any rational agent, just, honest; likewise wisdom, justice, common to God with men* — these are most rightly established and defended; provided a man knows how to distinguish, from the formal concepts themselves, the material concepts which belong to the same. For example: in man we recognise no *wisdom* unless it be an accident really distinct from the man; the wise man among men we are wont to call him in whom, as in a subject, wisdom really inheres. And yet, though in this manner no one, saving truth, conceives the wisdom of God, it still does not follow that *wisdom properly so-called* is in no way common to God with man, if its *formal concept* — which is the *science of the most eminent things*, i.e. *wisdom* as it is abstract from the divine and the created — be regarded. Let the same, *mutatis mutandis*, be the judgment of other things which just now have been reckoned common to God with men.

**Second.** Even if we stand on *their* side, so that at least the discourse be of acts honest or base to man per se and by his nature, still the syllogism by which the opponent attacks the sentence concerning voluntary human acts (as if they could be regarded as moral — honest or base — apart from reference to law) will be ***vicious in form***. The syllogism in form was such:

> *Whatever is not morally indifferent, this speaks a reference to the law. But some physical motion in human action considered apart from all reference to law is not morally indifferent. Therefore some physical motion in human action considered apart from all reference to law speaks a reference to the law.*

We shall form a syllogism of similar form:

> *Whatever is not uneducable, this speaks a reference to the act of teaching. But some man, considered apart from all reference to the act of teaching, is not uneducable. Therefore some man, considered apart from all reference to the act of teaching, speaks a reference to the act of teaching.*

One egg is not more like another egg than the posterior syllogism to the prior. **A different mode of considering does not take away the thing itself:** e.g. while we consider a man apart from all reference to the act of teaching, apart from the act of laughing, precisely as he is rational — the man meanwhile does not cease to be *teachable, risible*. Therefore the ***minor*** of the posterior syllogism is most true; nor would any one of sound forehead doubt of the *major's* truth: yet the conclusion remains most false, if it be understood thus: *some man, apart from all reference to the act of teaching, in so far as he is so considered, is considered as speaking a reference to the act of teaching*. Therefore the form of the syllogism is vicious. But if this be the sense of the conclusion — *some man, however he be not considered as speaking a reference to the act of teaching, nevertheless speaks a real reference to the act of teaching* — so the conclusion is most true, and no less most true remains the conclusion of the prior argument: *some physical motion in human action considered apart from all reference to law speaks a reference to law* — that is, *though no one of us think that physical motion in human action speaks a reference to law, nevertheless some physical motion in human action, considered apart from reference to law, speaks a true and real reference to law*. For those who defend the common and received sentence most readily grant, and have never denied, that any human act whatsoever which they call per se and by its nature honest or base ***is*** by law (even taking law as the mere decree of a superior) *commanded* or *forbidden*. Indeed, they most willingly deny that an act *per se and by its nature* honest or base can be given which would not be the sort of act to be committed or omitted by man from the decree of a superior — that is, prescribed or prohibited by law taken as the superior's decree.

By the consent, therefore, of all against whom this disputation is being so sharply and so impudently waged, every human act *perse and by its nature honest or base* speaks a perpetual and real relation of this sort to the law, so far as it argues the decree of a superior. He who should deny this would deny that he is a man — that is, an animal who has received a rational and social nature from the assignment and the gift of the Creator, because so it has seemed to the Wisdom and Goodness of the Supreme Deity. And he who denied this would plainly contradict himself. **But who are those who have denied?** To attribute to others an error through gross injustice, when one could have passed over the men, is not the mark of an *ingenuous* disputant. ***It is asserted*** by the opponent that *not only Grotius, but most of our native Theologians and Jurisconsults have defended this absurdity*. Yet every lover of truth knows that a manifest injury is done to them.

#### Response 3 — The Four Modes of Taking the Law of Nature

That this may stand more evidently, in the ***third*** place we observe that the ***law of nature*** is taken by all the more accurate Philosophers, Theologians, and Jurisconsults most frequently for ***the practical judgment of the intellect concerning acts per se and by their nature agreeing or disagreeing with rational nature***. Which judgment can be taken in a fourfold mode:

1. **As abstract** from the judgment of the increated and created intellect, divine and human.
2. **As common** to God with man.
3. **As God's** proper.
4. **As man's** proper.

In whichever manner at last it be taken, this remains most certain: that judgment — in our mode of conceiving, which is nevertheless solidly founded in the very truth of the thing — is **prior to the decree of will** (whether abstract from divine and created, or as God's, or as man's); and that judgment is not merely arbitrary but ***depends on the nature of its objects***.

**Example 1 — the love of God.** Of God, neither a rational creature nor God Himself can, saving truth, judge otherwise than ***that He is to be loved above all things, both by Himself and by every rational creature, because He is supremely and infinitely good***. And this practical judgment of the intellect, by which both God judges and we judge that God is to be loved above all things, is called ***the law of God common to men***.

**Example 2 — the keeping of promises.** Again, concerning a promise, neither a rational creature can, saving truth, nor God Himself judge otherwise than that ***a promise is to be kept***, whether it be made by God or by a rational creature; because whoever promises, be it God or be it creature (and no creature can promise except an intelligent one), renounces the right of changing his will concerning that which he promised. And this practical judgment of the intellect, by which both God judges and we judge that a promise is to be kept, is called ***the law of God common to men***.

**Example 3 — the forbidding of adultery.** Likewise concerning man, i.e. the rational and social animal: neither man nor God can judge otherwise, saving truth, than that ***neither can man, saving the perfection of his rational nature, will to concubit with another's wife whom he knows to be another's, nor can God move man's will to concubit with her***. And this practical judgment of the intellect is again called ***the law of God common to men***.

**The principle.** If, then, no intelligent being can judge otherwise — nay, cannot even will to judge otherwise — and by the consent of all sounder minds the ***truth of judgment depends on the truth of the object***, so that this is by nature prior and that by nature posterior, who, pray — not with a mind of railing but of recognising truth — can accuse the received and common sentence of foolishness or of horrible absurdities, if it be explained thus: ***There are given practical judgments of the human intellect, of the divine intellect, common to increated and created intellect, concerning acts per se and by their nature agreeing or disagreeing with rational nature, which, saving truth, neither God nor man can otherwise form, and the judgments contrary to which are necessarily most false and most iniquitous***? Therefore, in the sense in which — by the suffrages of all who refuse to be gnawers and detractors of philosophy and are at the same time endowed with judgment — ***the truth of the object is said to be by nature prior*** and has ever been said by the sounder to be by nature prior to the truth of the proposition and judgment, in this sense a certain voluntary act and its morality — that is, the necessary agreement or disagreement with rational nature — ***can be regarded as objectively prior to the practical judgment itself***, because it is that on which the truth of the practical judgment — which is called the *law of God common to men*, the *eternal law* as attributed to God and from eternity the judgment of the divine intellect, the *participation of the eternal law* as it is in man — ***solely rests***. For every foundation is by nature prior to the founded.

And in this sense, not another, do Grotius, Theologians, Jurisconsults, Philosophers pronounce *a certain physical motion in human action* — e.g. voluntary concubit with another's wife apart from reference to law, that is to the practical judgment of the intellect — ***moral***, and not morally indifferent, because the necessary agreement of voluntary concubit with another's wife *per se* founds the truth of any practical judgment by which it is judged that *adultery is not to be committed by man, nor is God's will to move man's will to concubit with another's wife*.

#### The Objection "Our Author Forgot We Speak of God"

**You answer:** *"Our author has forgotten that the discourse is of the will of Him whose object does not stand outside Himself unless He Himself willed it: and that the divine will determines objects outside itself, not that the objects determine the divine will."* — So are wont to dispute those who either plainly do not understand the terms (which even beginners can scarcely be ignorant of without shame) because of ***affected ignorance***, or ***malignly pervert*** them. Do not all Philosophers and Theologians teach — what one who in this present matter wishes to dispute against them *ought to know* — that ***God's knowledge is of two sorts***: of ***simple intelligence*** and of ***vision***; likewise, ***natural*** and ***free***? Certainly the objects of divine knowledge of simple intelligence and of natural knowledge, so far as they are the objects of such knowledge, plainly do not depend on God's will. But the necessary disagreement of certain acts with rational nature — if it be regarded ***as possible***, if it be regarded ***as unable to behave otherwise*** — falls under God's divine knowledge of simple intelligence and natural knowledge.

Indeed, by the very truth with which it is asserted ***that God's will cannot be borne upon the unknown***, and that the very act of God's will *presupposes* God's knowledge, by the same truth is it defended that the objects of natural law — taken for the practical judgment of the intellect — and the judgment itself concerning the necessary agreement or disagreement of some voluntary acts with rational nature, in our mode of conceiving ***stand antecedently to the will of God***, so long as this natural law is taken for the decree of a superior. Honesty and baseness, therefore — that is, necessary agreement or disagreement with rational nature — in so far as they are objects of divine knowledge which all Philosophers and Theologians call *of simple intelligence and natural*, do not follow upon law as the decree of a superior: for if God had never been going to constitute man, He would nevertheless from eternity by necessity have judged ***that a being cannot be feigned whose will could truly be moved to the falling from a promise; nor can an animal endowed with intellect and will be created by Him, whose will He could move to the concubit with another's wife***. These judgments wholly antecede every decree of God — which judgments nevertheless have been and still are called the ***Eternal Law of God***.

#### The "Scholastic Darkness" Dismissal

**You will urge back:** *"These are the trifles and empty things of the Scholastics who foster the kingdom of darkness together."* O lovely sagacity! Then **Justin**, **Lactantius**, **Augustine**, who ex professo defended this very sentence, were Scholastics who established and propagated the kingdom of darkness. Therefore ***Cicero*** and ***Plato*** have been made Papists: this one perhaps because he lived at Rome; that one because from his writings Cicero drew exactly the same sentence concerning the eternity of law as the practical judgment taken for God's law common to men, and owes it to Plato. For he must be a stranger from the reading of Plato's and Cicero's *de legibus* who does not know that they defended the same sentence which is Grotius's and that of our native Theologians, Jurisconsults, and Philosophers — which has just now been vindicated from the vainly attempted contradiction and absurdity.

Meanwhile, to beginners, it can be said altogether that ***morality speaks a real relation to the law — i.e. to the decree of a superior binding a subject; and that the honest act is an act commanded by the law of nature, and the base act an act forbidden by the law of nature*** — because these things are rightly said, with saving truth, to the capacity of mere beginners, to whom it has not yet been given to grasp the genuine foundations of moral doctrine and the ratio of morality *a priori*.

#### The Stoic-Fate Objection

But this still remains: that ***whoever asserts the morality of any human act to stand antecedently to God, is injurious to God the Creator***. For God is both of ***freest will*** and ***eternal***. Now if something honest or base were given in itself antecedently to God's will, either ***Stoic Fate*** must be admitted (to which God Himself is bound), or ***some principle prior or coeternal to God*** must be given, as it were ***determining and specifying the forms of things, which not even God must or could resist***. Both of these imply manifest contradiction: for if Stoic Fate were admitted (to which God Himself is bound), then God would at once be of freest will (which all Philosophers and Theologians confess) and yet would not be — since, being bound to Stoic Fate, every liberty and all ignorance of impediment or defect would fall into God Most High and yet would not fall. If, on the other hand, some principle prior or coeternal to God were given, as it were determining and specifying the forms of things, which not even God must or could resist, then God would be the first being (which all men hearing the voice *God* conceive) and yet would not be, if some principle prior to God were given; God would alone be eternal (because eternity is an attribute of God) and yet would not alone be eternal, if a principle coeternal to Him were given.

#### Veltheim's Two-Sense Distinction

But to this it will help to have briefly responded, by way of ***a second example***. ***In two modes*** can the honesty of certain human acts be understood as *standing antecedently to God's will*:

**Mode 1 (impious).** This sense: *It was not placed in God's free arbitrium whether He willed to give rational and social nature to man, or not — that is, whether He willed to constitute man or not — that is, whether He willed to constitute a rational and social animal or not; but by absolute necessity He constituted man* (i.e. a rational and social animal), *either because to the rational and social animal's rational and social nature certain acts dependent on his will necessarily agree, and certain necessarily disagree, and therefore the honesty and baseness of acts has itself antecedently to the will of God*.

**Mode 2 (orthodox).** This sense: *It was indeed placed in God's free arbitrium whether He willed to give rational and social nature to man, or not — that is, whether He willed to constitute man, or not — that is, whether He willed to constitute a rational and social animal or not; and so not from any necessity, but from His freest will He created the rational and social animal, whom we rightly and properly call man; yet, after that rational and social animal — whose rational and social nature once begun to exist from the freest beneplacitum of the divine will — certain acts proceeding from his knowing and willing necessarily agree with that nature or disagree (that is, are honest or base), nor can it ever come about by God's free will that those very acts, after that rational and social animal is produced and by its own will exists as the cause of such acts, should not be agreeing or disagreeing with the rational and social nature*.

If this proposition — *the honesty and baseness of certain human acts stand antecedently to God's will* — be taken in the ***prior*** sense, it is impious and most absurd: for with it standing, Stoic Fate (to which God Himself is bound) would altogether have to be admitted, as Stoic Fate is commonly wont to be defined. But neither Grotius, nor our native Theologians, nor the Jurisconsults and Philosophers in that prior sense ever asserted moral honesty and baseness as standing antecedently to God's will. In the ***posterior*** sense, however, if the adduced phrase be understood, it is indeed Grotius's sentence, and that of our native Theologians, Jurisconsults, and Philosophers: that the honesty and baseness of certain human acts stand antecedently to the will of GOD.

#### The Opponent's Re-Raising of Stoic Fate — "This Is Not to Learn But to Fabricate"

**Again you say:** *"With this assertion admitted, either Stoic Fate must be admitted (to which God Himself is bound), or some principle prior or coeternal to God, as it were determining and specifying the forms of things, which not even God must or could resist."* But this is ***not to learn but to fabricate***, to weave frauds and deceptions which an incautious Youth will not so easily notice, by which the received foundations of solid moral doctrine are rendered hateful to the same.

And certainly to men ignorant of all things, and especially to those who falsely persuade themselves that they are truly learned — nay, who count themselves (shall I say) as fellow soldiers of truly learned men, or as Masters and teachers surpassing them in number — this manner of proving pleases: ***"Just as it is often the case, when any one proposes some thesis whose absurd consequence he does not foresee, often even when he denies that it follows, that others assert it: so, once one or two Scholastics (if you know one of them, you know all) — who with great effort agitate great trifles — have pronounced some trans-handed dogma, Grotius, more of our native Theologians, most celebrated Jurisconsults and Philosophers so blindly see or squint that they cannot see that from that sentence most foolish absurdities, together with most impious and blasphemous fictions, follow. **I do not claim** that so many Theologians, Jurisconsults, and Philosophers — indeed almost all — ***expressly*** defend such a foolish and impious sentence: but I say this, that ***it follows from their sentence*** (ἀπροσδιονυσιακῶς — "irrelevantly," as the saying goes), ***all those who oppose the "per se" and "by their nature" to divine disposition and determination***. But what, at last, is this but ***to fight with one's own masks***, and to vomit forth smoke for fire?

If this is not so, the vanity of the adduced reasoning will become plainer when it is pressed more tightly on the present question — *nothing of morality in a point of reason stands antecedent to God's will* — if you form it in this way: ***If God from eternity could not judge otherwise concerning Himself than that He is to be loved above all things by Himself, and for the same reason by any rational creature which from the freest beneplacitum of His will He was going to constitute; likewise, that any promise is to be kept by Himself, and for the same reason by a rational creature which from the freest beneplacitum of His will He was going to constitute* — ***it follows that I owe to God both that I am, and that I am man, and that I am a creature capable of law, and that certain motions of my will have such a disposition that they ought entirely to be adapted to the norm which God in creation has enjoined upon me, and that my act, if it quadrates to that norm, is honest and good, where it discrepates from it, is bad and base; and so that God is not the creator of all things, nay I need not thank God, I need not bind myself to worship Him, nor do I know how to petition the Dominical prayer "Fiat voluntas tua."***

For whether you say in the more tightly-formed reasoning *the former is true*, or you say with the defenders of the received sentence *morality in point of reason stands antecedent to God's will*, you say the same. For this and nothing else do all those will who defend the received sentence. Let them snatch this consequence and let them adore it as an oracle sprung from Apollo's tripod — however many wish to; never will they certainly achieve that men of sane judgment grasp its force.

The same fate awaits this consequence: ***If from freest will God can constitute a creature or not constitute her, constitute such a creature (e.g. rational) and not constitute such a creature — yet not from God's free will does it depend whether He will constitute a creature who from the condition of her nature is bound to loving God, or one who from the condition of her nature is bound to pursuing God with hate; or whether He will create a rational animal whose will He may, from the freedom of His will, move to the love of a similar animal, or such an animal whose will He may move to the hate of a similar animal; to concubit with his own wife, or to concubit with another's wife*** — ***it follows that God did not create us, and that He in no way gave us body and soul, eyes and ears, reason and all senses***. I believe no rustic in all Thuringia is so stupid that he does not know how to deny these consequences, having at the same time the ground of denial seen through. What then must be accused: shall it be the opponent's dire rage of calumniating, or the miserable and jejune sophistication?

**The former**, if he still have this for an irrevocable assertion: ***"I wonder, that not only Grotius, but most Theologians, Jurisconsults, and Philosophers have defended that sentence than which nothing more foolish could be feigned, from which horrible, impious, blasphemous, and absurd things follow."*** Item: *"I never said that they expressly deny God the creator of all things; that they remove God from the office of creator; that they deny the children's catechesis in the exposition of the first article of the Apostles' Creed, where we profess **that God made us and gave us body and soul, eyes and ears, reason and all senses**; that they deny man to have God's will — in so far as certain motions or actions of man are altogether to agree with natural law; that they deem **there is in no way need for them to give thanks to God** because He formed them as rational creatures, and that they confess themselves bound to worship Him under this name; that they do not know how to ask the third petition of the Dominical prayer* **Fiat voluntas tua**, *and that perhaps they have been pleased to substitute for it this clause:* ***Fiat, quae per se et sua natura sunt necessaria*** — *'Let that be done, which per se and by its own nature is necessary.' But I say this: that it follows from their sentence, if the "per se" and "sua natura" opposes divine disposition and determination."* — **These**, if he thinks the same as those he refutes. For to demonstrate that, it suffices to say: *By atrocious calumny it is feigned on Grotius and on the other defenders of the received sentence that in the "per se" and "sua natura" — when they established acts per se and by their nature honest or base in man — they opposed divine disposition and determination*. #### Veltheim's Constructive Reply — The Possible-Nature Argument

**I.** The ***nature of those acts*** which, by absolute and intrinsic necessity, we call good or bad to man — if it be regarded precisely as *possible* — is produced neither by itself, nor by God, nor by any principle outside God; because the terminus of production is always *existence*, but the nature so regarded says no real existence of itself. Now just as it cannot be denied that from eternity God knew both ***the possible nature of these human acts***, and that it was so-and-not-otherwise-possible, so to every man even moderately versed in Theology — natural and revealed — it can and ought to be clear, that ***that nature as possible*** (as from eternity by God known as possible, as so-and-not-otherwise-possible) is rightly said, in this our mode of conceiving, ***to stand antecedently to the will of God***.

For the knowledge by which God knows this nature as possible — with no production of things being conjoined — is ***simple*** and ***bare***: namely God contemplates this nature as possible. Therefore this knowledge of God concerning that possible nature, so-and-not-otherwise-possible, is called the ***scientia simplicis intelligentiae*** — "science of simple intelligence" — by which God knows all possibles as possibles, not knowing their actual existences. It is also called ***scientia naturalis*** — "natural science" — "natural" here being the same as "necessary": for just as that nature of acts is necessarily possible, and so-and-not-otherwise-possible, so by God's knowledge it is necessarily known; and therefore this knowledge, which is conversant with that necessarily-possible-and-not-otherwise-possible nature of acts, is itself ***necessary*** or ***natural***.

**We argue in this way:**

> **Major.** Whatever God from eternity, before all free decree of the will, has known — that is truly said to stand antecedently to the will of God.
> **Minor.** But that possible nature, so-and-not-otherwise-possible, of human acts which we call to man per se and by their nature honest or base, God from eternity, before all free decree of the will, has known as truly possible, and as so-and-not-otherwise-possible.
> **Conclusion.** Therefore that possible nature of acts, so-and-not-otherwise-possible — or, what comes to the same, the morality of those acts regarded as possible, as so-and-not-otherwise-possible — is truly said to stand antecedently to the will of God.

The ***Major*** no man of sounder judgment, provided he be rightly versed in the terms, will call into doubt. The ***Minor*** is proved: (1) because that is called a possible nature which does not involve repugnance; but it cannot be that what does not involve repugnance should behave otherwise, that is, should involve repugnance, by reason of contradiction. In so far, then, as that nature of acts is possible, it is necessary, and constitutes an object of natural knowledge — that is, of that knowledge which is prior to the free decree. (2) Just as a possible nature is and is called that which does not involve repugnance, and a non-possible that which involves repugnance, so this one as so-and-not-otherwise-possible does not involve repugnance, and as otherwise-possible involves repugnance. And this has been, is, and will be the sentence of Philosophers and Theologians.

#### The Opponent Half-Concedes, Then Simulates Stupor Again

**Now you would believe the contradictor wishes to grasp this**, when he says: *"The morality of actions was objectively in the divine intellect from eternity."* But immediately he simulates stupor again, lest occasion for calumniating fail him: *"From these nothing can be extracted against me, who does nothing other than to assert that **the will of God is not to be excluded from the assigning of the forms of things**, and that **to it nothing stands antecedently**. For truly, the natures of actions — which constitute the object of natural law — were no less than any other things in the intellect of God from eternity according to being known: that is, God from eternity foresaw of what sort each thing to be created hereafter would be; but at the same time He knew it as to be created in its own time freely, that is with His will concurring, and endowed with such a nature. And so, although the morality of actions was objectively in the divine intellect from eternity, nevertheless it does not follow that it, as it exists in act, has it not from God's disposition and will — since among those which are said objectively to be in the divine intellect from eternity, this must be placed in the first place of all, that He Himself freely willed to create things."*

If, I say, you do nothing other than to assert ***that God's will is not to be excluded from the assigning of the forms of things, and that to it nothing stands antecedently*** — that is, that God from a free will created man, that God from a free will gave reason to man, since if He had willed, He could have brought it about that man plainly did not exist and so had no reason, much less could do anything either agreeing with or disagreeing with the rational and social nature, if he had plainly obtained no nature at all (and if he had obtained a savage and horrid nature, he would not have been man, but the species of a wild and horrid animal); likewise that ***nothing stands antecedent to God's will*** — that is, that neither can man be made such an animal (namely, rational and social) unless God wills him to be, nor can an act of such-or-such a nature be made by man if God simply wills it not to be made (for He can also (1) in the freedom of His will bring it about that man not act, (2) again lose him altogether) — if, I say, you do nothing more than to assert such things as these: with what face do you charge Grotius, almost all Theologians, Jurisconsults, Philosophers, with the utmost dullness and *βλασφημίας reos* — "guilty of blasphemy" — all of whom freely grant these matters and have never doubted of this, nor moved controversy with anyone?

#### Where the Opponent's Sophistication Fails — Possible vs. Future in Divine Knowledge

But, as a matter of fact, you are still doing something else: for you falsely assert that *"with that nature of actions which constitutes the object of natural law, it is set up as with any other things whatsoever — namely, as equally having been from eternity in the intellect of God according to being known."* This is most false. For *that nature of actions which constitutes the object of natural law* God knew ***as from it flows a possible agreement or disagreement with rational nature***, and indeed an agreement or disagreement which is not only necessarily possible but also necessarily possible ***in this mode, and not in another***. But who would be so absurd as to say that other things whatever were from eternity in the intellect of God according to possible being known ***under the ratio of such agreement or disagreement***? Here you have nothing which you can solidly except against.

For what you except — *"But does not this prohibit that the moral affections of an action arise from agreement or disagreement with a norm?"* — to this we respond: ***that norm is the rational nature itself***. For if you yourself recognise, as known by God from eternity, the agreement or repugnance of acts with rational nature, so-and-not-otherwise-possible, what prevents the rational nature from being named as the norm? And this is what the Theologians and Philosophers wish, when they well admonish: ***moral sin*** can be regarded in a twofold way: (1) in so far as it is moral evil; (2) in so far as it is turning-away from God and offence against God. Hence, on account of this twofold mode of considering, they also set up a twofold ***rule*** by whose intuition it holds the ratio of sin: one, which is the rule of sin so far as it is moral evil; the other, which is the rule of it so far as it is turning-away from God and offence against God. The ***prior rule*** of moral sin, namely in so far as it is moral evil, is ***the rational nature as it is rational***; the ***posterior rule*** of sin, namely in so far as it is turning-away from God and offence against God, is by the consent of all ***the law of God***. All indeed recognise that in itself the sin of man as moral evil, and the sin of man as turning-away from God and offence against God, do not differ; nor does any among them deny that every moral sin of man is also a certain turning-away from God and offence against God: yet they urge this — that the ***modes of considering*** the moral act are altogether diverse, and this prior to that; wherefore the prior pertains only to Philosophy, the posterior to Theology; and in this posterior mode alone is sin called ἁμαρτία καὶ ἀνομία, that is, sin as speaking disagreement with ***law***.

#### The "Order of Reason" Objection — God's Free Creation Comes First

**You will urge back:** *"That agreement and repugnance of human acts with rational nature freely formed by God depends on God's free will, inasmuch as He freely constituted such a creature. Hence it is false that in the point of reason the baseness of an act is prior to the definition made by God. But first of all, man is conceived as a rational creature to be freely produced by God; thence is conceived the habitude of the acts themselves as apt to be directed to a norm; after this, the law, as the norm to which those acts must necessarily be referred and directed; and lastly honesty and baseness as affections of acts related to the norm. To think up another point of reason here, or to invert this order, is against reason. Whosoever, therefore, refers the nature of those acts of which the discourse is, to the Author, God — as having been so adorned by Him in the creation of man — they consent with me; but those who contend that the acts' habitude and disposition do not have themselves from the divine definition, determination, and will — let them see what Creator they would adopt for themselves."*

Here, that it may appear every contrivance of the contradictor is in vain poured into the air, let one single thing be observed. ***When thus far the discourse has been of morality standing antecedently to the will of God, the words have been of the possible agreement*** — the so-and-not-otherwise-possible agreement — ***of acts with rational nature, precisely regarded as possible***. I believe no one is so stupid as, this observation being made, not to see that it is in vain here to make mention of the ***divine decree*** by which He freely constituted such a creature; of ***rational nature freely to be produced by God***; of the ***habitude and disposition*** which the acts have from the divine definition, determination, and will. For all these together and singly in no wise come into the present disquisition, where we treat of the ***nature of human acts per se and by their nature honest or base, uniquely as it was from eternity the object of God's knowledge***, and indeed of that knowledge which the Theologians and Philosophers call ***scientia simplicis intelligentiae*** and ***scientia naturalis***.

Away, therefore, with your trifles and improbable sophistications — *"from the very foundation of establishing natural law the will of God is excluded!"* For here there is no discourse at all of the ***existence*** of natural law taken as the decree of God by which He in time has obliged man to Himself constituted in time as subject; but we are precisely inspecting ***the nature of acts, which we call per se and by their nature base or honest to man, as from eternity it was in the intellect of God according to possible being known***.

If you wish to catch me by cavil — *"that from eternity God knew that He Himself would wish to create certain things, and so to the free decree of God concerning the nature of man to be freely formed, since it was from eternity, nothing stands antecedent; for to the eternal there is nothing prior"* — in vain do you cavil, forgetful of yourself. For in express words you were impugning and denying that ***in the point of reason*** (N.B. *in the point of reason*) the baseness of an act is said to be prior ***by definition and determination made from the free will of God***. Certainly God from eternity knew all things by one most simple act — which cognition is commonly called God's knowledge; God also willed from eternity all futures which He willed by one most simple act, which eternal volition of futures is commonly called God's eternal decree. Yet although God's knowledge and God's will on the side of God are one most simple entity, as the Philosophers speak — by which, by one most simple act, from eternity He knew and willed whatever has the ratio of knowable and of willed-by-God — on the side of the objects, into which they tend each in diverse modes, the intellect and will are wont to be distinguished mutually; and God's intellection, likewise His volition, admits various distinctions.

He must be most stupid who does not know how to distinguish between ***what from eternity God knew as possible*** and ***what He knew as future***. For although every future has been known by God from eternity as future, nevertheless in the order of reason something is ***prior*** in the ratio of possible than in the ratio of future: for first we conceive a thing as from itself ***possible*** — namely, because from itself it does not imply contradiction; afterwards we conceive it as ***future*** — because the possibility of a thing (as the Philosophers speak) presupposes God only as to being and being-able; but the *futurition* of a thing (if this diction may be used) presupposes God not only as to being and being-able, but as ***willing to produce*** or ***as the cause which will produce***.

Most true, therefore, it remains that in the point of reason ***the baseness of an act stands antecedently to the will of God***, nor is man first of all conceived as a rational creature to be freely produced by God. Those who assert that God's ***natural knowledge*** and ***knowledge of simple intelligence*** in our mode of conceiving stand antecedently to God's will, on that account in no way abnegate God as the creator of all things. The contradictor, therefore, has not yet by his jejune sophistications brought it about that — as hitherto, so in future — the morality of human acts, in so far as it was from eternity the object of God's knowledge of simple intelligence and natural knowledge, cannot be said to stand antecedently to the will of God.

#### Second Horn — Morality Considered as *Actually Given* Also Stands Antecedent to God's Will

**II.** The ***morality of those acts*** which we call *to man per se and by their nature* honest or base, ***even if it be regarded as it is given in act***, is rightly said to stand antecedently to the will of God — in its own mode.

**Our reason** is: because that ***necessity*** which is attributed to certain actions for honesty and baseness is ***absolute***; but what is of absolute necessity, in so far as it is of absolute necessity, does not depend on the free determination of the divine will. #### The Opponent's Hypothetical-Necessity Objection

But this assertion suffers the most atrocious assault. Let us see the very reason by which the man so hurls himself about, and let us briefly weigh it, if perhaps shame can be impressed on him.

#### Veltheim's Reply — The Twofold Absolute Necessity

Let us reduce the adduced reason into syllogistic form, that if perhaps one or another of the idiots — to the capacity of all of whom the contradictor glories to have deduced these things most fully — was terrified and clung, nothing immediately appearing which might be answered, he may now the more easily notice the colouring and the trifles. The argument will be such:

> **Major.** Whatever necessity supposes such an act which no one is who does not ascribe to the freest will of God, that necessity is not absolute, but hypothetical.
> **Minor.** But that necessity which is attributed to actions (which Grotius, the Theologians, Jurists, and Philosophers reckon per se, by their nature, and by absolute necessity to be honest or base to man) for honesty and baseness, supposes such an act which no one is who does not ascribe to the freest will of God, namely the creation of the rational animal.
> **Conclusion.** Therefore that necessity which is attributed to certain human actions is not absolute, but hypothetical.

Before responding to this argument, we presuppose that in its proper place in these very *Institutes* there is to be perspicuously handed down and explained the distinction of ***absolute necessity*** into:

- **Necessity of existence, of exercise, or of contradiction** (*necessitas existentiae, exercitii, seu contradictionis*)
- **Necessity of essence, of species, or of contrariety** (*necessitas essentiae, speciei, seu contrarietatis*)

All non-idiots understand this distinction, if only they know to distinguish between *liberty of exercise* (or *of contradiction*) and *liberty as to the species of the act* (or *of contrariety*) — which distinction stands in all philosophical books and compendia. Therefore let him be deservedly numbered among those to whom you yourself freely number yourself, since you simulate so gross a stupor — perhaps so that you may the more easily foist your sycophancies on idiots.

**Once more let us repeat the argument**, since it pleases you so immensely:

> *Whatever necessity supposes such an act which no one is who does not ascribe to the freest will of God, that is not absolute, but hypothetical.*

We ***limit*** this Major in this way: *Whatever necessity supposes such an act which no one is who does not ascribe to the freest will of God, that is not absolute necessity **of existence, of exercise, or of contradiction***. So with the Major conceded by us — which we altogether concede, nor has any of those against whom you dispute ever doubted of its truth — subsume:

> *But that necessity which is attributed to certain human actions (namely to those which by Grotius and from the common and received sentence are called per se and by their nature honest or base to man) for honesty and baseness, supposes such an act which no one is who does not ascribe to the freest will of God, namely the creation of the rational animal.*

Conclude:

> *Therefore that necessity which is attributed to certain human actions ... is not necessity absolute of existence, exercise, or contradiction.*

***We grant the whole argument.*** I do not do you an injury if I doubt — until you have clearly shown — in what place in Grotius's writings, or in those of our native Theologians, Jurists, Philosophers, this assertion occurs: ***"By absolute necessity of existence, exercise, or contradiction it is necessary that certain human actions be honest or base."*** For that is the same as if they had said: *Absolutely necessary it is that there exists a human act which is base*; and because a human act cannot exist unless man exists, *absolutely necessary it is that man exist*. Which comes to the same as if they had said: *Either God by absolute necessity created man* — that is, could not not create man — *or man exists from himself by absolute necessity, or has adopted another creator for himself, because it was absolutely necessary that he exist*. Nor does this here avail: *"But I say this, that this assertion follows from their sentence."* Where is your forehead, O Disputant!

#### The Argument Reformed for the Second Necessity — and Where It Fails

But let the argument proceed once more in this way:

> **Major.** Whatever necessity supposes such an act which no one is who does not ascribe to the freest will of God, that is not absolute necessity ***of essence, species, or contrariety***, but hypothetical.
> **Minor.** But that necessity which is attributed to certain human actions for honesty and baseness, &c.
> **Conclusion.** [Therefore...]

So the ***Major*** becomes most false. For although no act of man existing, no act of man exists honest or base; and therefore, that a human act exist honest or base, altogether presupposes that a man exist (who however owes to the freest will of God this, that he exists) — nevertheless, because *after* a man exists (that is, after a rational animal exists, and knowing and willing does many things), certain actions by man (i.e. by the rational animal, knowing and willing) performed cannot not agree or disagree with the existing rational nature of the existing man (i.e. of the existing rational animal); and again, certain other actions neither agree nor disagree with it — that is, are neither perfections nor imperfections of the rational nature as such — therefore to those actions ***absolute necessity of essence, species, or contrariety*** for honesty or baseness is attributed, to these not at all.

#### The Fire-Is-Hot Example

Let us illustrate the matter by an example, if perhaps anyone be of so dull a wit that he cannot grasp what we have said. The necessity which is attributed to fire for ***heat*** is not hypothetical but absolute necessity: for no man would say that fire is hot and water is hot by one and the same necessity. But what sort of absolute necessity, pray, is it that is attributed to fire for heat? Is it absolute necessity ***of existence, exercise, or contradiction***? Not at all. For it is *not* absolutely necessary that fire exist — just as it is not absolutely necessary that man exist. For God with equally freest will founded fire, as the creation of man comes from God's freest will: now, if fire plainly did not exist, neither would a hot fire exist, nor a cold fire, since of a non-existing thing there are no predicates which presuppose existence. For fire to exist as hot, fire must exist. Therefore by absolute necessity of existence, exercise, or contradiction it is *not* necessary that fire be hot, because fire can not exist, and if it does not exist, it does not exist as hot either. The power of creating fire, if it had so pleased God, could with the same liberty *not* exert itself, as with that liberty it did exert itself; nor does it imply contradiction that fire not exist, and so also not imply contradiction that fire not exist as hot.

Yet still there is absolute necessity ***of essence, species, or contrariety***, whence absolutely necessary it is and is said that ***fire is hot***. For God by His free will cannot bring it about that fire rightly and properly so called exist and yet in act be not hot but cold: because if in act it were not hot, there would not exist *fire* but *a being of another essence and species*. If, therefore, God can bring it about by His free will that fire not exist, and yet cannot bring it about that fire exist and yet in act be not hot — then it is *not* necessary that there exist a hot fire; yet it is necessary that an existing fire exist as hot. Whereas, on the contrary, it is not necessary that water exist as hot, nor is it necessary that existing water exist as hot: because water exists even though it be not hot — although by *hypothetical* necessity it is hot, if fire heats a pot filled with water which it surrounds on every side.

**The parallel to the moral case.** Just as fire's heat stands under absolute necessity of essence (though not of existence), so the per-se honesty or baseness of certain human acts stands under absolute necessity of essence — presupposing only that a man exists (which he does by God's free creation), but once he exists, the honesty of some of his acts and the baseness of others cannot be unsettled by any further decree of the divine will, any more than God's free will could make an actually-existing fire be cold while remaining fire. The opponent's hypothetical-necessity argument succeeds only against the straw-man position that *absolute necessity of existence* attaches to these actions — a position no one has ever held. Against the actually-held position (absolute necessity of essence), his argument is impotent.

#### Fifthly — The *Si Currit, Movetur* Example Abused

**Fifthly**, you play the ***most miserable and truly to-be-lamented Sophist***, when you dare — in order to palliate your trifles — to use the vulgarly-known example of hypothetical necessity: ***"If he runs, he is moved."*** As though so open a matter could be palliated! Your words: *"'If he runs, he is moved' — you prattle — is an example of hypothetical necessity. Before anyone freely undertook a course, neither to run nor to be moved was necessary to him; but with the course freely undertaken, motion cannot not simultaneously exist. So before God created man, neither to create man nor to bear the law of nature was necessary to God. But once the creation of man was by Him freely undertaken, the law of nature could not not be simultaneously borne."*

(1) These two must not be confounded: one thing is to say *If a man runs, he is moved*; another, *Running is motion*. The ***prior*** is an example of *merely hypothetical* necessity: for neither by absolute necessity of exercise, existence, or contradiction is it necessary to a man that he run or make any other local motion; nor is it necessary by necessity of essence, species, or contrariety that man run, that man be moved from place to place; since though a man not run, though a man not be moved from place to place, he does not on that account lose his essence as he had it before — i.e. he does not cease to be a rational animal. The ***posterior*** is an example of absolute necessity — not of absolute necessity of existence, exercise, or contradiction (for it is not absolutely necessary that a course exist, but a course is freely undertaken, and so it is not necessary that there exist a running motion, since when a course plainly is not in act, neither is it in act motion), but of absolute necessity of essence, species, or contrariety: for it cannot be that a course exist, unless it be in act local motion; since unless the course were local motion, it would not be a course, but another kind of thing.

Likewise, most distinct are these two examples of necessity: ***If a man willingly concubits with another's wife, he does basely***; and, ***Voluntary concubit with another's wife is a base deed***. The prior is an example of merely hypothetical necessity, since neither by necessity of existence, exercise, or contradiction, nor by necessity of essence, species, or contrariety, is it absolutely necessary that a man by his will concubit with another's wife, that a man do basely. The posterior, however, is an example of absolute necessity of essence, species, or contrariety: because it cannot come about that there exist voluntary concubit with another's wife, and yet in act it be not a base deed — i.e. with no one thinking of it, be disagreeing with rational nature as such.

(2) Most distinct also are these two examples of necessity: ***If a man runs, he is moved***; and, ***If God created man, He bore the law of nature to him*** — e.g., prohibited to him voluntary concubit with another's wife. For (α) a course is itself a certain motion; but the creation of man is not itself a certain law-bearing. (β) A man can be moved though he not run; but the law of nature cannot be borne to man by God unless man be created by God. (γ) It is not impossible that a man be given who neither runs nor is moved; but it is impossible that a man be given who is neither created by God nor has the law of nature borne to him by God. (δ) That a man be moved does not prerequire any absolutely-necessary agreement or disagreement with the rational nature of man as it is rational, which it would respect as its *foundation*; but that the law of nature be borne to man by God *does* rest on the necessary agreement or disagreement of human acts (concerning which, namely, the law of nature disposes) with the rational nature of man as it is such — a relation which can indeed be regarded antecedently to the will of a superior. (ε) Grotius does not assert that God by absolute necessity enjoined or forbade on man certain acts ***before man existed*** to whom they could be enjoined or forbidden — for who without the utmost dullness could feign so most-dull a sentence to Grotius? But he uniquely hints that God, to a man who is *in act* or exists, by absolute necessity enjoined or forbade certain acts: e.g. God, saving His justice, could have *not* forbidden voluntary concubit with another's wife — namely if He had not created man (for man, saving the divine justice, could have been not created; whence if God had created no rational animal, there would not then have existed any to whom He could have forbidden voluntary concubit with another's wife); but to an existing man, saving His justice, He cannot not forbid voluntary concubit with another's wife. For the former's truth, contingency in respect of exercise or existence or contradiction suffices; the latter's truth is grounded in the truth of essence or contrariety, with which contingency in respect of exercise or existence or contradiction can altogether stand.

#### The Defence of Grotius's *Etiamsi Daremus Non Esse Deum*

**You except:** *"This conspires with that little flower of Grotius not far distant from blasphemy — as if natural law would oblige nations even if we conceded that there is no God, or that human affairs are not cared for by Him; and that in this universe integrity and order could stand, even if God had never created this universe, or if He were plainly relegated outside the world."*

Good God, into what forms the dire rage of calumniating does not know how to turn itself! Let anyone inspect the passage of Grotius which stands in the Prolegomena to the three books *De Jure Belli ac Pacis* no. 11, and compare it with what precedes, and he will easily see what an atrocious injury is done to Grotius.

**(1)** It did not come into Grotius's mind even in a dream, as if natural law — taken for the decree of a superior — would oblige men, even if we conceded that there is no God, and so that no superior at all is given. For that would be the same as if Grotius had said: *If we conceded that there is no superior, we should still altogether assert that there is a superior*.

**(2)** But this is what Grotius willed: ***The law of nature, taken for a rational judgment concerning acts by absolute necessity of species, essence, or contrariety honest or base to man*** — i.e. agreeing or disagreeing with man's rational nature as it is such — ***would still have place, even if we were to grant, which cannot be granted without the utmost wickedness, that there is no God, or that human affairs are not cared for by Him.*** For example: if we were to grant — which cannot be granted without the utmost wickedness — that this man does not have his rational nature from God, still, if we granted that he *is* man, this man could judge that *to keep a promise necessarily agrees with his rational nature as such*, and *to fall from a promise necessarily disagrees with his rational nature as such* — just as God, who has no superior to Himself nor has His rational nature from any other, nevertheless can judge, and from eternity has judged, that this act (namely, by necessity of species or essence or contrariety absolute) agrees with His rational nature, and that this disagrees.

**(3)** Grotius writes: *"If we were to grant, **which cannot be granted without the utmost wickedness**, namely that the contrary is implanted in us by reason — that is, that we and all our things owe themselves to God Himself as artificer"* — why have you omitted these words: ***"the utmost wickedness"***, &c.? Namely, that to your allegations against someone having any trust, Grotius's assertion might be rendered more and more hateful. Is it not allowed sometimes to a disputant to put something ***per impossibile*** or ***per absurdum*** — nay, ***per impium ac pestilentissimum absurdum***?

**(4)** It is shameful to a Doctor to be refuted *with fault*. Let the cordate Man judge, pray, whether this little flower verges little from blasphemy: ***the judgment concerning the absolutely-necessary agreement or disagreement of this or that human act with rational nature as it is such would have place, even if we were to grant — which cannot be granted without the utmost wickedness, and whose contrary reason has implanted in us — that the man who exists and uses this judgment does not have his rational nature from God***. By what title the sequels are worthy, you will see:

*"If man were born only for himself alone, we confess it would be fitting that it should be left to his absolute arbitrium whether to love or hate himself, to preserve or destroy himself. For if the molestations which accompany human life, far surpassing that scanty and vile portion of pleasures that recur in the same tenor, but languidly and not without disgust — and the longer life is drawn out, the more evils it can take on — were weighed: how few would not at the first time break off life, had not instinct so greatly commended it! So in your judgment, here too it will be lawful to exclaim: ***"What madness is argued by this, which is said: 'If man were born only for himself alone, we confess it would be fitting that it should be left to his absolute arbitrium what he should decide concerning himself!' Therefore it will seem fitting to you too that in this universe integrity and order could stand, that man would exist of his own arbitrium, that an arbitrium would be given of all things, even if God had never created this universe nor produced man — or if God were plainly relegated outside the world."*** We say that this absurd seems to you fitting, yet it follows from your sentence by the very same right by which you feign it to follow from Grotius's sentence.

**Eighthly**, you confess at great length your own baseness: *"Even if I had maximally received some Scholastic term"* — namely the term of ***hypothetical necessity*** — *"differently from the genuine and divinely-sanctioned Metaphysics among the Jenenses, or from the Stahlian tables (as if by the eternal law of the Parcae) it is sanctioned, still it would not be fitting that so acidly a MAN should be insulted. Why not Grotius alone, but most Theologians, Jurists, Philosophers, have you called such ***that they have thus far peddled nothing but mere trifles, most dull most absurd fictions, blasphemous opinions, for the genuine foundations of natural law and of every science of just and unjust — and have obtruded their futile and stupid principles as true and right upon Academic Youth***?

The same is the fruit of another of your cavillations: *"Given the rational creature, it is necessary for her to worship God. #### The Calvinist Parallel — The Same Sophistication Would Favour the Reformed

For what is said: *"To God's will, since it is eternal and since God Himself is as to the thing eternal, nothing can be given antecedent in our mode of conceiving"* — you shall most rightly call these ***senile sophistications***. For if this collection held, the ***Calvinists*** would have reason to congratulate themselves in their own bosom. For by the same, nay perhaps greater, right could they say: ***since the foreknowledge of God by which from eternity He knew the futures — Adam's and ours, our sins — was eternal, and to the eternal nothing is given prior; no other cause can be given why from eternity it was true that Adam and all men would be sinners in time, than this eternal foreknowledge: for what God from eternity foreknew, the will of Adam in time necessarily enacted; and if the will acted otherwise than what God from eternity foreknew, God would not have rightly foreknown, and the divine foreknowledge would not be infallible.***

And I should wish to know what in reply the patrons and commenders of the contrary sentence could here answer. For if they themselves should answer that by this argument of the Calvinists nothing is proved except ***hypothetical necessity*** — by which the sins of Adam and of all men foreseen as futures by God from eternity were necessarily futures — but that by hypothetical necessity liberty is in no way removed, as it can stand together with that necessity: this reply would be no reply at all, unless something in the ***point of reason*** were conceived as prior to God's eternal foreknowledge (which however these same men mordantly deny). Namely (as is the diction of the Reformed), ***the very futurition of sin is prior***, so that in our mode of conceiving it is prior that man's will would in time determine itself according to its own liberty to sin, than that it would be known by God — however much from eternity it be known. For God from eternity would not have known the future unless it had been going to be future; and so eternal foreknowledge, as posterior to the futurition determination of the human will, could not have brought necessity to the will. The parallel is exact: the opponent's reasoning on *to-the-eternal-nothing-is-prior* would prove too much, dissolving the Lutheran defence against Reformed necessitarianism no less than it would dissolve ours against him.

#### The *Fiat voluntas tua* — the Orthodox Petition Across Three Categories

As to the ***fourth petition of the Lord's Prayer*** — *Fiat voluntas tua* — to which the opponent provokes: we altogether pray this petition.

**Fiat voluntas tua**, O most just God, in those things which per se and by their nature are honest to us — that is, which by absolute necessity of species necessarily agree with our rational nature as such (which rational nature we owe to Thy goodness and have received from Thy freest will), and which on this account by Thee, O most just God, are necessarily commanded: ***Fiat voluntas tua***, to which we owe the lowliest obedience; let it be done, that we may choose such things, and on occasion given do them; and so bear ourselves rightly toward Thee, O fount of all just and honest, who art the very immutable justice; rightly bear ourselves toward ourselves, rightly toward our neighbour; and so live piously, soberly, and justly.

**Fiat voluntas tua**, O most just God, in those things which per se and by their nature are base to us — that is, by absolute necessity of species necessarily repugnant to our rational nature as such (which we uniquely refer to Thy freest will and Thy goodness as received), and which on this account by Thee, O most just God, are necessarily prohibited: ***Fiat voluntas tua***, to which we owe the lowliest obedience; let it be done, that we may flee such things and always omit them.

**Fiat voluntas tua**, O most just God, in those things which per se and by their nature to us are neither honest nor base: that, if by other law or mandate they be enjoined to us or forbidden, we may choose or flee, commit or omit them, and so render to the superior's will the obedience owed; but if they be of our merely freest arbitrium, then all and single things which we choose or shun, commit or omit, we may uniquely choose or shun, commit or omit, unto the glory of Thy most holy name.

Those, however, who pour out prayers in ***this*** sense to God, these are compelled to subject themselves to these censures of yours: *"It appears that they do not best promise themselves of God's will. Hence they so hard-pressedly labour to inject bonds upon it, or to remove it from the governance of things, lest perhaps it degenerate into licence and promulgate absurd laws to men. What opinions these creatures should we believe to hold of God, who do not even know the third petition of the Dominical prayer? Likewise, if some evil threatened mortals from the liberty of the divine will, would our Saviour not have commanded us to pray* **FIAT VOLUNTAS TUA**? *Unless perhaps it pleases you to substitute for that clause:* ***FIANT QUAE PER SE ET SUA NATURA SUNT NECESSARIA***." Item: *"It appears that they do not promise themselves better of God's will than of the lust of some Caligula or Nero. The more therefore they declaim on these matters, the more they flog their own folly."*

Marvellous, unless they wish to pray: ***"Fiat voluntas tua"*** — that is: *Do, O God, by Thy free will, lest perhaps we worship Thee, before we are; do, O God, by Thy free will, lest sooner we lose our nose, eyes, and ears, and other members, and misuse them, than from Thy free will we received them.* Nay, if any wished to suppose that altogether absurd hypothesis — long rejected and exploded in the schools of the ancient Philosophers — that we are at single moments generated afresh, would he not still be able to pray, ***"Let obedience be rendered to Thy moral dictate in those things which in themselves by their nature are honest to man"*** — namely, because the nature of man is so composed that these cannot not agree with it?

#### Transition to the Second Example — The Socinian Zwicker on the Trinity

One single example remains to be adduced for the Cultivators of Theology, that they may the more rightly judge of the salutary ***use*** and noxious ***abuse*** of this axiom. That example is set forth by one ***Daniel Zwicker***, a notorious Socinian: ***COMPELLE INTRARE, or TRACTATUS TRACTATUUM DE CONTRADICTIONE*** — "The Treatise of Treatises on Contradiction."

This whole ***Treatise of Treatises*** of Zwicker's, so entitled — which if you had heard its bare title named you would have believed a work of most vast bulk — is completed in a single quaternion, whose first leaf exhibits the mere title, while on the remaining three leaves the whole *Treatise of Treatises* is contained. The complete title is this: ***Compelle intrare, or the Treatise of Treatises on Contradiction, by which — nearly alone, if rightly recognised — most of the Churches of today, miserably (for grief!) collapsed, namely the Roman, Greek, Lutheran, and Calvinist, could, if they wish, be restored and most easily brought to the mutual cultivation of peace. Thus, by the divine compassion, with Daniel Zwicker recalling Christians astray into the way of truth.***

The ***summary*** of the whole *Treatise of Treatises* comprehended in the remaining three leaves the Author himself expresses toward the end of the quaternion, with this note prefixed:

> ***The syllogism, into which all things in this writing are briefly resolved, is this:***
>
> **Major.** *Whosoever in a true contradiction concerning idolatry and eternal damnation have hitherto stuck — these, if they wish to be saved, ought at once to cast off this contradictory and at once noxious sentence of theirs, and to exchange it for a truer and more salutary one.* (Which is self-evident, nor needs proof.)
> **Minor.** *But most Churches, namely the Roman, Greek, Lutheran, and Calvinist, in a true contradiction concerning idolatry and eternal damnation have hitherto stuck.* (As throughout this writing is now evidently shown.)
> **Conclusion.** *Therefore most of these Churches, namely those expressly named, if they wish to be saved, ought at once to cast off this contradictory and most noxious sentence of theirs, and exchange it for a truer and more salutary one.*

How much Zwicker ascribes to his own *Treatise of Treatises* appears from the words next added to this syllogism: *"This syllogism, I say, is woven from the unanimous and perpetual consent of the Doctors — manifest and infallible, nay, received and approved even by those who in the highest degree contradict one another and in that very contradiction do not wish their own thing to be true and false simultaneously — adorned therefore with eternal truth; and so to be far preferred to the decisions and decrees of all Councils, Fathers, Popes, and Doctors imprudently departing from a truth so firmly, so plainly divine. Let the remembered Churches now be willing to weigh it."*

At the close of the tract is this ***Admonition***: *"This writing shall be divulged in kingdoms, regions, cities, towns, academies, conventions, and gatherings of Theologians, through Europe and other parts of the Globe, so that as in a General Council of the Globe discourse of it may be held, and something certain decided for the reformation of the Churches."*

By what right, by what occasion, these things and others shortly more copiously to be adduced have been brought forward in the present, is easily collected from Zwicker's words, which on the obverse of the second page the first leaf exhibits (whose first page carried the title): *"You have the judge of controversies in the Church, and him not suspected, but who bears sentence to the one erring — than whom no man can be dearer to him: namely, he himself to himself. For if in disputing he is reduced to such a point that he is caught in a most manifest contradiction, he must confess that he has sinned; so that he is not only his own judge, but also such a judge as clearly condemns him; since **two contradictories can never be simultaneously true**, since always the one argues the other of falsity. Therefore the contradictor argues against himself of falsity, and so is his own infallible judge. Thus Cornelius Martini in his Analysis Logica p. m. 115 in the preface here alleged, and this on the ground that in the very vestibule of his writing it should at once appear that he who presses contradiction is to be far preferred to all Councils and their reasonings (which, as Bellarmine testifies, Councils have drawn their conclusions or decrees from Scripture by this means) — since with contradiction shown, the adversary of any sort proves himself of falsity and lying."*

#### Zwicker's Case Against the Trinity — The Syllogism

Let us now examine the ***very argument*** by which Zwicker proves what he had undertaken to prove in itself — namely that **the Mystery of the Most Holy Trinity, as we call it, implicates a most manifest contradiction**. For thus from the beginning of folio 2 he trifles: *"It is my mind to prove that the aforementioned Churches, in a capital matter of the gravest moment — namely in the sentence on the Trinity — have so rendered themselves guilty of contradiction that unless they at once wash out this stain, I do not see how they can have God propitious and favourable to them."*

To this returns the whole argument, if we form it more pressedly and reduce it to this syllogistic form:

> **Major.** *Whatever implicates most true and most manifest contradiction, that does not fall under salvific faith* — that is, cannot be believed with salvific faith; and whoever believes that this is believed by himself with salvific faith, his faith is not salvific but dull and impious faith.
> **Minor.** *But the so-called Mystery of the Most Holy Trinity implicates most true and most manifest contradiction.*
> **Conclusion.** *Therefore the so-called Mystery of the Most Holy Trinity does not fall under salvific faith* — i.e. cannot be believed by salvific faith; and whoever believes that this is believed by himself with salvific faith, his faith is not salvific but dull and impious faith.

**Wishing to prove the Major**, Zwicker urges in the place of proof ***the unanimous, constant, and perpetual consent of the Doctors*** (the nature of the thing itself, and the immutable sanctity of God who can neither be deceived nor lie — than which the authority of any contradicting men or even of Councils, as similarly not sanctioning concerning the natures of things but, chiefly solicitous of their own uncertain or false traditions, often erring and from themselves dissenting, is always more weighty and firmer — thoroughly inspecting), *"that which truly implicates contradiction can neither by divine power come to be, but altogether remains simply impossible and false, and so a pure, pure lie; hence it ought not for any reason to be set or admitted in things divine or in Sacred Scripture."* (p. 2.) And truly there was no cause why he himself should expend so much time on the proof of a thing known and most manifest to all; nor should he so anxiously have conquered the consent of the Doctors of the Church. The truth of the **Minor** proposition he believes most easy of perception: *"It is not longer necessary"* — he says at p. 4 — *"to show how far all the churches by me recalled stick to implicated contradiction, on this ground: (1) that in this again there is the consent of all Theologians, that God is some nature or essence, and that supremely intelligent; which it suffices to know in this place; (2) that a divine person, of whatever sort it also be said to be, since it is intelligent, is necessarily essence — which again suffices here; (3) that it is necessarily something which in itself and its essence is undivided and divided from any other, if given."* Zwicker continues on the same page: *"Since therefore those things are necessarily to be thought, partly of God and of the divine Person, partly of unity — so that unless they be thought, God, person, and unity are plainly abolished and denied — hence descriptions of God, of person, of unity, containing these quiddities, are as truly, essentially necessary as if you were to say man is an animal; and besides, whatever is opposed to this at once implicates true contradiction. For example: if a person be set up without a proper essence (as necessarily happens if three persons are said to be one essence in number), then at once there will be and there will not be a person: which is contradictory. Item: if singular or one-in-number essence be said to be common to three persons, then at once it will be singular or one-in-number and will not be — which again manifestly contradicts itself."*

**And this is Zwicker's argument**, by which he so marvellously vaunts himself, and whose conclusion he often himself repeats. For thus at p. 5: *"It stands fixed, namely, that all the recalled Churches in their sentence on the Trinity stick to implicated contradiction, and so to things impossible, most false, and most trifling; nay (since, besides the one God the Father, they also for the one God worshipped a Second Person of the Trinity — which however, as has hitherto been shown, in the nature of things and in God's one-in-number essence never was, nor could be, nor is, and so remains a mere idol — and this not without a depression and grave contumely both of God Himself, whose glory they diminish, and of the Man and Lord Jesus Christ, to be worshipped and adored next after God) — impiously, idolatrously, and with perpetual anathema to be damned and rejected, they have hitherto believed and believe of God, and have driven and compelled others to believe the same impiety with themselves, perishing together."* On the same page: *"They make idolaters and damned persons by that article on the Trinity."* On page 8: *"It is manifest that the said Churches have now been openly convicted of a perilous and damnable heresy, and this in their primary article of faith."* Likewise: *"I break off, since I foresee that I have now by this reasoning not only brought the Lutherans and Calvinists or Reformed to a Christian moderation, but even have cast the Roman Pope down from his throne and seat. For he who hitherto has been believed unable to err, is now in his primary article of faith caught erring most grossly; and this by this* **COMPELLE INTRARE** — *which is not of man but of God."*

#### The First Response — Zwicker Fails the *Secundum Idem* Condition of Contradiction

Certainly, if some Cultivator of Philosophy and Theology not yet so versed or little exercised in Theological Controversies should look upon ***Zwicker's argument*** itself, at first intuition it might seem to him as if it were most easy to respond to it by denying the Minor proposition — that is, by thoroughly denying that the Mystery of the Most Holy Trinity, so-stated, implicates a most-true and most-manifest contradiction. For to a true contradiction it is required that the same thing be said ***to be and not to be***, and so that the same thing be affirmed and denied ***of the same, in the same manner, according to the same, in order to the same, and at the same time*** — which second of these four perpetual conditions of true contradiction was by no means observed here by Zwicker. For he would then have proved that the Churches, ***in a capital matter*** (his very words were) ***and of gravest moment — namely in the sentence on the Trinity — have so rendered themselves guilty of contradiction that unless they at once wash out this stain, it does not appear how they can have God propitious and favourable to them***, if the Churches had said that God is ***according to the same*** one and three — that is, if they had asserted God to be *one in essence and at the same time three in essences, three in persons and at the same time one in person*, so as to have simultaneously confessed one God and three Gods, three persons of the Divinity and one single person of the Divinity. But all and single the Churches pronounce God ***one in essence, not three in essences; three in persons, not one in person*** — and so pronounce Him one and three ***not according to the same***.

#### Zwicker's Rejoinder — Essence and Person Identified in Divinis

Yet a lover of more accurate erudition, having pondered the matter more intimately and accurately, will easily see that not all the difficulty has yet been removed, and that Zwicker still has something whereby to except against this response. Namely, that ***essence and person in divinis are the same***, so that while God is believed to be one in essence (not three), three in persons (not one), ***according to the same*** God is said to be one and not one, three and not three — since the Theologians themselves confess that ***in divinis the persons differ really among themselves***, but not from the divine essence. The cause the Theologians give is this: the persons are opposed to one another ***relatively***, which opposition always infers a real distinction; but between person and essence no such opposition intercedes; whence that saying of the Theologians: ***Where there is no relative opposition, there is no real distinction***.

#### The Scholastic Response — Essence and Person Virtually Distinguished

The Scholastic Theologians, noting this very point and to meet this difficulty, said that ***essence and person in divinis are not indeed distinguished really, yet are distinguished virtually***, and that this is the cause why the contradiction does not stand *according to the same*, and so no true contradiction is given in saying that God is one in essence and three in persons. This response too some of our own Philosophers and Theologians do not disapprove — the distinction of which an occasion of treating will be given in its proper place.

#### The Preferred Response — Refutation by Inversion

Meanwhile, I rejoice that by men of no vulgar erudition, and by Theologians of great judgment, is approved the mode of responding to Zwicker's argument ***by inversion*** — as the Logicians call it — a mode which also most suitably serves the understanding of those not so intently hunting after ἀκρίβεια (scholastic precision), and by which the ***use*** of our axiom is remarkably illustrated, and the ***abuse*** of it is removed. Namely, ***the Major is simply converted***, and so by the benefit of simple conversion the whole argument is inverted in this manner:

> **Major.** *Whatever falls under salvific faith — that is, can and ought to be believed by salvific faith — that does not implicate most-true and most-manifest contradiction.*
> **Minor.** *But the Mystery of the Most Holy Trinity falls under salvific faith — that is, it can and ought to be believed by salvific faith that God is one in essence, three in persons.*
> **Conclusion.** *Therefore the Mystery of the Most Holy Trinity — namely, that God is one in essence, three in persons — does not implicate most-true and most-manifest contradiction.*

The ***Major*** proposition Zwicker cannot deny, since from the truth of what is simply convertible to the truth of its simple converse the consequence always holds. But the Major proposition in Zwicker's argument was a ***universal negative***, and so simply convertible — and is now simply converted.

This Major can also most easily be proved otherwise. For if something were to fall under salvific faith, and yet simultaneously implicate a most-true and most-manifest contradiction, then that thing would simultaneously be possible (since it falls under faith) and yet not possible, but ***simply and absolutely impossible*** (since it would implicate a most-manifest and most-true contradiction). For whatever implicates a most-manifest and most-true contradiction is absolutely impossible. Therefore the safest way is this: that, with reason taken captive under the obedience of Christ, we insist simply on divine revelation; and that what God has revealed about Himself, and so willed to be believed, we believe — however much ***how*** these things can simultaneously stand our reason cannot attain.

#### Zwicker's Counter — "But Reason Cannot See How They Agree"

Let Zwicker urge: ***yet these things do fight among themselves and contradictorily repugn one another, which our reason cannot see***. It is answered: by no means do these things fight and contradictorily repugn one another which God has revealed, since two contradictorily fighting things cannot simultaneously be true, and therefore cannot simultaneously be revealed by God. For God, because He is the ***immutable Truth*** itself (as in its place will be demonstrated), cannot contradict Himself; and so what God has simultaneously revealed cannot be contradictory.

#### Zwicker's Augustine-Abuse — Reason Preferred to Scripture

Let Zwicker except again: ***That provocation to Sacred Scriptures is altogether useless, if a certain principle of reason has been brought by someone; nay, that he be held most demented, whoever shall have contradicted such reason, and, abandoning common sense, shall have willed to betake himself to the Scriptures***. For by these words Zwicker himself uses, on his page 6, and at the same time abuses this of ***Augustine*** so disputing: *"If to a most manifest and certain reason the authority of the Sacred Scriptures is objected, as it were, he who does this does not understand, and he opposes to truth not the sense of those Scriptures (which he could not penetrate), but rather his own; nor what in them, but what in himself (as it were on their behalf) he finds, does he set over against it. For (that we may see how true this is) the authority of the Scriptures is not necessary, since common sense itself by evident reason proclaims this to be so true, that whoever shall have contradicted shall be held most demented."* Thence Zwicker exclaims on the same and the following page: ***"Therefore, since those acting contrary, as Augustine witnesses, do not understand what they do, nay are held most demented, it follows: (1) that all Trinitarians universally, not Augustine himself excepted, do not understand what they do, nor are sufficiently prudent — even though they also call their sentence a divine mystery, and so provoke to Scripture. For if the Papists most rightly hiss off the sentence of the Calvinists as convicted of contradiction, although these too have called the same thing a mystery and have provoked to Scripture — which the Calvinists likewise rightly do with the Papists, the anti-Lutherans with the Lutherans, and these in turn with the Calvinists, with the Transubstantiator Papists (as they too, abandoning common sense, likewise prattle only of empty mysteries), by hissing off and exploding those contradictory and so impossible mysteries — whence (2) it also appears how greatly all the recalled Sects stand to implicated contradiction. Why therefore should not all down to one Trinitarian be worthy of hissing and explosion, since what they call mysteries 'yet divine and delivered in Scriptures' are contradictory in the common sense?"***

#### Veltheim's Reply — Scripture as Sole Principle; Zwicker's Second Assertion Collapses

For if, most evidently, a true contradiction could be proved — then indeed in the interpretation of Sacred Scripture such a conciliation should by all means be sought, that the contradiction be avoided, since two contradictories cannot be simultaneously true, while divine revelation is infallibly true. ***But those things which God has revealed cannot implicate an evident and true contradiction, as has just been shown***: for if two things evidently contradicting one another were simultaneously revealed, it would have to be admitted that something false had been divinely revealed, and the infallible truth of divine revelation would fall — which is impious to say and most absurd. Meanwhile there is no cause why Zwicker should urge that ***one part of a contradicting pair, convicted of contradiction, is most rightly hissed off and exploded by the other contradicting part***. For either that part was truly convicted of contradiction, or it was not: if the latter, by no right can it be exploded; if the former, that sentence, if truly convicted of contradiction, could not have been revealed by God. It very often happens that Reformed, Papists, Socinians, and other heretics hold a most true sentence revealed by God Himself to be, by their most grave error, convicted of contradiction — and these, in their hissing and exploding, prostitute themselves, and deserve nothing but to be hissed back at. (On these men it is not of this place to look.)

Likewise, Zwicker's other assertion falls of its own accord — the one from which he had not blushed to collect from the former, that ***the dogma concerning the mystery of the Most Holy Trinity, because it is argued of contradiction by him, cannot be held for a dogma divinely revealed***. For who, pray, pondering the matter most accurately with himself, does not advert that Zwicker is wholly bent on this — ***to lead the defenders of the orthodox sentence away from divine revelation***, in which alone the mystery of the Most Holy Trinity solidly rests, and by which Zwicker with his associates comes to be constrained and convicted? For this is the ***solemn and proper device of those men***: to constitute the ***ultimate principle of knowing in reason***, and to admit the contents of Sacred Scripture only if they appear congruent with (Socinian) reason — but if they seem to reason incongruent, to reject them, and not to hold them for such as are contained in Sacred Scripture.

Therefore, by the sentence of the most accurate Theologians, the greatest care is to be applied, and it is to be sedulously watched, that if divine revelation be perspicuous and in all its passages well-constant with itself, we not suffer ourselves to be led away. Ours it then will be strictly and most rigorously to insist on revelation and on the propriety of the words, and never in these matters to admit the judgment of reason concerning contradiction — a judgment drawn from principles taken out of created things — as a rule of good interpretation; especially since matters of faith are hidden from reason and above its grasp (which is why they come under the name of ***mysteries***), and Christ and the Apostles in the New Testament, the Prophets in the Old, have remitted us to Scripture as to the one and adequate principle of what is to be believed, never to human reason. For the rest, Augustine's words neither touch nor pinch the true defenders of the orthodox sentence on the mystery of the Most Holy Trinity. For that cannot be called a ***most manifest and certain reason*** which Sacred Scripture opposes, and the contrary of which it evidently teaches. Nay, whatsoever principles Zwicker and the other Socinians assert for evincing a contradiction in the mystery of the Most Holy Trinity, each and every one are uniquely and solely taken ***from created and finite things***, from which to conclude anything about the infinite essence of God is plainly not permitted, and much less by them can anything evidently be proved about those things which God has revealed concerning Himself and His infinite essence, and the things that are given in His infinite essence.

#### The Minor of the Inverted Syllogism — How the Theologians Prove It

To proceed now to the ***Minor of Zwicker's syllogism, happily inverted***, the plan of our institute does not permit — let it suffice (by leave of the venerable Theologians) only this one thing to touch in passing: that in proof of the Minor the most acute Theologians are wont to proceed thus — namely to prove in antecedence that the ***Father, Son, and Holy Spirit are true persons, and are three really distinct persons among themselves***, and then to form such an argument:

> ***The true God is in essence one God.*** This proposition is sufficiently and most evidently probable both from the light of nature and from Sacred Scripture.
> ***The three really distinct persons — Father, Son, and Holy Spirit — are the true God.*** The several propositions — *the Father is true God, the Son is true God, the Holy Spirit is true God* — the Theologians most firmly and most solidly prove from Sacred Scripture.
> ***Therefore the three really distinct persons — Father, Son, and Holy Spirit — are in essence one God.***

#### Zwicker Baits Meisner — Fuller Treatment Reserved to the *Apparatus Metaphysicae*

This one thing remains in Zwicker's Treatise of Treatises: that on p. 4, in a marvellous manner, he agitates certain Lutherans — and chiefly Meisner — ***who were spontaneously and openly confessing that the Trinity of Persons in one divine essence implicates contradiction in every Philosophy, and yet were unable to advert that by this very fact this sentence is utterly impugned and rejected***. But on this matter below, in our ***Apparatus Metaphysicae***, there will be an occasion of looking at it, and at the same time of vindicating the Blessed Meisner — of whose ***Philosophia Sobria*** part 1, section 4, question 3, *Metaphysica*, Zwicker makes his sport. Let it suffice in the present to have given warning of the following:

#### Three Monitory Points — Scripture, Erring Philosophy, Philosophy *Abstracte* vs. *Concretive*

**(1)** Both Sacred Scripture, and right reason which fittingly teaches from its genuine principles, have ***God as their Author***, from whom nothing but what is true can come. Just as therefore truth cannot be contradictorily opposed to truth, so neither can it come about that what Sacred Scripture asserts right reason denies, and that in it [Scripture] a contradiction be implicated.

**(2)** As often as it happens that some assertion drawn from Philosophy repugns some mystery of the faith, so often is it certain that this assertion is either ***not from true Philosophy, but is the assertion of a Philosopher wandering from right reason***, or is the ***assertion of right reason and true Philosophy ill-understood or incongruently applied***.

**(3)** Philosophy is sometimes taken ***abstractly and in respect of essence***, sometimes ***in respect of existence and concretively***.

#### Meisner Vindicated — First, Philosophy Taken *Abstracte*

**If Philosophy be taken in the ***prior*** mode**, it will be easy to show that the Blessed Meisner was never of the opinion as if the Mystery of the Most Holy Trinity implicated contradiction in Philosophy. For Meisner himself at *Theorem 3, Metaph.* p. 548 § 25 says: ***That which is once truly true, cannot in any place, in any time, AND IN NO DISCIPLINE, be false; much less (as it will be in every Philosophy) false, but is in every place, always, and for all, true, and remains most constantly so.*** And after he had proved this very thing both from the nature of truth and from the Efficient Cause (God), he concludes at last in these words: ***If, therefore, that Philosophical principle "fire naturally burns" be contrary to this Theological utterance "fire naturally does not burn", God will set up contraries. How then will the Truth of God, how the immutability of God, remain safe and unharmed? Is He truthful who utters contradictories? Will each part of the contradictories be true?***

#### Meisner Vindicated — Second, Philosophy Taken *Concretive*

**If Philosophy be taken in the ***posterior*** mode** — that is, ***concretively, or in respect of the subject in which it exists*** — further it should be noted that both Meisner and many other orthodox Theologians (chief among whom are also Martin Chemnitz and Johann Gerhard), by Philosophy taken in this latter mode, commonly understand Philosophy ***as it is applied as a principle and foundation of the dogmas of faith, and a norm of Theological controversies***, such that nothing is held for true unless it quadrates with Philosophical conclusions. And indeed in every Philosophy (Philosophy being so taken) the Mystery of the Most Holy Trinity *does* implicate contradiction — which Zwicker's own Philosophy sufficiently confirms by its luculent example. For instance, these principles:

> *How many persons there are, so many essences; the unity of person prerequires the unity of essence; if person be set up without a singular incommunicable essence, then simultaneously it will be and will not be a person — which is a contradiction; if singular or numerically-one essence be said to be common to three persons, then simultaneously it will be singular or numerically one and will not be — which again manifestly contradicts itself.*

If Philosophy be taken abstractly and in respect of essence, in Philosophy these are most true, since Philosophy absolutely and simply never asserts and nowhere teaches the truth of these principles ***except in so far as they are precisely received and understood of things subject to merely human reason left to itself — namely of created and finite persons and essences***. But whether in the one divine essence (inasmuch as it is infinite) several persons can be given; whether for the plurality of persons in divinis the unity of the divine essence suffices (since this essence is infinite); whether the essence of the divine persons remains numerically one and singular, even though it truly is in several supposits, and so is common to several persons, without any mark of true contradiction; whether, if a singular or numerically one divine essence be said to be common to three persons of the Divinity, it still remains without any true contradiction numerically one and singular — the decision of these questions ***Philosophy taken abstractly and in respect of essence altogether ignores***; whereas revealed Theology proves and most solidly maintains the affirmative of all and each of these questions from the Word of God, which alone is the ***norm, principle, and foundation of these questions to be decided affirmatively***, and from which right reason in man-left-to-himself is to be supplied and perfected.

#### *Nescio* vs. *Scio* — The Socinian Boast

By the same reasoning, if from the Blessed Doctors Meisner and Gerhard (who are flogged beyond their deserving) Zwicker had exacted ***as from Philosophers*** responses to these same questions, we are most certain that each of them would have answered nothing — but in place of a response would have redoubled a single word: ***NESCIO*** (*I do not know*). And this is the response ***of such questions, and of Philosophy regarded in itself and abstractly, and of our Philosophers (qua such), to such questions***. But if from Zwicker you should ask: *What does he respond to the questions just proposed?* — he himself will respond: ***SCIO*** (*I know*) that the negative of each question is most true, and he will not be afraid to call them ***unwise, rude in every Philosophy, most demented and most stupid*** who, as Philosophers, respond to such questions: *Nescio*. The same thing in truth will all the Socinians respond — namely, that that ***Nescio*** is not the response of Philosophers, but ***"of the Ubiquitarian Lutherans"*** (these are the cavillations of Zwicker), and especially ***"of Meisner the Lutheran in his Philosophia Sobria, publicly approved and commended by the Giessen and other Lutheran Theologians"*** (these are Zwicker's words).

Things so standing, I do not wonder that the Blessed Meisner and other orthodox Theologians, in disputing against men of this stamp and their like, called ***their mode of philosophizing alone "Philosophy"*** — because these men hold nothing for true in revealed Theology unless it quadrates with the conclusions of ***so-called*** Philosophy, nor acknowledge any other Philosophy. So no injury is done to them if you say that in their mind ***this one corrupt doctrine*** (which the Blessed Meisner calls *Philosophy*, likewise abusively ***all Philosophy***) is *Philosophy*. You see, kind Reader, on what title the Blessed Meisner can be excused, when in his *Philosophia Sobria* part 1, section 4, question 3, *Metaph.* p. 530, he expressly wrote:

> *That God is three in persons and one in number, in such a way that the nature is really identified with each person while the persons are really distinguished among themselves, implicates contradiction in every Philosophy.*

And on the same page, concluding his proof, he adds this: ***But I will not pile up more such thorny arguments of reason, since from these it is abundantly manifest how incomprehensible, how contrary to EVERY REASON NOT TAKEN CAPTIVE UNDER THE OBEDIENCE OF FAITH*** (NB. here you see, Reader, what the Blessed Meisner understood by *every Philosophy*) ***is the Mystery of the Most Holy Trinity; and so if the Calvinistic principle be pressed*** (NB. by the same and stronger right he could have said: ***if the Socinian principle be pressed, e.g. the Zwickerian***) ***that God does not propose incomprehensible things, and that nothing is to be believed which is against reason, I truly do not see how one can extricate himself and satisfy the adversaries*** (the Anti-Trinitarians).

#### Zwicker's Contradiction-Charge Against Meisner Repelled

But there was no cause why Zwicker should have added the sequels: ***Here Meisner clearly says that very thing which was to be shown by me — namely that the said Mystery of the Trinity is contrary to all reason; and at the same time he presages that none of the Trinitarians sticking in such contradiction will therefore extricate himself, nor ever satisfy the Antitrinitarian adversaries — i.e. those who oppose that contradiction and argue it of lying and impossibility.*** For nowhere did the Blessed Meisner say that the Mystery of the Most Holy Trinity is contrary to ***every*** reason — but to every reason ***not taken captive under the obedience of faith*** — such as pre-eminently is the reason of Zwicker and of all Socinians.

In brief: this is what the Blessed Meisner wills — ***if, abandoning divine revelation, we descend with the Socinians and other Antitrinitarians to their philosophical arguments, and so sink down to merely philosophical controversies, the adversaries will always have something to assert and talk back***. This is the mind of the Blessed Man, which the preceding words sufficiently openly indicate, when he had expressly asserted: ***What is once truly true, in no place, no time, AND IN NO DISCIPLINE, can be false***. Zwicker recognises these words, and ***admits that Meisner here wrote more rightly, affirming yet that Meisner himself contradicted himself***. But in truth no contradiction is given: for when he asserts that in every Philosophy the Mystery of the Most Holy Trinity implicates contradiction, he speaks of Philosophy ***as it is the work of perverse reason***, and is set up by the Socinians as the norm of the mysteries of faith; but when he denies the Mystery of the Trinity to be false in any Philosophy, he speaks of Philosophy ***taken abstractly and in respect of essence*** — and of the Philosophy of those who acknowledge that reason-left-to-itself cannot know, nor have from its own principles whence to determine anything certain concerning the divine subsistences or persons, but that this defect is supplied from revelation, so that reason captive under the obedience of faith holds for certain that no contradiction underlies the Mystery of the Trinity (since otherwise it could not have been so often and so clearly revealed by God, who is the sum of truth).

Nor does it come any longer to be wondered at why the Socinians urge their thrasonical ***SCIO*** and their Philosophy: namely, they take ***those principles of right reason which, when understood of finite and created essences and persons, are always true — but of the infinite essence and uncreated persons by no means proceed*** — as if they were most universal principles valid in divinis equally as in finite things, and as a rule, foundation, and norm of what is to be believed.

#### Closing — So Much for the Abuse of This Axiom

So long therefore as Zwicker, with his own, cherishes this most pestilential error, the Mystery of the Trinity cannot but be ***folly*** to this *animal man*, inasmuch as he does not know how to discern it spiritually. For if he learned to subtract the faculty of judging those things which are of the Spirit from his reason, he would easily see that his ***COMPELLE INTRARE*** is the voice of the Spirit of Darkness, and of men who suffer themselves to be led away from the trail of celestial truth — who, ***thinking themselves alone wise, were made fools and frustrated through their dialogisms***; and not the voice of God, who preserves us in His truth, and converts them if they cease to put an obstacle to the grace of celestial truth.

So much, then, for the ***abuse of this Axiom***, and for the whole of this Axiom.

### Axioma II

> ***Quodlibet est vel non est.***
>
> **Whatever thing you please either is or is not.**

#### Sense of the Axiom

Between true contradictories ***no middle*** is given. Or, which comes to the same: the immutable and perpetual repugnance which is given between true contradictories is ***immediate***. For example: no middle is given between *being* and *non-being*, between *animal* and *non-animal*, between *seeing* and *non-seeing*, between *white* and *non-white*, between *Father* and *non-Father*.

#### Confirmation

This axiom is so cognate to the preceding that you may find Metaphysicians who had rather sell both for plainly one and the same. Yet it is not difficult to show its difference from the former by solid reasons.

**(1)** It must be noted that just as elsewhere a twofold ***first partial or inadequate concept*** of a thing is set up — one ***common*** (as the first concept common to man with brute is *animal*), another ***proper*** (as *rational* is commonly called the first concept proper to man) — so of true contradictories there are likewise given ***two concepts***: one ***common and corresponding to genus***, consisting namely in this, that contradictories ***be repugnant***; another ***proper***, that they ***be immediate***. Hence this argument may be drawn:

> *Whatever axiom expresses the first proper concept of true contradictories — which corresponds to specific difference — altogether differs from that axiom which expresses only the first common concept of true contradictories, which corresponds to genus; and differs from it as posterior from prior.*
>
> *But this axiom — "Quodlibet est vel non est" — expresses the first proper concept of true contradictories, corresponding to specific difference; whereas that axiom — "Impossibile est idem simul esse et non esse" — expresses only the first common concept, corresponding to genus.*
>
> *Therefore this axiom "Quodlibet est vel non est" altogether differs from that axiom "Impossibile est idem simul esse et non esse", and differs from it as posterior from prior.*

The ***Major*** is plain, because the first common concept and the first proper concept are distinct concepts: for the common differs from the proper, and is prior to the proper. The ***Minor*** is easy of perception, if we only call back to mind the sense of each axiom.

**(2)** This reason also stands: ***If, per impossibile, we posit this axiom "Quodlibet est vel non est" to be false, still the prior axiom "Impossibile est idem simul esse et non esse" would remain most true.*** Whence again it is lawful to collect: Whatever axiom still remains true if per impossibile the present axiom were false, from that axiom the present axiom certainly differs. But the axiom *Impossibile est idem simul esse et non esse* still would remain true, if by the uttermost absurdity our present axiom *Quodlibet est vel non est* were posited to be false. Therefore from it the present axiom certainly differs.

The ***Major*** needs no proof, since it is impossible that the same axiom be simultaneously true and false. The ***Minor*** is thus confirmed: If, per impossibile, true contradictories were given that nevertheless were ***not immediate*** but ***mediate***, then indeed this proposition — *Quodlibet est vel non est* — would be false; yet still that proposition — *Impossibile est idem simul esse et non esse* — would remain true. For ***from denial of the middle the consequence does not hold to denial of repugnance***. For all the remaining classes of true opposites acknowledge a middle, of which both opposites can be denied (what is commonly called the ***medium negationis***); and contradictories alone are plainly ***immediate***. For instance: *seeing* and *blind* are *privatively* opposed; *white* and *black* ***contrarily*** opposed; *father* and *son* ***relatively*** opposed. And because these are mediate opposites, it is false if you say: *Whatever is seeing or blind, white or black, father or son* — for a red wall, for instance, is neither seeing nor blind, neither white nor black, neither father nor son. Yet it remains most true that *impossibile est idem simul esse videns & cœcum, album & nigrum, patrem & filium* — namely according to the same, in order to the same, in the same mode, and at the same time. Why, with the prior being false, the posterior assertion remains most true, the reason is this: that the remaining opposites, though not lacking a middle, nevertheless ***do speak repugnance***.

**(3)** The ***axiom first of all*** — *Impossibile est idem simul esse et non esse* — cannot be placed in the ***demonstration itself***, even if it be restricted to certain matter, but ***only in the conclusion***, and this in such wise:

> *Animal is substance, and is not non-substance. Man is animal. Therefore man is substance, and is not non-substance.*

(Here the axiom restricted to a certain subject enters the conclusion, and is added by contradiction to the major extreme.) But the ***present axiom*** — *Quodlibet est vel non est* — can also enter the demonstration itself, if it be accommodated to certain matter: e.g.

> *The diameter is either commensurable with the side, or incommensurable. But it is not commensurable* — for the side responds to an even number, the diameter to odd; whence if the diameter were commensurable with the side, an even number would be commensurable with an odd, which is absurd. ***Therefore incommensurable.***

**Having now shown the difference of the present axiom from the prior**, we confirm the axiom itself thus: ***Because it is impossible for the same simultaneously to be and not to be, therefore whatever thing you please either is or is not.*** For if something so behaved as not to be or not to be, but as ***to be and not to be*** (whether the speech be about *to be* and *not to be* simply, or about such-and-such *to be* or *not to be* — e.g. if a red wall were neither seeing nor non-seeing, white nor non-white, father nor non-father), then it would not be impossible that the same simultaneously be and not be.

Confirmation can also be made in this manner: ***Whatsoever cannot simultaneously be and not be, that either is or is not. But whatever thing cannot simultaneously be and not be — that is, it is impossible that the same thing simultaneously be and not be. Therefore whatever thing either is or is not.***

#### Demonstration per Absurdum et Impossibile

The denier of the infallible and immutable truth of this axiom can be reduced to absurdity by this syllogism:

> *Whatever thing so is that it is not, that thing can simultaneously be and not be.*
> *But some thing so is that it is not.*
> *Therefore some thing can simultaneously be and not be.*

The ***conclusion*** is most absurd and simply impossible, since it is simply impossible and most absurd that the same simultaneously be and not be. Hence, since the form of the syllogism was best, either the Major will be false or the Minor. Not the Major, as one whose truth to anyone of sound forehead is clear from the terms. Therefore the Minor is simply impossible and most absurd.

The syllogism leading to the absurd and impossible can also be formed thus:

> *Whatsoever is impossible to be and not to be simultaneously, that is or is not.*
> *But some thing neither is nor is not.*
> *Therefore it is not impossible that some thing simultaneously be and not be.*

The ***conclusion*** is most absurd and simply impossible; the form is best; the Major most manifest. Therefore the Minor will be most absurd.

#### Practical Use

The amplest use of this axiom it will be permitted to illustrate by one or another example from the ***practical disciplines*** and the ***higher Faculties***, as they are commonly called.

#### The Moral Example — Grotius Defended Again

So, for example, the Moral sentence that ***voluntary acts are given, just by their very nature and per se — that is, antecedently to law, in so far as law denotes the decree of a superior, by which it obliges its subject to compose its actions after that superior's prescript*** — we prove, among other arguments, by the following:

> *Either God is from His nature just, or He is not from His nature just. If He is not from His nature just, He is not God — because all things which are in God are God Himself, and no one but an atheist will deny that God is properly and par excellence just, perpetually and immutably, and so by absolute and intrinsic necessity just (for what sort of God would He be if, properly speaking, He were not the just avenger of crimes and the most equitable rewarder of good deeds?). If He is from His nature just, then there will be given, from His nature, something just — namely, from His nature, per se and intrinsically, and so by absolute necessity, just. For God is just in His actions. Therefore there will be given a just act apart from reference to law, if law be taken for the decree of a superior by which he obliges his subject to compose his actions after his prescript.*

**You retort:** *"But I do not accept the consequence: 'if the supreme legislator is just by nature, therefore acts of rational creature, taken for physical motions, are honest and base before all reference to law issued by the Creator.' For my proposition was to inquire into the ***origin of human justice***, from which the divine differs in many ways. Nor indeed was this ***our*** collection, which you by ignorance or malice feign to us."*

Our argument comes to this: ***If God is from His nature just, it follows that acts are given per se, by their nature and by absolute necessity just, apart from reference to a decree issuing from a superior by which he had bound his subject; and so it will stand most true that just acts are given common to God with men — which, inasmuch as they are common to God with men, cannot speak reference to the decree of a superior, since God has no one as His superior.***

Indeed, it could have seemed to someone as if that reason — just now adduced — did not strike the man who expressly protests that he is not disputing about ***acts of God***, but only about those which are referred among the duties of a rational and social animal, in that it is deprived of its sting by this hypothesis: ***"Our proposition was to inquire into the origin of human justice, from which the divine differs in many ways."*** But in truth, whosoever first persuaded this to himself (so, I believe, it was), and then imposed it on others — let him lead himself and others into error.

That the ***kind Reader*** advert this, I would wish him to call back to memory the first occasion of this controversy. Grotius, in that signal work ***De Jure Belli ac Pacis***, had defended that acts are given, just by their nature, per se and by absolute necessity — whose justice stands antecedently to law, taken for the decree of a superior by which he obliges his subject to compose his actions after his prescript. On this the common opinion had consented, and this sentence was approved by Philosophers, Jurists, Theologians. There ***rose up against the dead Grotius a bitter adversary***, who gloried that he had convicted not Grotius alone, but most also of our Theologians, Jurists, and Philosophers who follow this sentence, of ***the utmost stolidity, of horrible absurdities, even of ψευδοφιλία (false-philosophy)***. In order to show that an injury sufficiently manifest and atrocious was being done to Grotius and so many and so great men, I willed, among other arguments for Grotius's sentence, that this reason also be at hand:

> *If God is from His nature just, it follows that acts are given per se and by their nature just, whose justice namely stands antecedently to the decree of a superior by which he obliges his subject. For whatever just acts can be divided into acts of God and acts of rational creature, these just acts qua such do not speak reference to the law issued by the Creator — that is, to the decree by which He as superior willed to oblige His subjects. For otherwise God would have Himself as His superior, and the decree of a superior would be given common to God with men — which both are impious and most absurd.*

Now let anyone judge, who is free from preconceived opinions and endowed with a sounder judgment, whether this reason is deprived of its sting by the hypothesis of the adversary, saying that his proposition is ***to inquire only into the origin of human justice, from which the divine differs in many ways***. Clearly Grotius's proposition was not ***precisely to inquire into the origin of human justice***, but he inquired into the ***genuine and solid foundation of acts per se and by their nature and by absolute necessity just, whether they proceed from God or from a rational creature*** — as to anyone inspecting the Prolegomena to Grotius's work, and especially *l*. 1 c. 1 § 10 § 11 n. 5, and of the work itself book one chapter one, chapter ten number six, and book two chapter one, chapter twenty-six, abundantly stands clear. I think it needs no ingenious wit to understand how empty and bald this exception of the adversary is: ***"it is not of my purpose to deal with acts of God, but with acts of human conduct"*** — and therefore by the greatest right I called the adversary most-stolid, and that his sentence be most stolid, blasphemous, impious, since (**when he** at *lib. 2 c. 11 § 4* defends — after he had provoked to the divine oracles, which teach that God Himself, who can be bound by no established law, will act against His own nature unless He fulfill promises, and had heaped up to this end the dicta of the Sacred Code, Old and New Testament — that promises are kept, as coming from the nature of ***immutable justice which God has in common with men***; and when at *lib. 1 c. 15* he made mention of a ***norm which is common to certain acts of God with certain acts of rational creature***, in such wise that the agreement of certain acts both of God and of rational creature with such norm ***is properly justice***, and God Himself according to this norm therefore in many places of Sacred Scripture — alleged by Grotius there — suffers Himself to be judged.

#### The *Animal–Man* and *Substance–God* Analogies

It pleases me to make the matter clear by a crude example, since the adversary simulates so great a stupor. Let someone say that ***the nature of animal — animality, so to call it — stands antecedently to man***. Let another call this sentence most-stolid. The first urges this reason:

> *If animal is the genus of man as species, it follows that it stands antecedently to man, because genus and nature is prior to its species, and is really abstracted from its species, and can be divided into this and another species.*

Here, lest he be stolidly said to have argued the adversary of foolishness, let him except: ***My proposition was to deal with animal only in so far as and insofar as it is man.***

We will give another example. Let someone teach ***that substance stands antecedently to God and created substance***. Let another call this sentence most-stolid. The first proves his sentence by this reason:

> *If the concept of substance rightly and properly so-called is common to God with created substance — e.g. with angel, animal, etc. — it follows that substance stands antecedently to uncreated substance (that is, God) and created substance, because the common is naturally prior to those to whom it is common.*

The other, lest he be held to confess his own foolishness, prattles that his proposition was to deal with ***created substance***.

It will not need too acute a judgment to understand that the disputant on each side ***stolidly fights with his own shadow*** (the words are not mine), and that one who ***divides animal into man and brute (substance into uncreated and created), yet denies that animal stands antecedently to man and brute*** (substance antecedently to uncreated and created) — and one who is bound to acknowledge that just actions, properly so-called, are rightly divided into acts of God (uncreated) and acts of rational creature (created) — ***is forced also to acknowledge that acts just in and per se and by their nature are given which are common to God with men***. The same man acknowledges that there is no law unless it be the decree of a superior binding its subject; nor can he not acknowledge that a decree of a superior by which God be obliged as a subject cannot be given. Yet he denies that the justice of certain acts common to God with men, ***if that common justice be regarded in itself***, can be regarded before all reference to the decree of a superior binding a subject.

#### The Promise-Keeping Example — Justice *qua* Rational Agent Comes Prior

For the rest, one who regards God as just-by-His-own-nature does not at once regard Him as the supreme legislator-just-by-nature. For example, when God is regarded as keeping a promise, He is regarded as just by His nature, but not as supreme legislator. For that God keep a promise is not that God bear a law, nor that God in another way discharge the office of legislator. ***More widely also lies open the just act of a rational agent qua such, than the just act of a rational creature*** — as Grotius noted; nor can it be denied by a man of sounder judgment that ***to keep a promise, as it abstracts from divine and human promise, is just before all reference to a superior and his decree***.

Therefore, when the adversary pronounces that reason — ***If God is just from His nature, it follows that acts are given per se just*** — similar to this: ***"If the supreme legislator is from His nature just, therefore acts of a rational creature, considered as physical motion, are honest and base before all reference to a law issued by the Creator"*** — I leave to him the option whether he prefer his stupor marked, or the stingers of insults.

Likewise, no one has ever denied that in many ways divine justice differs from human justice. Yet the ***formal concept of justice properly so-called*** remains common to divine and human justice — just as the concept of *animal* is common to man and ass, the concept of *substance* common to God and created substance — although man from ass, and God from created substance, differ in many ways.

#### The *Twice-Served Cabbage* — The Second Edition's Answer

The same censure undergoes the ***second edition***, which the adversary himself calls ***cramben bis adpositam*** (twice-served cabbage), where these words are read:

> *"So Veltheim reasons: 'If God is from His nature just, it follows that acts are given per se honest and by their nature owed.' But I truly think there is here no consequence from God to man. For from a plainly different principle is it, that God is said to be unable to do certain things or unable not to do them, than from that whence a man is said to have to do some things and omit others. In God this comes from the perfection of His essence; in man, from the empire of a superior. By which very fact fall the machines which the Same strives to build up against my sentence from pp. 18, 19. For in God, to lie or to deceive by oath is impossible on account of the perfection of divine essence; among men moreover, lies and false oaths are to be shunned on account of natural law, because without the sanctity of these the society between men cannot be safe."*

I drew ***no consequence from God to man***, but with Grotius and those who defend his sentence, I argued from ***acts agreeing with the rational agent qua such, per se and by their nature and by absolute necessity, to acts just common to God with men considered antecedently to the decree of a superior by which he obliges his subject***. For since acts of God agree with acts of rational creature — if these be precisely considered on the part of that in which acts proceeding immediately from God and from rational creature agree — they cannot speak reference to the decree of a superior that would be common to God with men.

#### The *Sentient-Animal* Analogy — Rudely Set Out

Let us take a crude example, in favour of him who affects crudeness. If anyone should argue thus:

> *If man qua animal is sentient, is appetitive, it follows that animal per se is sentient, per se is appetitive, and before all reference to rationality; and that the "sentient" is naturally prior to the rational and irrational, man and brute; and so brute is equally sentient as man, although it speaks no reference to the "rational". The prior is true. Therefore the posterior.*

Would any man of sound mind here think the consequence to be ***from man to brute***? For the argumentation is not instituted thus: *If man is sentient, brute is sentient*. Rather, just as ***animal qua such*** is sentient, is appetitive, so are given voluntary acts which qua such are agreeing with rational nature, with the rational agent qua such. Just as it is ***prior that animal be sentient, than that animal be rational or irrational***, so is it ***prior that an act agree with rational nature, than that an act be agreeing with the divine or the created rational nature*** — since each nature is rational. ***To keep a promise agrees with God on account of the perfection of the divine essence*** (NB. inasmuch as that perfection is of moral consideration, and belongs to Moral treatment — of which below, in that part of our Institutes which we shall call Natural Theology, we shall deal).

And just as it does not follow — *man is a rational animal, docile; therefore man cannot be regarded as animal before all reference to rationality and docility* — so there is no consequence: ***"Many acts man cannot omit on account of the empire of a superior; many acts, after God freely assigned to man a rational nature, cannot not agree with the rational nature freely assigned by God to him. Therefore, that such acts be committed by man (e.g. that promises be kept) in no way comes from the nature of immutable justice, which to God and to all these that use reason is in its own mode common. Therefore that justice cannot be regarded before all reference to the decree of a superior."***

#### The *Blasphemous* Charge Reversed; Closing of the Moral Example

But to shake out this matter and the trifles of the adversary's part more pressingly, since calling-stolid is yoked to it: ***I call blasphemous him who*** says that any act is-in and per se and by its nature or by absolute necessity honest or just to man in this sense — ***that at the same time he denies that man is bound to that act by the empire of a superior***. But him who says that Grotius, most of our Theologians, Jurists and Philosophers thus either taught, or teach, or that from their sentence (when they taught a certain justice, likewise justice as abstracted from divine and human, likewise as common to certain acts both of God and of rational creatures, ***to have itself antecedently to the decree of a superior, nay even antecedently to the right judgment of God***) there follows either Stoic fate, or some other besides God who produced the forms of things — I say such a one is either ***notably unlearned***, nay stupid, or at least ***improbable***.

If anyone is troubled by these words — ***justice considered antecedently to the decree of a superior*** — let him erase them, by my leave. For lest I be deceived or cavilled at, I have written nothing for a man so unskilled or improbable as cannot or will not take this or a similar term.

So much for the Moral example.

#### The Political Example — Majority Rule in Aristocracies and Democracies

A **Political example** we shall touch in passing. Let the question be: *In an Aristocratic or Democratic legal state, does the ***major part*** have the right of the whole, if the major and minor parts dissent from each other?* Or (what comes to the same): *in the Aristocracies and Democracies which are given, is there some right in the society of the major part, and do the votes of the major part prevail?*

I proceed thus:

> *Either there is some reason for expediting affairs in it, or there is not. Not the latter — for it must altogether be believed to have been the will of those entering into society that there be some reason for expediting affairs, since otherwise the society would have been entered into in vain. Not the former alternative without consequences either — for if there is a reason for expediting affairs, either there will be ***no empire of associates upon one another*** in those matters where there is place for dissensions and contradictions, or there will be some ***right of a part over a part***. The ***prior*** repugns the nature of society — for nothing is more pernicious to human society than ***anarchy***, which cannot long consist anywhere, where each one undertakes to perpetrate everything according to his own arbitrium; and contradictories cannot simultaneously be done. Therefore, the ***posterior*** standing: either there will be a right of the minor part over the major, or of the major over the minor. Not of the minor over the major, for it is manifestly iniquitous that the major part follow the minor: from this it would be collected that ***inferiors can naturally command superiors*** — which is utterly absurd. ***That also is ratified and prevails more, which the greater number seem to approve*** (witness Aristotle, Politics VI c. 2). Therefore ***naturally, with pacts and laws (which impose a form on the handling of affairs) set aside, the major part has the right of the whole***.*

The great men: **Hugo Grotius**, *De Jure Belli & Pacis* II c. 5 § 17; and **Hermann Conring** in his most accurate *Dissertation political on Subjection and Empire*.

#### The Theological Example — The Perspicuity of Scripture in Things Necessary for Salvation

We wish ***Theological examples*** also to be at hand. There is a controversy between our Theologians and the Papists concerning the ***perspicuity of Sacred Scripture*** in those things which are necessary for knowledge for eternal salvation; and indeed a ***double question*** comes into controversy: one ***de iure*** (whether Sacred Scripture, in those things which are necessary for knowledge for eternal salvation, ***ought to be*** perspicuous); the other ***de facto*** (whether Sacred Scripture, in those things which are necessary for knowledge for eternal salvation, is ***in act*** perspicuous).

**The affirmative of the prior question**, orthodox Theologians prove, among other reasons, thus:

> *Either Sacred Scripture ought to be infallible, or it ought not. If not, it is not Sacred Scripture — because Sacred Scripture is the Word of God, and the Word of God ought to be infallible: for if it ought not be infallible, it could be fallacious; and so either God could have been deceived (which fights with God's infinite knowledge), or He could deceive (which repugns God's infinite goodness). If it ought [to be infallible], ***it ought also to be perspicuous***, since by obscure Scripture we could easily have been led into error.*

**The affirmative of the posterior question** (the question of fact, whose weightier reason our Theologians judge should be had) they prove against the Papists by this reasoning, among several other arguments:

> *Either God through Sacred Scripture willed to teach us perspicuously what is necessary to be believed for eternal salvation, or He did not will. If He did not will, then Sacred Scripture deceives — for the words of Sacred Scripture have it thus:*
>
> ***"These things have been written that you may believe that Jesus is the Christ, the Son of God, and that believing you may have life in His name"*** *(John 20:31). Likewise:* ***"Search the Scriptures, for in them you think to yourselves to have eternal life; and these are they which testify of me"*** *(John 5:39).*
>
> *(To pass over other Scripture-dicta which most luculently testify that God seriously desires the salvation of men, and wills to teach us perspicuously the way of arriving at eternal salvation.) What Sacred Scripture expressly testifies cannot not be of infallible truth. If the former — i.e. if God willed through Sacred Scripture perspicuously to teach what is necessary to be believed for eternal salvation — then either God ***could*** perspicuously hand down in Sacred Scripture the things necessary to be believed for eternal salvation, or He could not. If He could not, He is not the true God — which is impious. Whence rightly ***Hilary***, De Trinitate VIII:* ***"Was He, who is virtue, in such weakness that He could not speak forth what He wished to be understood?"*** *To which a similar passage in* ***Lactantius***, lib. VI c. 12. *If He could — and simultaneously, as has been shown, He willed to teach us perspicuously by His Word the way of arriving at eternal salvation — surely Sacred Scripture altogether, in those things which are necessary for knowledge for eternal salvation, will be perspicuous. For He who wills to teach us perspicuously the way of arriving at eternal salvation, and is also such a perfect master of speaking that He can teach perspicuously, has taught us that way perspicuously.*

Others also proceed thus:

> *Either the Papists affirm that there can be shown a dogma necessary for knowledge for salvation which can be confirmed by no perspicuous testimony of Sacred Scripture, or they deny it. If they deny, then it is left that Sacred Scripture in those things which are necessary for knowledge for eternal salvation is perspicuous. If they affirm, the burden of proof lies on the Papists; but by experience they will learn (nay, they long ago have learned) how difficult (that is, utterly impossible) it is to show a dogma necessary for eternal salvation which can be confirmed by no perspicuous Scripture testimony. And indeed our Theologians stand ready to confirm each single dogma — both of things to be believed and of things to be done — by perspicuous testimonies of Sacred Scripture. Nor however do they wish ***consequences*** to be entirely removed and excluded: for what is deduced by necessary and evident consequence from a proposition revealed in perspicuous words is equally perspicuous with it, equally certain with it.*

#### The Juridical Examples — Three Cases from the Digest

Nor are ***Juridical examples*** at all lacking.

Perhaps a perspicuous and easy example is furnished by ***L. Si inter me & te*** 15, D. *de exceptione rei iudicatae*. The words of the law run thus:

> *"If there was a controversy between me and you concerning an inheritance, and you possess certain things from the same, I others, nothing prevents me from suing you and you me in turn concerning the inheritance. If after this it begins to be judged, it matters whether it has been pronounced that the inheritance is mine, or the contrary. If it be pronounced to be mine, the exception of res iudicata will harm you — EX IPSO, QUO MEAM ESSE PRONUNTIATUM EST, EX DIVERSO TUAM NON ESSE PRONUNTIATUM VIDETUR. If, however, mine not to be, in like manner is understood to have been judged: QUIA DUM AJUNT MEAM NON ESSE, HOC IPSUM AJUNT TUAM ESSE."*

Add ***L. Pomponius*** 40, D. *de Procuratoribus*, where among other things is noted: *"When it is judged that a thing is mine, it is simultaneously judged that it is not another's."* The Jurists of most eminent name demonstrate the mind of the law and simultaneously the use of our proposed axiom in this way:

> *Either the thing is mine (which, after res iudicata, you began to sue from me), or it is not mine. If it is not mine, why by sentence has it been pronounced that the thing is mine? If it is mine, either simultaneously it is yours, or it is not yours. If it is yours, how could it be mine? — since two cannot be lords of the same thing in solidum, nor can two possess the same thing in solidum (**L. si certo loco** 5, **L. si duobus** 25, D. Commodati; and **L. possideri** 3, **L. ex contrario** 5, D. de acquirenda possessione). If it is not yours, by no right can you sue it.*

Another ***everyday example*** Cicero and Boëthius furnish in the *Topics*. A husband bequeathed to his wife the ***usufruct of his goods***; he had wine, oil, and other things which are consumed by use. The question is asked: ***are these owed to the wife?*** And thus they decide:

> *Either usufruct properly consists in wine, oil, and those other things which are consumed by use, or it does not consist. If it does consist, usufruct will not be ***usufruct***, but rather ***abusus***. If it does not consist, therefore these are not owed to the wife in the present case.*

Add ***L. constituitur*** 2, Institutes *de usufructu*.

Again, if a frequent occasion is otherwise given to Doctors and Interpreters of Civil Law to prove and commend the use of this axiom, a beautiful one — unless all things deceive me — is furnished by these words of ***L. aut aut, aut ille*** 124, D. *de verborum significatione*:

> *"These words — ILLE AUT ILLE — are not only ***disjunctive***, but also ***subdisjunctive*** speech. Disjunctive, as when we say* ***AUT DIES AUT NOX EST*** *— where, with one posited, it is necessary that the other be removed; likewise, with one removed, the other to be posited. In the same manner, a verb can be subdisjunctive. There are two kinds of subdisjunctives:*
>
> *One, when of the proposed ends ***both cannot be, yet both can be absent***, as when we say* ***AUT SEDET AUT AMBULAT*** *— for just as no one can do both simultaneously, so someone can do neither, as he who reclines at table.*
>
> *The other kind is when of the proposed ends ***thus neither can not be, that both can be***, as when we say* ***OMNE ANIMAL AUT FACIT AUT PATITUR*** *— for there is nothing which neither acts nor suffers; but one can simultaneously act and suffer."*

(b) The **Theological example** — perspicuity of Scripture in things necessary for salvation, the double question *de iure* / *de facto*: (de iure) Scripture-is-Word-of-God → must be infallible → must be perspicuous (else God could be deceived or deceive, repugning His infinite knowledge/goodness); (de facto) John 20:31 and John 5:39 + Hilary *De Trinitate* VIII and Lactantius VI.12's *si potuit* dilemma; the Papist burden-shift: either admit no dogma lacks perspicuous Scripture-confirmation, or bear the proof — and necessary-and-evident consequence from a perspicuously-revealed proposition is equally perspicuous and equally certain. (c) The **three Juridical examples**: (1) *L. Si inter me & te* 15 D. *de except. rei iudicatae* + *L. Pomponius* 40 D. *de Procuratoribus* — the res-iudicata either/or (if mine → not yours; if not mine → yours; cross-references to D. commodati and D. de acquirenda possessione); (2) The Cicero/Boëthius *Topics* usufruct case — wife bequeathed usufruct of consumable goods (wine/oil): either usufruct consists in them (then it's *abusus* not *ususfructus*) or not (then not owed); cross to *Institutes* de usufructu; (3) *L. aut aut, aut ille* 124 D. *de verborum significatione* — the Roman grammatical distinction between *disjunctive* (AUT DIES AUT NOX — both possible negated) and two kinds of *subdisjunctive* (AUT SEDET AUT AMBULAT = both-not-simultaneously but both-can-be-absent; OMNE ANIMAL AUT FACIT AUT PATITUR = none-neither but both-can-be). T18 opens the **Abusus Practicus of Axioma II** — three abuses in particular: (1) treating as *immediate oppositions* what are *mediate* (the first and most to-be-detested; the Moral example of *omnis ignorantia invincibilis actionem reddit involuntariam* — the ajens vs. negans with the common initial-trap syllogism); (2) and (3) to follow.]*

#### Practical Abuse

A ***threefold abuse*** of this axiom in particular is given.

#### The First Abuse — Treating Mediate Oppositions as Immediate

The ***first abuse***, worthy of animadversion before the rest, is when very many hold for ***immediate opposites*** those things which are ***not immediate opposites***. For it cannot otherwise come about than that these stick in errors, as long as they cannot or will not change the sentence of their mind — and so much the more do they prostitute themselves, the more acerbly or impudently they urge the ***immediate opposition*** where they rashly believe it to be, when yet it is not.

Let us see one or two examples of the matter.

#### The Hunter's Invincible Ignorance — Voluntary / Involuntary / Non-Voluntary

In Morals it is asked: ***Does every invincible ignorance render an action involuntary?*** So it may seem to beginners, who commonly defend the ***affirmative*** of the question and impose it upon themselves by this reasoning:

> *Every action which proceeds from an agent labouring under invincible ignorance is either voluntary to such an agent, or involuntary. A third cannot be given. But it is not voluntary to him. For whatever action is voluntary to an agent proceeds from the agent's free arbitrium or will. But that which was not, nor ought to have been, known to the agent — or what comes to the same, that of which the agent laboured under invincible ignorance — could not come to be by the agent's will, since the will is never borne to the unknown, by that saying* ***"Ignoti nulla cupido."***

These things may be said to beginners and those like them. The contrary can easily be evinced by one single case:

> *A hunter, going out at first dawn into the forest, kills by chance a man whom he neither knew, nor ought to have known, nay nor could have known, to be lurking there — so that it is certain the slayer laboured under invincible ignorance. The disquisition comes: ought this slaying be called ***involuntary*** to the slayer?*

We say: nothing can be answered to this question by the one asking, because he silently passes over a circumstance which contributes most greatly to the decision of this question, and without knowledge of which the decision is awaited in vain. Namely, it is necessary first to know: ***Would the hunter, absent invincible ignorance, have been going to do the same, or not?*** Or, to use terms received in the Moral forum: ***Was that invincible ignorance antecedent, or concomitant?*** Or (as pertains to the present case): was that invincible ignorance ***ἀκούσιον*** (*unwilling*) or ***οὐχ ἑκούσιον*** (*not-willing*)? — which is the Greeks' distinction.

With deliberate study we have been silent about this circumstance, which we now determine: namely, that invincible ignorance in the given example was ***concomitant*** and ***ἀκούσιον***. For the hunter would have been going to do the same, had he known his enemy to be lurking there — the enemy to whom he had long since threatened ultimate harm, and whose slaying, when he sees it has befallen, he congratulates himself on the event — not responding to a recent attempt, yet exactly responding to a long-standing wish.

With this circumstance duly perceived, he must be rude in every practical doctrine who cannot acknowledge that this slaying was not involuntary to the hunter here and now. Let us repeat the same example (which at first sight these or those may have treated as diverse from the prior), yet so repeat it that the circumstance which earlier in the state of the question was suppressed is now had in express words added:

> *Either that hunter in the given case acted ***according to*** his will, or ***against*** his will. A third is not given. But he did not act against his will (as is plain from what has just been deduced). Therefore he acted according to his will. Therefore invincible ignorance will be given which leaves the action voluntary.*

This decision forensic practice seems to confirm, since a slayer of this kind, caught and convicted, is punished. ***But truly, there was leapt from one extreme to the other*** — as if a third could not be given between what is done by a rational agent *according to his will* and *against his will*. That the hunter in such a case by no means acted against his will, is most true, and the reason given no longer allows us to doubt: nay, as *"of the unknown no desire,"* so of the same there is no aversion — for the same reason that obtains on both sides. But that reason also was solid, by which in the immediately preceding ratiocination it was evinced that the action was not voluntary to the hunter in question — that is, by which locution the same is signified: ***the hunter did not act according to his will***. Therefore the hunter in the adduced case neither acted *according to his will*, nor acted *against his will*, but (which is the ***middle***) ***apart from his will*** (*praeter voluntatem*). Nor is it any obstacle that that hunter caught and convicted is punished. For he is not punished as the ***voluntary cause*** of the slaying — because the slaying was by no means voluntary to him — but ***on account of the ex-post-facto (as they speak) willed slaying***, or more accurately speaking, on account of the ***voluntary approbation*** of the non-voluntary slaying, is he affected with penalty.

Therefore ***non-voluntary*** lies open more widely than ***involuntary***: between *voluntary* and *involuntary* a middle is given, as the present case teaches; but between *voluntary* and *non-voluntary* no middle is given. The present case argues an example of a fact neither *voluntary* nor *involuntary*, yet ***non-voluntary***.

#### The Second Moral Example — The Opponent's *Produced-from-Itself vs. Produced-by-God* Dilemma

Another Moral example it pleases to adduce, and that in fewest words. Since in the explication of the preceding axiom (where we indicated the practical abuse by a Moral example) it was shown with what acerbity the sentence is contradicted whereby ***Morality*** can be considered to have itself antecedently to the will of God as binding His subject — let us see whether the adversary's reason by which he impugns that sentence does not itself offer the example of a notable ***abuse of the present axiom***. His reason is this:

> *Morality considered as having itself antecedently to the will of God binding its subjects, is either produced from itself, or is not produced from itself. A third is not given. If it is produced from itself, therefore it will be the cause of itself; therefore a being-from-itself or independent besides God is given. If it is not produced from itself, either it will be produced by God, or it will not be produced by God. If it is produced by God, therefore it cannot have itself antecedently to the will of God, because nothing is produced by God unless He has decreed to produce it. If it is not produced by God, therefore another is given who besides God has produced it. Who then besides God formed the natures of things?*

It is answered **first**: ***a third is given here***. For when morality is considered as having itself antecedently to the will of God binding His subjects, we treat of morality ***as a possibility*** — that is, of the necessary habitude (agreement or disagreement) of voluntary acts with rational nature *qua* such (as it is abstracted from uncreated and created). Nor is it considered as ***produced or to be produced***, but precisely as ***possible***, and thus and not otherwise possible. The matter stands here just as if the man who used so jejune a reasoning had wished to argue thus:

> *If animal can be considered as having itself antecedently to man and brute, as having itself antecedently to every generation, then either animal having itself antecedently to man and brute will be produced from itself, or it will not be produced from itself, but by another.*

Ridiculous and plainly inept would have been this collection. For when animal is considered in the common and ***qua*** such, in vain is it asked concerning its production — because there is no question of ***existence*** (which is of a singular thing: for whatever exists, by that very fact that it exists, is singular; but the term of production is ***existence***, not ***possibility*** — of which there was treated at length in the preceding axiom). ***Possibility*** neither God nor another formed, nor did it fashion itself.

**Secondly**, the morality of acts of God — that is, the real agreement of God's acts with rational nature qua such, which, because it is of moral consideration, can indeed be denominated *morality*, inasmuch as it is given in act — does not depend upon the will of God binding Himself as a subject.

**Thirdly**, the morality of acts of rational creature, inasmuch as it is given in act, if it be precisely considered as it agrees in the being of morality with the morality of acts of God, altogether has itself antecedently to the will of a superior.

**Fourthly**, a wonder if the contradictor should not in the end set up ***every baseness as produced by God***. More things on this matter may be seen from the preceding axiom.

#### The Theological Example — The Arian & Photinian Attack on the Trinity

By a ***Theological example*** we shall demonstrate the abuse of this axiom. The ***Arians and Photinians*** strive to impugn the eternal production of the second and third persons of the Divinity — and so the divinity of the Son and Holy Spirit — among others by the following argument:

> *If the Son and Holy Spirit were produced from eternity, either they were produced ***from some subject*** or ***in dependence on a subject***, or ***from nothing***. A third is not given. If the former, they are not true God, because true God is immaterial: but to be produced from a subject is to be produced from matter — whence whoever is produced from a subject must be material. If the latter, then they will be a creature, because to be produced from nothing is to be ***created***.*

So the Antitrinitarians abuse our axiom. And indeed thus it is, if anyone could suffer himself to be persuaded that between ***production from a subject (or dependent on a subject)*** and ***production from nothing*** no third production is given — he could easily slide into the most pestilential error of the Antitrinitarians. For this is more certain than certain: whatever is produced in either of these two ways (i.e. either from some subject, or from nothing) is not true and eternal God, but creature.

But Students of Theology, instructed in better and salutary things, know a ***third mode of production***: namely, ***that the one produced be of the same numerical substance as the one producing***. For orthodox Theologians by unanimous consent teach, and from Sacred Scripture demonstrate by arguments beyond every exception, that:

> *God the Father produced the Son from His substance from eternity; the Father and the Son produced the Holy Spirit from Their substance from eternity — so that both are produced ***of the substance of the one producing***: the Son, namely, of the substance of the Father; the Holy Spirit of the substance of the Father and the Son.*

Not that the ***substance or divine essence*** be a material principle, from which the Son and Holy Spirit have been produced — but because the Father by generation communicated His substance or essence to the Son, and the Father and Son by spiration communicated Theirs to the Holy Spirit — such that that very essence which is in the Father (as in the person producing the Son) is wholly in the Son; and that which is in the Father and the Son (as in the persons producing the Holy Spirit) is wholly in the Holy Spirit. And on this account the Son is said to be produced ***of the substance of the Father***, the Holy Spirit ***of the substance of the Father and the Son***. Hence our Theologians show that in this mode the Son and Holy Spirit have been produced:

> *Whatever persons are produced, are produced either from some subject, or from nothing, or ***of the substance of the one producing***. But the Son and Holy Spirit are produced neither from any subject nor from nothing — for otherwise they would not be true and eternal God, but creature, since whatever is produced in either of those two modes is creature. Therefore it is left that each is produced of the substance of the one producing: namely, the Son of the substance of the Father, and the Holy Spirit of the substance of the Father and the Son.*

And because the substance or essence of divinity is one, most simple, and plainly indivisible, it necessarily follows that the Son is produced ***of the substance of the Father***, and the Holy Spirit ***of the substance of the Father and the Son***, in such wise that the one receives that very essence which is in the Father, and the other that very essence which is in the Father and the Son — and that essence whole and undivided. If anyone further press here, ***how*** the Father could have communicated His whole essence to the Son, and yet the whole essence of the Father have remained, and simultaneously also be the essence of the Son — this indeed our Theologians judge that they can neither attain by thought, nor sufficiently explicate.
