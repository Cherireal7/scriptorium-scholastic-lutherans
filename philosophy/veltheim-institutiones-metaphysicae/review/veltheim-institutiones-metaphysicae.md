# Translator's Note

## 1. The text-base

This working English translation follows the **first and only edition** of Valentin Veltheim's *Institutes of Metaphysics*, printed at **Jena, at the expense of the Author, from the Press of the Werthers, in the year of the World Redeemed M.DC.LXXX (1680)**, under the full title ***Institutiones Metaphysicae, Continuis Exemplis Inprimis Theologicis, Iuridicis, Moralibus, Politicis Illustratae, ut omnium Facultatum ac disciplinarum Studiosis eorumque usibus apprime serviant*** — *"Institutes of Metaphysics, illustrated with continuous examples drawn chiefly from theology, jurisprudence, morals, and politics, so that they may suitably serve students of all faculties and disciplines and their uses."*

The source copy used here is **Universitätsbibliothek Kiel, shelfmark K 2791-1 and K 2791-2** (persistent identifier PPN72572448X), digitised by schriftgut.sh. Its plaintext OCR endpoint — some 2.6 MB of UTF-8 Latin, in 67,379 lines — has been pulled as our working extract and reconstructed silently against the OCR errors characteristic of a late-17th-century octavo in Dutch-Fraktur-adjacent Latin fount: long-*s* → *f*, intermittent digit substitutions in all-caps display (e.g. *THEOLOGIC1S* for *THEOLOGICIS*, *polit1cis* for *politicis*), dropped diacritics in the title-page cartouche, split chapter headings at page boundaries (e.g. *CAPUT NON UM* for *CAPUT NONUM*), and running headers interleaved with body text where the OCR column-recognition broke across page breaks.

## 2. What this book actually is

Veltheim's *Institutiones Metaphysicae* is the **inaugural textbook of his Jena chair**. Valentin Veltheim (1645–1700) — *Hallensis Saxo*, Hallensian of Saxony — was elected Public Ordinary Professor of Logic and First Philosophy at Jena in 1679, one year before this volume went to press; it is the first fruit of that chair and the official metaphysical textbook he laid before his students and the four Jena faculties. He trained under the long shadow of two revered Jena predecessors, **Daniel Stahl** (†1654) and **Johann Andreas Scherzer von Slevogt** (†1659) — both prolific textbook metaphysicians of the generation that had codified the orthodox Lutheran *Schulmetaphysik* after the Thirty Years' War — and under the Jena dean of theology **Friedemann Bechmann** (†1703), whose moral and dogmatic *Institutiones* were the shelf-companions to the present volume. The *Institutiones Metaphysicae* is Veltheim's considered attempt to fuse this inherited Jena method with the methodological reformism of the Altdorf school — Philipp Scherb, Ernst Soner, Michael Piccart, Johannes Kobius — whom Veltheim repeatedly cites as authorities for his key methodological claim: that metaphysics has a *special* use in every one of the superior faculties.

That claim gives the book its distinctive shape. Veltheim does not treat metaphysics as a free-standing speculative discipline chained only to Aristotle's *Metaphysics*. He treats it as the **general propaedeutic to every faculty of the University** — theology, jurisprudence, medicine, moral philosophy, politics, natural law, the law of nations — and accordingly fills every chapter with *continuous examples* drawn precisely from the questions those faculties argue about: the causality of justification, the authority of Scripture as *principium primum* of theology, the capacity of the testator and the formal requisites of a will, the causality of restitution in moral cases, the formal object of the beatific vision, the metaphysics of sacrifice and satisfaction, the real and modal distinctions on which the Lutheran Christological and Eucharistic doctrines turn, and much else. The programme announced in the title (*Theologicis, Iuridicis, Moralibus, Politicis*) is executed consistently across all 49 numbered Capita.

## 3. Structural outline

**No** *Libri*, **no** *Partes* at the top level. The body is one unbroken sequence of chapters:

- **Caput Praeliminare** — *On the Nature, Constitution, and Use of Metaphysics* (what metaphysics is, that it is a true science, its material and formal object, abstraction of reason and real abstraction, its division into a *common* and a *proper* part, and especially its *general* and *special use* in the other disciplines).
- **Capita I–XLIX** — the body of metaphysics proper, running from *ens* and its first distinctions, through the transcendentals, cause, substance and accident, the categories, the distinctions (real, modal, rational), duration and time, signs, the predicates of God and of created spirit, to the Pneumatica (*God, angels, the rational soul separately considered*) at the end.

An **Index Capitum** and **Index Rerum** close the volume. Each chapter is subdivided into numbered **§§** (usually 10–40 per chapter), in the exposition style the title promises: definition, corollary, scholastic note, continuous example from one of the four target faculties.

## 4. Register and conventions in this English rendering

- **Numbering preserved.** Veltheim's §-numbers are retained verbatim; where the OCR corrupted a numeral (e.g. §26 printed as "§16"), the restored number is given silently.
- **Scholastic Latin kept where it carries weight.** *Ens, substantia, accidens, ratio formalis objectiva, praecisio, abstractio praecisiva, principium cognoscendi, principium essendi, abstractio secundum essentiam / secundum indifferentiam, actus signatus / exercitus, causa per se / per accidens* — these are kept italicised and glossed on first appearance, not domesticated.
- **Veltheim's Greek handled in the way he himself handles it.** He regularly embeds Aristotelian Greek phrases (ἀναπόδεικτος, ἀκρότατον, πάθος, ποιητική, θεωρητική, &c.); these are transliterated inline with a glossing English equivalent, as his own text would have been read by a Jena student.
- **The vernacular glosses Veltheim prints in Early New High German** — for example his dictum on real abstraction: *es kann wahrhaftig eins sein ohne das andere, auch wenn gleich niemand daran dencken solte* — are preserved and translated inline.
- **The Scholastic Lutherans house conventions are kept:** scripture citations in bold Vulgate/KJV form; §§-level separators by `— ❦ —` only between chapters, not between §§.

## 5. On the auditor-list

The front matter closes with a four-page list of the names of the students who sat under Veltheim in the privatissimum from which this book grew — one of the few places where a 17th-century Jena *Hörsaal* presents itself under its own names. We reproduce it verbatim (silently correcting the OCR of the German tails and preserving the geographic epithets), both because Veltheim himself writes that he could not in conscience print the book without acknowledging them, and because for the historian of orthodox Lutheran education in the generation before Francke's Halle, this is a snapshot of the student body of Jena at the moment it was still the pre-eminent Protestant university of the Empire.

# Dedicatory Epistle

**To the Magnificent Rector and the Whole Senate of the illustrious University of Jena, to the most reverend and most learned Doctors, Jurisconsults, Experienced and Illustrious Men — patrons, promoters, and most honoured colleagues of the highest observance — Valentin Veltheim dedicates and consecrates these Institutes of Metaphysics with the greatest humility.**

*"I pay what I owe, and still I owe what I pay."* For if I have brought forth anything by writing, I owe it to many of You who, partly by the living voice, partly by Your outstanding writings, have unfolded for the common use of all faculties and the single disciplines which my book treats, those very points of doctrine which I have here either explicated or applied. For as **Pliny** truly says, *"It is a kindly thing, and full of ingenuous modesty, to confess by whom one has made one's progress."* Therefore, that I may make my own what **Cicero** said of himself in the oration *pro Archia*, so I may truly say of You: *"If there is in me any talent, which I feel how slight it is; or any practice of speaking, in which I do not deny that I am of some competent habit; or any exercise and discipline derived from the knowledge of the most honourable arts, from which no part of my life has shrunk — from all these things above all these men can rightly claim their fruit from me. If, moreover, I have been of any use in the University, next after God Most High and the munificent nurturers of the University, the most serene Princes and our most clement Lords, I owe it wholly to Your illustrious Order."*

So far, indeed, am I from thinking that any of You has at any time taken amiss my manner of teaching or of writing, that rather from each of the four Venerable Faculties, either single men or most of You (You know these things were written by me with inmost sincerity, under the eye of Conscience) have added spur to me: namely, that You have approved both the goal set before my public labours and the means by which I have been led to that goal — You who have been Patrons, Promoters, Commenders of my studies.

Accept therefore, best of Patrons and Supporters, with a benevolent hand this Book of Institutes, as at once a token of my observance toward You and a pledge of my future cares for the welfare of the students who aspire to a solid erudition. With a most tenacious memory I keep this: that the profession of Logic and First Philosophy has always been esteemed, both by the Most Ample Academic Senate and by the Philosophical Faculty, so highly indeed that the Most Serene Nurturers of this University, our most Clement Princes and Lords, have been given to understand at great length the weight of what both these disciplines, if solidly, faithfully, and diligently cultivated, bring to the studies of all and single faculties and disciplines — and how great and how numerous are the damages which the neglect of this office can bring, since the students of the Academy who fail in these matters cannot thereafter happily succeed either in any other of the philosophical disciplines or in any faculty whatsoever. Which thing also the Most Serene and Most Illustrious Nurturers, our Princes and Clement Lords, have not long since asserted most clearly in sundry of their most clement Mandates and Rescripts; declaring thereby their zeal for the fostering and promotion of these studies, and their grace and inestimable benignity toward the cultivators and professors of them, by living proofs. Wherefore, with God's help, I will leave no stone unturned, lest the hopes which You have conceived of me be deceived — that *sparta* which, by Your counsels and desires, I have obtained, I will adorn to the uttermost, so long as God and the Most Serene Dukes, the munificent Nurturers, shall will it so.

But — as Fabius narrates concerning the most excellent painter **Timanthes**, that at the sacrifice of Iphigenia, when he had painted a sad Calchas, a sadder Ulysses, and had set beside Menelaus the utmost grief that art could compass, finding no mode by which he could worthily express the countenance of the Father, he veiled his head, and left to each man's imagination to estimate what he could not depict — so I, conscious of my own slightness, do not undertake to paint the grief of Agamemnon, but rather the surpassing virtues of You my Patrons: Your signal and most eminent deserts of this illustrious *Salana* [the University of Jena], Your immense and incredible zeal for the advancement of letters, Your most flourishing and most noble bevy of the finest Students, Your singular prudence in the administration of academic business, Your goodness and equity toward the readers that come under You — all these I leave to be weighed by candid and equitable readers.

Meanwhile, for that benevolence with which hitherto You have embraced me, unworthy — I place before You, within Your most learned pulpits, an altar whose title is:

> *To the Magnificent Rector of the University*
> *and to the T. Senate,*
> *Alone and Necessary Patron,*
> *Most Equitable Estimator of Philosophy:*
> *Farewell and favour me.*

Of Your most celebrated names a cultivator, devoted to every service and office,

VALENTIN VELTHEIM

# Preface to the Reader

**I set before thee, benevolent reader, those fundamental precepts of First Philosophy which men now vulgarly call *Metaphysics* — not confected out of my own brain, nor born only in the last few days, but handed down long ago from the most approved and most celebrated Philosophers, received into use among the Doctors of every Faculty, and wont to be applied to the material of any discipline whatsoever.**

The whole learned world has long seen, and still sees, the signal deserts of my *predecessors* in this chair, especially the most celebrated **Daniel Stahl** of blessed memory and **Johann Andreas Slevogt** of blessed memory, formerly most illustrious Professors of Logic and Metaphysics in this our *Salana* — with what applause, with what fruit and praise they taught Logic and Metaphysics both within the public walls and within the private; how great a number of the best and most learned Students in this *Salana* they increased and preserved to the envy of others; and how, by their lectures, they trained up men not only for the cultivation of the other faculties, but also such as, with the highest praise and the ineffable increase of the public good, were advanced to so many and so weighty ecclesiastical and civil offices. Witness of this are their innumerable disciples and auditors, afterwards great Doctors both in universities and in other places, likewise men constituted in the highest dignities; they still bear witness, as often as, with grateful mind, they call again to memory the Stahlian and Slevogtian deserts.

That I might usefully step in the footprints of such men, the first help came from the *dexterity* of the Most Illustrious **Valentin Bergerus** of blessed memory, Rector of the Gymnasium in my fatherland and a man of signally honoured deserts, which deserts I shall never sufficiently preach forth. For during four years, after the necessary foundational studies, he instilled into me — a slow learner — and into my fellows, by teaching, by examining, by disputing, by most frequent repetition, the doctrine of Stahl and of Slevogt (whose assiduous auditor he had been in this our *Salana* through ten full years and more); so instilled it, I say, that even the dullest might profit and recognise Stahl in Stahl and Slevogt in Slevogt. His deserts toward my welfare I cannot sufficiently preach forth: he judged me, unworthy as I am, worthy to be his successor in the governance of the said Gymnasium or in its Rectorate, and shortly before his blessed death confirmed anew his wishes concerning this matter — wishes which the Fathers of my Fatherland (whom I shall venerate to the utmost) refused to be void; whereby the divine goodness, by this manifest calling of me as by the hand itself, abundantly declared to my grateful spirit that it was called forth to the offices of piety and of thankful observance.

The full weight that the Bergerian formation added was added by the most worthy *successor* of Stahl and Slevogt, a man most eminently deserving both of the public and of my own slender person, continually meriting more every hour: **Friedemann Bechmann**. He admitted me, equipped with so many public testimonies and letters of recommendation, with the utmost benignity as I came to the Jena Muses; he explored my progress, prescribed the method of continuing my studies, taught Logic and Metaphysics most faithfully, and by his paternal counsels blesses me to this very day. Rather than enumerate his deserts, I venerate them in a dutiful silence, because I know that the singular modesty of my venerable preceptor shrinks from my praises. I pass over now the names of my other most free-born preceptors, continuers of the Stahlian doctrine, highest men: among the Theologians, **Johann Musaeus**, **Christoph Chemnitz**, my own host **Johann Ernst Gerhard** of blessed memory, Preceptor equivalent to a Parent, and **Sebastian Niemann** likewise my host; among the Philosophers, **Zeisold** of blessed memory, my Promoter, **Posner**, and **Gottfried Posner**: I shall venerate for ever their benefits, by which the whole fruit of the Logical and Metaphysical doctrines was derived into my studies in Theology, Physics, and Practical Philosophy.

These things I write, benevolent Reader, that thou mayest know to which Doctors I owe the doctrines I have written. Nor let the *prolixity* of these Institutes deter thee: in the Precepts themselves, the fundamental, as they are called, I have so proceeded that neither can I be accused of over-abundance nor of defective brevity, having had the chief precepts printed in a larger type so that they might be committed to memory, repeated, explained — and these in truth do not take up so great a part of the book that I can be rightly accused of any prolixity on their account. Those matters, however, which stand under the title *Notes* and are set in smaller type have been drawn out at greater length by me, in order that many Students, more rightly than is commonly done, should estimate the metaphysical doctrines and their use in the (so-called) superior Faculties, and above all should have something which, after public or private lectures, they might read at home, ruminate, imitate, and learn to turn — as the saying is — into the *marrow and blood* of their principal studies, so to become more fully conversant with them as time goes on.

Of the praises of the metaphysical study I shall not here undertake to speak: I have attempted this in a separate little tract whose title is: ***Apparatus moralis***, that is, *a brief disquisition on the suitable and necessary means and aids by which those must be equipped who wish, in the University, to carry off the fruit of metaphysical studies*, where I have also treated the *origin and progress of the metaphysical study*, and *which writings are most apt for the obtaining of a metaphysical erudition*, together with the deserts of the most illustrious men in First Philosophy. (This *Apparatus Metaphysicus* will see the light, God propitious, shortly.) Much less do I now stop over to answer the *architectonical detractors* and corrupters of First Philosophy — those who dare to judge it, out of the number of the disciplines, as useless, trifling, injurious; who have essayed, with wholly rash attempt — nay, who even now essay — either to drive out the very memorials of this discipline and of the Analytic (whose very name scarce any of them has ever learnt), by rendering them odious to the Students and so by degrees snatching them away altogether; or else, posing themselves as the best doctors and leaders of these very disciplines (though they are rather *seducers* and *cheaters*), ask to be so believed and so honoured by all; a sin which, however much it has been more frequently committed, even by the most inept and the most lazy, against no discipline than against First Philosophy — commonly called Metaphysics — which it has now become fashionable to assail with abuses, though they understand it not a whit more than our *lame Ockershausen pedlar* understands of the Cabala.

Yet hope cherished us, that by the manifest utility of the same, especially in Theological controversies, those who sneered at the light with closed eyes — whether calumniators, or corrupters — would at last yield to the truth, and would be strangers and immune, henceforth, from all guilt of seducing or corrupting the Philosophical Youth. But it has fallen out otherwise: new frauds are daily being devised against the innocence of this discipline, new ambushes laid against the truth — even by those who in theological controversies clearly show that they have in no way been able to spare themselves the use and help of this discipline. And would that they had always used it with that *integrity* which was due; perhaps there would now be far fewer errors, and far fewer contentions, than we — not without groans — now experience. These are the words of the most celebrated Metaphysician **Jakob Reeb**, in his *Apology for the Truth of First Philosophy or Metaphysics against the Celebrated Theologian **William Ames**, Calvinist D. D., and against his sharp and railing-filled disputation entitled* **Disputatio Theologica adversus Metaphysicam**, *first printed at Leiden, afterwards reprinted at Hanau*. But concerning these matters I have treated more at length in the said *Apparatus Metaphysicus*, which, God propitious, will see the light shortly, and in particular capita *on the damages and detriments of Metaphysics, item on the haters and resisters of Metaphysics*. Examples meanwhile of such haters' *Practical Philosophy* occur throughout the Institutes themselves, where if any of them seem to me too sharply refuted and to besmirch the Institutes, Thy *equity* and Thy *prudence*, Reader (if indeed Thou art of prudent mind), will easily free me from all guilt, if Thou consider — as I write with God and a good conscience as my witnesses, as Thy own δύσεις [thine own judgment] can abundantly testify to Thee — that these very words (which do indeed seem sharp) are not mine but my adversaries', turned against himself by me (saving the laws divine and human): a man who did not blush to vomit forth the most dreadful atrocities (which, saving honesty, could scarcely, scarcely be repeated by me) against my innocence.

Three things more, benevolent Reader, it concerns Thee to know.

**First**, I offer Thee these Institutes *complete* — except that we treat God, angels, and the rational soul in so far as positively *separable* and *separate*, in a sequel. For since even today Metaphysical Doctors — not few of them, nor obscure — are found who treat of God, angels, and the separated soul in a *special* discipline which they call **Pneumatology** or **Pneumatics** — not without a sufficient or at least highly plausible reason, which we have inspected in our *Apparatus Metaphysicus* — we have decided that nevertheless the whole discipline of Metaphysics remains full and unimpaired, even if, in the given case, the very name of *theologia naturalis* could not so conveniently be defended as if it were denied. On this hypothesis, our *prior part* will be *abstract metaphysics* — in which, namely, we have treated all those things which, excepting indeed Pneumatics, are treated commonly, whether in the so-called general part or in the other, which men call *special*. And whoever considers this with himself will no longer wonder why we have included the *posterior part* of the Institutes in a thinner quaternion. In short, Pneumatics, in a separate little book included therein, shall see the light.

**Then**, since the cultivators of Theology, of Law, and of Morals promise themselves above all a *practical use* of Metaphysics from the questions touching upon these matters — by the method after which the *axioms* appended to the first chapter of the Institutes have been handed down — a work on *Metaphysical Axiomatics* (if we may so call it) will be treated in a separate opus. Meanwhile, benevolent Reader, if it so please Thee, make good use of these Institutes, written for the glory of God and to Thy own advantage; and if in this or that I have erred, pardon me. For — if, as becomes an honest and prudent man, Thou admonish me — I shall more promptly do it than Thou shalt put forth the monition.

**Lastly**, it is worth my pains to call back to memory, and publicly to proclaim, the singular diligence and the benevolence of my honoured AUDITORS, and their ardour toward these studies: for these are they who, by the most honest entreaties, obtained from me both that I should commit these Institutes to the public types, and that I should enter into private walls with them. Ungrateful I were, did I not honour, by the title and the honour of ***Benevolent, Studious, Attentive, and most Grateful*** both in reality and in disposition, those Auditors who with grateful mind have pursued my name, and have paid their didactic by thought and by hand. May God grant them all those things which must be the solemn wish of good academic Auditors and of a Professor. The memory of this matter I have willed to leave in these pages, out of my gratitude to Those Benevolent Auditors who shared this private Collegium with me — I omit all distinctions of dignity, of erudition, and of surname:

# Caput Praeliminare

### *On the Nature, Constitution, and Use of Metaphysics*

---

**§ 1.** For those about to enter upon the metaphysical study in the University with any fruit, there is as much need of a *propaedeutic* (παιδεία) to Metaphysics as there is, for those about to undertake a long journey, of travelling-provisions. By a *propaedeutic* (παιδεία) to Metaphysics we understand the whole of that doctrine with which anyone who would reach — with gain and without loss of time — the desired goal of metaphysical studies ought to be equipped, rather than seek it in the midst of the exercises of this doctrine itself.

**§ 2.** Most rightly says **Aristotle**: *Ἀεὶ δεῖ πεπαιδεῦσθαι ἔν τι τοῦ πρὸς τὴν ἔντευξιν ἔξεως φιλοσόφου* — *"The hearer or the teacher must always have been propaedeutically trained before he can rightly discharge his office."* And again: *Σπουδαιότατον ἔοικεν εἶναι τὸ ἅμα ζητεῖν τὸ ἐπιστήμην καὶ τὸν τρόπον τῆς ἐπιστήμης* — *"It is a wholly inept and absurd thing to seek simultaneously the science and the mode of the science"* (that is: the doctrine or discipline, and the mode of teaching and of learning it).

**§ 3.** Altogether the future apt auditors of Metaphysics must first equip themselves in these matters, since it will be *their proper office to judge* whether the Master is teaching Metaphysics rightly or ill, whether he keeps himself within the limits of his discipline, and whether in the treatment of things he proceeds *metaphysically* — that is, in such wise that he keeps his promises; that he uses defined terms and ones more known to the auditor; that his *principles* (certified to others also) and his vernacular glosses are fittingly accommodated to his science.

**§ 4.** For those auditors who do not have judgment enough to discern what has the force of *proving* and *demonstrating* and what has not — what produces science and accurate cognition, what passes off ignorance for science, what produces only a tenuous cognition or the mere shadow of a science; in what the Master accommodates himself still more to the auditor's capacity and uses *common* arguments, and in what the nature of the thing requires that he teach according to its own and *domestic* principles; how far the *necessary* differs from the *contingent*, the *true* from the *false*, the *certain* from the *doubtful*, the *common* from the *proper*, the *foreign* from the *domestic* and the *genuine*, the *solid* reasons from any mere declamation whatever, the learned Master from the less learned — such auditors are, as of no other science so least of all of Metaphysics (whose propaedeutic is the most difficult of all), *γνήσιοι* **genuine and apt** auditors.

**§ 5.** For the rest, we leave the *propaedeutic* in the present matter — as something elsewhere ascertained — in its own place; namely that the propaedeutic of Metaphysics, as of every other discipline, is partly *more common* and partly *more special*. The *more common* propaedeutic is plainly the same as the Analytical doctrine — Aristotelian, understand, not such as this or that *Modern* now feigns; for ignorance of the Analytic leaves both the auditors and the Master of Metaphysics groping, and they will feel nothing of the happy progress in solid solid Metaphysical doctrine. To hand down that *more common* propaedeutic now would be to reproduce the whole of the Analytic doctrine, which the reason of our present institute neither requires nor permits.

**§ 6.** This, however (namely the *more special* propaedeutic), cannot be more rightly or more conveniently handed down elsewhere than here; whence, before passing to the Institutes of Metaphysics themselves, we shall touch upon it very briefly: and this both because by other most illustrious Men and Preceptors, whose most honoured footprints to tread (so far as can be done by God's grace and the strength of our wit — let this be the sacred law of our enterprise!) we are vowed to tread, we have been so advised and have seen it so observed; and because this was advised and observed by Aristotle himself.

**§ 7.** And first, that there is a *Metaphysical Science* every man easily admits in whom the natural desire for science is felt; since Metaphysics is above all a science — whence by Aristotle and the Ancients it is called ἀκρότατον, the *topmost* science, the *first* science, the *most honourable* science, the *princess of the sciences*, to whom (as to a mistress) it is fit that the other sciences, as handmaids, not contradict.

**§ 8.** If we regard the *signification of the name* "Metaphysica", properly and according to the use of speech received among the Philosophers, Metaphysics denotes nothing other than a *habit by which it is given to us to know the most commonly-used notions, the most commonly-used axioms, and the most eminent things*. This signification *use* — which is the best master of speech — approves in the universities; although by metonymy also the *discipline* which supplies the most commonly-used notions, the most commonly-used axioms, and the most eminent things, is often called by the same name.

**§ 9.** The *definition* of Metaphysics which, in the schools of the Metaphysical Doctors accommodating themselves ever to the capacity of their auditors, we know to have obtained, is this:

> **Metaphysics is a demonstrative (theoretical) science of being and of the inferiors of being, in so far as they are *really abstract from sensible matter*, or have predicates *really abstracted* from sensible matter.**

**§ 10.** The *common concept* of Metaphysics, which answers to the genus, is expressed by the word *Science*. The *proper concept*, which exhausts the whole essential differentia, is given by the remaining words of the definition.

**§ 11.** For a right perception of that concept, cognition of two things is needed: first, that it be abundantly settled that Metaphysics is truly and properly so-called a *Science*; second, that there are *other* sciences, besides Metaphysics, truly and properly so-called. Both are easy to recognise, and will appear in what follows.

**§ 12.** For the exposition of this concept it is pertinent to know that Metaphysics is: (1) a science *demonstrative*, (2) *theoretical*, (3) *of being and of the inferiors of being, in so far as these are really abstract from sensible matter, and in so far as they have predicates really abstracted from sensible matter.*

**§ 13.** To prove most exactly that *Metaphysics is a science, and indeed a demonstrative one*, is to teach all and single the demonstrations by which the whole Metaphysical science is acquired. Let it suffice to have adduced one or another example of Metaphysical demonstrations in passing. Certainly, as it is established from the Analytic that of demonstrations some are called *ὅτι* [demonstrations *that*], some *διότι* [demonstrations *why*], some *per impossibile* — so it is most certain that *all* and *single* modes of demonstration have their place in Metaphysics.

**§ 14.** Is not that a *most excellent demonstration* by which the Metaphysician demonstrates the infallible truth of this proposition — ***GOD IS*** — in the following way? *Whatever is, either is from itself or from another. The whole universe is. Therefore, either it is from itself or from another. But it is not from itself, since these corruptible things cannot be the cause of themselves — indeed, nothing is the cause of its own prior being. Therefore the whole universe is from another. Further, there is no progress to infinity. Therefore it is necessary that we come to rest in some one First, which is the cause of all the rest and depends on no other. And that is **Being of Beings**, that is, **GOD**.* For all men, hearing the name of God, understand that infinite, most noble, and first of all beings, which depends on none; and that there should be two or more infinite beings implicates a manifest contradiction. All these things will be more accurately and more clearly treated in the *latter part* of these Institutes.

**§ 15.** Equally, *demonstrations διότι* ["demonstrations by cause"] have altogether their place in Metaphysics. Does not the Metaphysician, for example, demonstrate the *eternity* of God *through immutability*; immutability *through utter simplicity*; utter simplicity *through infinity*; infinity *through independence*, and so forth? For the demonstrations *of this kind* do not always require that what is demonstrated and the cause through which it is demonstrated really differ among themselves; it suffices that the reason be *domestic* and simply *proximate*, which can satisfy *per totum* a solid question put by an attentive auditor.

**§ 16.** And *demonstrations per impossibile* also have the amplest place in Metaphysics. For example: if the Metaphysician would demonstrate that *God is wholly ἀπαθής, bare of all passive potency*, he demonstrates it *through the absurd and the impossible* thus: ***If in God there were some passive potency, he could receive something through it — either a perfection, or something involving some imperfection. Neither can be said. Therefore in God there is no passive potency.*** For that He cannot receive any *perfection* through it is easily seen, since He would so not be *perfect* but could still receive another; which is absurd, foreign to all piety. That He cannot receive, through such passive potency, anything involving *imperfection* is likewise easily seen, since so there would be imperfection in God, which also is absurd and impious. — Likewise, if any should deny the truth of this proposition, *God can be*, he may easily be led into the absurd thus: *Whatever cannot be, is impossible to be. God cannot be. Therefore it is impossible that God be.* The conclusion is absurd and foolish — for *"the fool hath said in his heart, there is no God"* (**Ps. 14:1**). The form of the syllogism is best; whence, since from true premises in legitimate form a false conclusion cannot follow, it must be that either both premises or at least one of them is false. Of the *major* premise's truth no man of sane judgment doubts, since what cannot be is not possible to be, and what is not possible to be is impossible to be, as even a blind man sees. Therefore the *minor* is false. But the minor was, *God cannot be*; and since this minor is contradictorily opposed to the proposition laid down before, *God can be*, and two contradictorily opposed propositions cannot both be false, it is necessary — since this one, *God cannot be*, is false — that the former, *God can be*, is true, since whatever is or is not, it is impossible for the same to be and not to be at once. — As, then, from what has just been adduced it comes out of its own forehead that Metaphysics is a truly demonstrative habit, that is, a demonstrative science, and so truly and properly so-called a science: since *Physics*, *Mathematics*, and many practical and effective habits are likewise acquired through demonstrations proper to their kind, there is no doubt but that *science* may with saving truth be predicated of many habits — and is actually predicated — so that it comes very near to answering as a *genus*.

**§ 17.** It might indeed seem that *Metaphysics* cannot be defined through *science* as its *integral part* (as they say) — for by the common consent of Aristotle and all the Philosophers Metaphysics is **wisdom** (*σοφία*), and the received and popularly known definition of wisdom is this: ***Wisdom is a habit composed of understanding and science of the most eminent things.*** Hence he who should wish to call an *integral part* of Metaphysics its *genus* would seem as inept as he who should call the head the genus of the man. The whole difficulty, in truth, is bred by the too thin exposition of Aristotle's dictum, in which (at *Nic. Eth.* VI, c. 7) he had called wisdom *νοῦς καὶ ἐπιστήμη* — *understanding and science* — and in the same chapter had defined it in express words: *ἡ σοφία ἄρα ἔσται νοῦς καὶ ἐπιστήμη τῶν τιμιωτάτων τῇ φύσει* — *"wisdom therefore is both science and understanding, concerning the by-nature-most-eminent things."* For neither from these words of Aristotle nor from the context can it be gathered — nor can it ever be admitted without loss of truth — that wisdom is truly and really composed of understanding and science, however much the vulgar of philosophisers suffer themselves to be persuaded of this. For wisdom and understanding (ἐπιστήμη and σοφία) are adequately distinct habits, nowise regarding one another as *integral part* and *integral whole*. *Understanding* (νοῦς) is a habit *ἀναπόδεικτος* — one which cannot be acquired by any demonstration, or (what comes to the same) which can be neither taught nor learnt. *Wisdom*, by contrast, is a habit *οὐκ ἀναπόδεικτος* — truly demonstrative, that is, able to be taught and learnt. It does not *include* understanding but presupposes it; for *understanding* is the habit of the first principles, while *wisdom* is the habit of *confirming* those first principles (known certainly and easily by the mediation of understanding) for the benefit of the doubtful, and of *demonstrating* them, against deaf and stubborn adversaries, *per impossibile et absurdum*. Deliberately we say *per impossibile et absurdum*: for the first principles cannot be known by a demonstration *ὅτι*, since such a demonstration necessarily rests on priors, whereas the first principles — being first — admit nothing prior to themselves, since to the first there is no prior.

**§ 18.** Nor is there anything in the objection that at least *wisdom* and *science absolutely so-called* (or *theoretical science*) remain essentially distinct habits under one and the same proximate genus — namely, under the *theoretical intellectual habit* — and mutually opposed, so that it can never come about that two *disparate species* are predicated *in quid* of one and the same inferior, namely of Metaphysics, and therefore that Metaphysics must either not be a *science* or not be a *wisdom*. For either *demonstrative science* is taken for any demonstrative habit whatsoever, from first principles, without heed whether those principles be *simply first* or *first in a certain genus*; or it is taken for a demonstrative habit from principles that are first in a certain genus (which some call *secondary* principles). If science be taken in the former way, then any party-free man can easily see that *demonstrative science* so taken and *wisdom* are not disparate but *subordinate*, and that *science* can altogether be the *genus*, or at least answer to the character of a genus in order to *wisdom* (that is, to Metaphysics) and to *Physics* and many other habits: for *Metaphysics*, *Physics*, *Mathematics*, &c., are habits demonstrative from first principles, whatever may be in it that to these only *secondary* principles belong, while to those also *primary* principles — i.e. simply and absolutely first — do belong, since this does not in any way enter into the essential constitution of science so taken. If science be taken in the latter way, we readily grant that science in that rigour is neither the genus of Metaphysics nor answers to its genus — since Metaphysics is not a demonstrative habit that uses only secondary principles.

**§ 19.** It stands, then, hitherto, both from the definition of science and from the definition of wisdom, that Metaphysics is both science and wisdom; nor can it any longer be obscure *why only the former, and not the latter, can be the genus of Metaphysics*. For *science* extends more widely than *Metaphysics*; *wisdom* not at all — since all and only Metaphysics is wisdom, and conversely all and only wisdom is Metaphysics. Hence, since to every genus simply and necessarily it is required that it be able to extend more widely than its species — and since this can never truly be said of *wisdom* as against Metaphysics — the reason stands out clear why *wisdom* cannot be called the genus of Metaphysics.

**§ 20.** You say: *Wisdom can be divided into First and Second, so that wisdom extends more widely than Metaphysics, which alone is First Wisdom*; and you cite Aristotle as patron of this division. But a truer reason dictates that **Second Wisdom**, accurately and properly speaking, is not wisdom at all: for wisdom rigorously so called is conversant about *first principles*, since (as said above) it is the office of wisdom to unfold the objects of *understanding* itself — that is, to expound those notions out of which the first principles consist, to *confirm* the first principles themselves for the benefit of the doubting, and to *demonstrate* them *per impossibile* against those who deny. More clearly: *Understanding* is conversant about absolutely first principles as being *first and known per se*; *Wisdom* treats them as *conclusions*. Understanding gives the intellect readiness of assent to the first principles; Wisdom supplies evidence of them. — Nay, feign at length some other wisdom besides First Wisdom (i.e. Metaphysics): it will be conversant either about things *less first*, or *more first*, or *equally first*. Not the last: for if Metaphysics and your supposed wisdom were conversant about equally first principles, the principles of both would be comprehended under *earlier* and *more common* principles, and so neither of the two "wisdoms" would be first — which is absurd. Not the middle: that is, it cannot be that your supposed wisdom be conversant about *more first* things, because nothing more common and nothing prior than what is handed down in Metaphysics can you feign, however much you may wish to err; and if you do feign it, then Metaphysics against your own assertion will not remain *first*. Not the first alternative either: for if your feigned wisdom were conversant about *less* common and first things, and Metaphysics about *more* common, then your feigned wisdom would be demonstrative in such wise that in all its demonstrations it would depend on Metaphysics — which plainly repugns to wisdom rightly so called. The matter is at a stand, unless we would play with terms, so that *science* and *wisdom* be taken for the same and signify entirely the same thing: and this play — to what end? Nor is it ever to be deduced from Aristotle's Philosophy that he divided *wisdom*, in opposition to *science*, into First and Second. *φιλοσοφία πρώτη καὶ δευτέρα* — "First and Second Philosophy" — in the Philosopher's mind are the First and Second Sciences; for now *σοφία* denotes the same as *science*, now the same as *wisdom*; which no man denies, unless he would call the Seven Sages of Greece the Seven Metaphysicians of Greece. Each signification is proper and received in use. Let the like judgment be made of the word *φιλοσοφία*, if you consider the usage of speech.

**§ 21.** Not without good reason have we assigned a *twofold essential differentia* to Metaphysics in calling it a *science* and *demonstrative*; because *science without demonstration* can also be given — such namely as is *understanding* (νοῦς) and *theorematic* (θεωρηματική), that is, such as cannot be acquired except by acts of the intellect purely *theoretic* — acts, that is, that are directive of no *actio in the strict sense* nor of any *effectio*, as the Schools speak. For such are called those acts by which the intellect judges whether or how an *external action strictly so-called* (ἔνδοθεν or ἔξωθεν) or an *effection* ought to be committed or omitted. — The apt auditors or cultivators of Metaphysics must have very clearly perceived that no more accurate division of demonstrative science is given, nor can be given, than if it be divided into *contemplative, active, and effective* (θεωρητική, πρακτική, καὶ ποιητική).

**§ 22.** Lastly, the *essential differentia* of Metaphysics was sought from its *total or adequate object*, which, as in other sciences, is wont commonly to be stated as twofold in Metaphysics: the *material*, that is, the thing about which the science is conversant — here, *being with its inferiors*; and the *formal*, that is, the character under which the science is conversant about the thing (its material object) — which, so far as this present matter is concerned, is *real abstraction from sensible matter*.

**§ 23.** Therefore the ***total and adequate object*** of Metaphysics will be: *being and the inferiors of being*, in so far as they are *really abstract from sensible matter* and have *predicates really abstracted from sensible matter*.

**§ 24.** Something different in the meantime (if we would philosophise more accurately) is the *object*, and something different the *formal objective reason* (*ratio formalis objectiva*, as the Schools speak). It can happen — nay, in all sciences and disciplines it actually does happen — that many objects are given of one and the same science or discipline, yet so that there remains *one and the same first ratio of knowing*, into which all other cognitions of all and single objects at last run and tend. For example: many objects of the physical science are given — stone, lion, man, tree, sky, earth, water, &c.; but these and far more others have *one formal ratio* under which they are known in Physics, namely, in so far as they are *natural*. So if we take, by way of example, a *faculty*, as *sight*: there again many objects are given — wood, stone, lion, man, rose, cherry-tree, &c.; but these and innumerable others have *one ratio* under which they are known, and by which they have it that they are objects of sight, namely *the ratio of the coloured*: for they are not visible except in so far as they are coloured.

**§ 25.** And this is the cause why the Schoolmen Doctors distinguish *formal objective ratio* from the *objects themselves*: for just as elsewhere whatever is, is by its *form*, and *form gives being to the thing*, and the thing itself is said to receive its *denomination* and its *being* from the form — so many things have it, that they are objects of one science or discipline, from *one ratio of knowing*, which was for that reason called *ratio formalis objectiva* by the Scholastics. For unless there were one genuine and formal ratio from which many things have it that they are objects of one science or discipline, one science or discipline would not be *one*, which implicates a contradiction.

**§ 26.** As, therefore, there is said to be *one formal object of sight*, because all visible things name *one and the same ratio*, namely the ratio of *colouredness*, as just noted — so it cannot be denied that there are *innumerable objects* of the Metaphysical science (since Metaphysics is conversant not only about *being*, but also about *the inferiors of being*), while all the objects agree in *one and the same formal ratio*, which is and is called *real abstraction from sensible matter*.

**§ 27.** Here comes to be said what is called *abstraction*, what *real abstraction from sensible matter*, what *sensible matter*. The notion of abstraction in common cannot be defined; and at once there is need of the distinction between *abstraction of reason* (*abstractio rationis*) and *real abstraction* (*abstractio realis*). From the former something is denominated *ratione abstractum* (abstract by reason); from the latter, *realiter abstractum* (really abstract). Two things, therefore, are required to any abstraction whatsoever: one, *what is abstracted*; another, *that from which it is abstracted*.

**§ 28.** ***Abstract by reason*** is said to be ***that which, apart from another, is known by the intellect or through the mediation of the intellect***, whether it agree or not with that other, and whether it be really one and the same with it or not. *Man denckt an eins, und an das andere nicht; das ist, man denckt an eins ohne das andere.* "One thinks of the one, and not of the other; that is to say, one thinks of the one without the other." For example: when, by my intellect, I know or think that *man is an animal*, yet do not at the same time know or think that *he is partaker of reason*, I abstract by my reason the *animality* of man from his *rationality*, since I know the animality of man without distinctly knowing man's rationality at the same time. Hence it is easily recognised what is *abstraction of reason*.

**§ 29.** The Scholastics make the *abstraction of reason* twofold: one *negative*, the other *praecisive*. The *negative abstraction* of reason they call that by which reason or the intellect *divides one thing from another*, to which division a negation corresponds: e.g. when my intellect knows that *man is not stone*, or thinks that *man is not rational*, this thought the Scholastics call *negative* abstraction, also *divisive*, since the intellect *divides* man from stone, or man from the rational, to which act these negative propositions correspond: *man is not stone, man is not rational*. ***Praecisive*** abstraction is the abstraction of reason by which one is known without the other, yet without any intervening *division* or *negation*: as, when I know what an *animal* is — namely, a mixed, animated, sensing natural body — yet do not at the same time know that it is a *man*, nor divide animal from man, nor (which comes to the same thing) deny man of animal: this manner of cognition is called *praecisive* abstraction of reason. For us, whenever in Metaphysics we speak of *abstraction of reason*, the speech is of *praecisive* abstraction — which is also called *praecisio* and, by excellence (*κατ' ἐξοχήν*), *abstractio rationis*.

**§ 30.** And of this (namely, of *praecisive* abstraction of reason) is to be understood that renowned saying — ***Solus est philosophus, qui bene abstrahit*** — "He alone is a Philosopher who abstracts well": that is, that man is the accurate Philosopher who knows how to recognise and weigh the objects of his science not confusedly, but becomingly and distinctly. To which fits well the common saying, *Qui bene distinguit, bene docet* — "He who distinguishes well, teaches well." Of *praecisive abstraction* we also accept this other dictum: ***Abstrahentium non est mendacium*** — "There is no lying in abstracters." For he who knows the one without knowing the other at the same time does not on that account at once affirm the one of the other, or deny the other; and so he cannot be convicted of lying. The contrary is the case with *negative abstraction*, by whose users one often lies. So much for *abstraction of reason*.

**§ 31.** ***Really abstract*** (*realiter abstractum*) is said to be ***that which, with no one thinking of it, can be without the other***. — *Es kan wahrhafftig eins seyn ohne das ander, wenn auch gleich niemand dran dencken solte, dass es ohne das andere seyn könne.* "It can truly be one thing without the other, even if nobody at all should think about it that it can be without the other." For example: with no one thinking of it, an *animal* can be without a *brute*, because though no brute at all were given, still an animal could be given with no one thinking of it, since a *man* can still be given; whence an animal is said to be really abstract from a brute. So *spirit* can be, with no one thinking of it, without matter — indeed is without matter, because to the essence of spirit *immateriality* belongs, that is, out of its own essence or by its own strength of being spirit is immaterial; whence spirit is denominated as really abstracted from matter.

**§ 32.** For the rest, something can be said *really abstract* from another whether it be in that other or belong to it or not — provided only, with no one thinking of it, it can be without that other. For it is not required of a *really abstract* that it always *actually be* without that from which it is really abstract. And here we especially note, lest we confound (as many are wont) *real abstraction* with *real separation*, that of a *really separated* it is certain that it is actually *without* that from which it is really separated; but not every *really abstract* is also *really separated*. For example: *animal* is really abstract from *man* and from *brute*, yet it is not really separated from man and brute. So the Philosophers rightly say that *genus* is really abstract from its species, though it can never come about that *genus* is really separated from all its species. — Real *separation* is twofold: one *positive*, by whose force that which is really separated exists separately on its own after separation (such is the real positive separation of nail from wall — for after it the nail exists apart, perhaps in your hand, the wall exists apart from it). The other *privative*, by whose force that which is really separated cannot after the completed separation exist any longer: as when *whiteness* is separated from a wall, since once the real separation of whiteness from the wall is made, the wall remains, the whiteness must perish — it could not remain. Now if *animal* were really separated from *man* and *brute* with a *positive* separation, then it could happen that an animal would be given which is neither man nor brute, or again that a man or a brute would be given which is not an animal — which is absurd. If *animal* were really separated or really separable from man and brute with a *privative* separation, then again it could happen that no animal would be, yet man or brute would be — and could be without animal — which blows forth a great absurdity.

**§ 33.** ***Real abstraction*** is in turn twofold: one is called by the Scholastics *absolute*, also *abstraction according to essence*, also *abstraction according to excellence*; the other is called *permissive* abstraction, also *abstraction according to indifference*. The *former* is the real abstraction by which *the one which is said to be really abstract from the other* ***cannot not be without the other*** — that is, cannot be *in* or *with* that other, or be that other: as *God* is said to be abstract from sensible matter *according to essence and absolutely*, since he cannot be sensible matter nor material. The *latter* is the real abstraction by which the thing (namely, what is held as really abstract from another) **can also be in that other, or with it, or itself be it**: as *being* is really abstract from sensible matter *permissively, i.e. according to indifference*, since being is given without any matter, being is given which is itself matter (because matter is being), being is given material, such as is every being that depends in its being on matter.

**§ 34.** ***Sensible matter*** is called *physical matter*, in so far as it can fall under the external senses or be known by the senses. Thus semen, menstrual blood, and the like are *sensible matter*, since they can be tasted, touched, &c. So too metal, wood, are sensible matter, since they can be seen, touched.

**§ 35.** As, therefore, every man will now with easy effort attain *what it is to say*: *being and the inferiors of being are really abstract from sensible matter* — namely, this: *being and the inferiors of being so stand, that with no one thinking of them they can be without sensible matter* — so one easily perceives also that things *really abstract from sensible matter* are of two kinds. First, there are given things abstract by the *real absolute* abstraction, or *abstraction according to essence*; that is, such that they *cannot be sensible matter itself, nor in sensible matter nor with sensible matter*. Thus, e.g., really abstract from sensible matter by *abstraction according to essence* or *absolute* abstraction, are these inferiors of being: *spirit, God, angels, the soul positively separated*. Next, there are given things really abstract by *the real permissive* abstraction, or *abstraction according to indifference*: namely such that they can also be sensible matter itself, in or with sensible matter: in this mode really abstract are these inferiors of being: *substance, accident*, and the like.

**§ 36.** ***The Parts of Metaphysics are twofold:***

> **Prior** — the part that treats of being and the inferiors of being, in so far as they are really abstract from sensible matter *according to indifference* or by permissive abstraction, not by absolute abstraction. — **Posterior** — the part that treats of those inferiors of being which are really abstract from sensible matter *according to excellence* or by absolute abstraction.

**§ 37.** The common division of Metaphysics is thus ordinarily set up: that one part be called *common* or quasi *general*, which treats of being in common and of the most common affections of being; the other, quasi *special* and *proper*, which treats of the inferiors of being, in so far as they are really abstract from sensible matter, or have *less common predicates* really abstracted from sensible matter. (For in so far as the inferiors of being have the most common predicates really abstracted from sensible matter, so far they are wont to be referred to the *common* part.) In the meantime, for the sake of a better order and more fittingly to our purpose, it pleases us — saving a better judgment and with the good leave of any — to hand down the division of Metaphysics differently. Far be it, meanwhile, that we reject the common and received division: nor yet is there reason for anyone to wonder if, by the diversity of division, we respond diversely to the same question. For example: if it be asked, *to which part of Metaphysics does the natural body belong, if we take it precisely as it has predicates really abstract from sensible matter* — the answer is: if you take up the vulgarly-received division of Metaphysics into *common* and *proper* parts, it pertains to *both* — to the *common* indeed in so far as the natural body has predicates really abstracted from sensible matter which are *most common* to the natural body in community with other inferiors; to the *proper*, if the natural body's predicates really abstracted from sensible matter be considered which are *not common to all inferiors of being*: as, when the natural body is considered as *being*, as *one*, as *true being*, &c., the most common predicates are considered, whose treatment altogether pertains to the common part. But if the natural body be considered as *substance*, as *second substance*, &c., those predicates are attended to whose consideration is wont to be referred to the *special* part. — By our division, however, the whole consideration of the predicates really abstracted from sensible matter — which belong to the natural body — is rightly referred to one and the same part, since all and single those predicates are really abstract from sensible matter, not by absolute but by permissive abstraction.

**§ 38.** But this we especially note concerning our proposed division: that **to Metaphysics there also belong those inferiors of being which, though not themselves really abstract from sensible matter, nevertheless have predicates really abstracted from sensible matter**: e.g. natural body, sky, tree, stone, animal, &c., are inferiors of being and are not really abstract from sensible matter (since they cannot be without matter, and matter itself is their essential part); yet they do have predicates abstracted from sensible matter — being, substance, &c.

**§ 39.** ***The use of Metaphysics is nothing other than the fitting, decent, and genuine application of the metaphysical doctrines to the other disciplines and faculties.***

**§ 40.** ***And the use of Metaphysics is twofold: general and special.*** The *general* extends to all disciplines and faculties. The *special* refers to one precise discipline — e.g. Theology, forensic Jurisprudence, Moral Philosophy, &c.

**§ 41.** And indeed as to the *general use* of Metaphysics — that which Metaphysics itself furnishes rather than promises to the cultivators and doctors of all the Faculties and disciplines — no man will deny it who rightly attends to the office of the Professor and Master of Metaphysics; which is comprehended in these two: (1) that he hand down **the most common notions**, most familiar to every kind of discipline and faculty, so that certainly no discipline can excuse itself from them, and so that the cultivators and doctors of every faculty — nill they, will they — must own the eminent use of these notions. Of this sort are, for example, the notions of *formal reason*, *essence*, of *adequate* and *inadequate concept*, of *primary* and *secondary* concept, of *principle*, of *cause*, of *ratio of causing*, of *causality*, of *necessary conditions*, of *order*, of *formal effect*, of *form*, of *subject*, of *object*, of *end*, of *efficient*, of *impulsive cause*, of *foundation*, of *sign*, of *norm*, of *actus primus*, *actus secundus*, *actus signatus*, *actus exercitus*, of *complete cause*, of *subordinated causes*, of *coordinated causes*, &c. Here also belong the *highest genera* of all things. Certainly no discipline can be learned or taught with any happy accuracy unless the use of these notions is admitted. (2) That he open up ***the most common and the first fountains of knowing*** — that is, exhibit the most common dicta and axioms upon which all doctrines and disciplines whatsoever rely, into which all the rest of Philosophy at last runs and resolves, and which are most common to Theology, to Jurisprudence, and to the science of the art of medicine.

**§ 42.** Deliberately we say *the axioms and first principles of Theology and Jurisprudence are most common.* For what is vulgarly said — that *Philosophy relies on rational principles, Theology and Jurisprudence on bare authority* — is said indeed most truly, but nowise to the prejudice of our assertion. It is said most truly, because a great difference does intercede between theological and juridical argumentations on the one side and philosophical ones on the other. In philosophical argumentations, when the *principle of knowing* is at once the *cause*, it is either connected immediately with the extremes of the conclusion, or mediately: if the former, it is a first principle, known *per se*, and needs no proof from elsewhere; if the latter, it must be proved from some other prior principle, yet that very principle from which the connection has to be demonstrated is drawn from the nature of the thing itself. But in Theology and in forensic Jurisprudence the matter stands otherwise. For though Theology, in matters to be told, teaches that one is the cause of another — e.g. that faith is the cause of justification, and justification the cause of grace with God, of tranquillity of a good conscience, and of hope of eternal life — yet since this is not known to us except out of **Sacred Scripture** and divine revelation, we must at last recur to divine revelation as the ultimate principle: **Whatsoever the Canonical Sacred Scripture teaches is infallibly true** — whose certainty cannot escape anyone, nor be called into doubt by any, who has the nature and essence of Sacred Scripture rightly before his eyes. Similarly, though forensic Jurisprudence treats of things through their causes — as e.g. if any would fully *prove* a testament having full juridical effect, he must prove that all and single causes, and all and single necessary requisites of making a will (as they say) on the side of the causes, were present: on the side of the **efficient cause**, that the testator (that is, he whose testament is now fully to be proved) is *paterfamilias*, of sound mind, not a minor, &c.; on the side of **matter** or **object**, that he has free administration of his goods; and, if the testator is a *filiusfamilias*, that he has made testament only about *peculium castrense* or *quasi-castrense*, &c.; on the side of **form** or **mode**, that he employed and specifically called the witnesses — and, since he was blind, employed besides the seven witnesses also a *tabularius*, or an eighth witness, &c.; on the side of **end**, that he has not been deprived of testamentary faculty for a crime or for some other public cause, &c. — yet because of these matters nothing can be clear to anyone except out of the **Civil Law** and the decrees of the legislator, we must at last take refuge in the Civil Law; and the so-called Corpus of Civil Law is itself the seat and quasi-domicile of the principles of Law, to which the Jurisconsult must equally recur, and from which his proofs must be sought — just as the Theologian is bound to flee to Sacred Scripture, if any dogma of the faith among Christians be called into doubt and must be confirmed by solid arguments. There remains therefore the ***first principle*** into which all juridical proofs are at last resolved: **the authority of Civil Law**; so that whatever Civil Law establishes, ratifies, approves, is *civilly just*, that is, has the effect of civil law.

**§ 43.** Rightly, therefore, is to be understood what the Theologians and Jurisconsults commonly teach, when they say of themselves: *We argue only from authority; the Philosophers argue only from reason.* Namely, there must not be confounded the ***first principle proper*** of Theology or of Jurisprudence, and the ***first principles of Theology and Jurisprudence common in use with other disciplines*** (deliberately we say *common in use*, since the same principles remain partly Metaphysical, partly Analytical by their own proper consideration). Most true indeed is this of the first principle *proper* to Theology or to Civil Jurisprudence, into which (as we said) all theological and juridical proofs run and are at last resolved — for whatever has to be proved by Theologians or by Jurisconsults, these men prove not from anywhere else than, the one from Sacred Scripture, the other from Civil Law. But the students of Theology and of Civil Jurisprudence certainly deceive themselves — as many as persuade themselves in vain that they can therefore do without the study of Metaphysics and of Logic; they deceive themselves — as many as are haters and gnawers of these studies — since Theologians and Jurisconsults, just as the Philosophers, argue from cause, from end, from efficient, from object, from form, from subject, from effect, from property, &c. And to say it briefly: all those modes of arguing which are prescribed in Logic have their place also in Theology and in Jurisprudence, if the speech be of *proximate principles* from which theological and juridical conclusions are proximately deduced. Since these things are true and will easily, without further demonstration, obtain assent from all who are endowed with sound judgment, who, I ask — provided he be free of preconceived opinions — would doubt of the manifest use of the metaphysical study in Theology and Jurisprudence as in the other disciplines?

**§ 44.** For it cannot be that any cultivator of Theology or Jurisprudence should rightly use the *loci* of argumentations unless he has previously known and intimately seen through the metaphysical doctrine, since there is no argumentation from a logical *locus* that cannot and must not be referred to some chapter of the Metaphysical doctrine. For example: how can theological or juridical questions concerning the *causes* of spiritual and theological or forensic things and businesses be answered unless you have thoroughly held the Metaphysical doctrine of *causes*? All questions, if we except the question *an sit*? "*is it*?", are — as in the other disciplines, so also in Theology and Jurisprudence (except where there is discussion of the sense of a dictum of Sacred Scripture or of civil law) — about *one or some or many or all causes*. In Theology and in forensic Jurisprudence it is only when the cause of each theological or forensic thing is fully known that something is truly known.

**§ 45.** Let us resume the examples cited above at § 42. Though it be clear only from Sacred Scripture that man is justified by faith, that he who is justified by faith has God propitious to him, that is, grace with God, tranquillity of conscience (i.e. a glad and tranquil conscience), and the hope of eternal life — yet it cannot be denied that in this theological argumentation — *Whoever is justified has peace of conscience, grace with God, hope of eternal life. But whosoever believes in Christ is justified. Therefore...* (whose Major is proved from **Rom. 5:1–2**, whose Minor from **Rom. 3:28**) — ***justification*** stands as cause, and ***peace of conscience, grace with God, hope of eternal life*** stand as effects of justification; and this whole argumentation is drawn from the *locus of cause*, and rests on this double axiom: ***Posita causa ponitur effectus*** — "Given the cause, the effect is given"; and ***Causa causae est causa causati*** — "The cause of a cause is the cause of what is caused" (for *faith* is the cause of justification, and justification itself is the cause of peace of conscience, of grace with God, of hope of eternal life; and so faith is the cause of peace of conscience, of grace with God, of hope of eternal life). For these two do not fight with one another: *The premisses in this theological argumentation contain the principle of being, i.e. the cause why the predicate belongs to the subject*; and, *These premisses are at last resolved into the principle of knowing, which is not at once the principle of being*. Both remain most true. For since neither premiss can be proved except out of Sacred Scripture, the whole argumentation is at last resolved into this common principle: ***Whatsoever Sacred Scripture says is infallibly true*** — which principle is not a principle *of being*, but only a principle *of knowing*. **Behold, then, the use of Metaphysics in Sacred Scripture!**

**§ 46.** Let the same judgment be made of the other example cited (§ 42). For although it be known to us only from Civil Law that the faculty of testating is granted by Civil Law only to *patresfamilias* (*paterfamilias uti legassit*, &c.), and that *filiifamilias* cannot make a testament (**Institutes**, title *Quibus non est permissum facere testamentum*; **Dig.** 28.1.6; **C.** eod. tit., §penult.) — and formerly indeed could not, because they had nothing of their own (**Ulp. tit. 20**, §9); nor to-day can, since they do not yet have anything by full right as *patresfamilias* save for *peculium castrense* or *quasi* (princip. *Institutes*, ead. tit.); likewise that those who are destitute of mind cannot make a testament (**Inst.** eod. loc.; **Dig.** 28.1.2 §13 *in fine*, *de inoff. test.*); likewise that even the testament of minors is void, though they left something in time of plague, or with authority of guardians, or for pious causes (**Carpzov, Pars III, Const. 6, Def. 8**); likewise that the blind cannot make a testament except by the observance which the law of divine Justinian (namely **C.** 6.22.8, *Quibus non est permissum facere testamentum*) introduced — by which it is provided that the blind, about to name an heir, shall employ besides the seven witnesses also a *tabularius*, who shall receive the will of the one declaring into writing; or, if there is no copy of a *tabularius* at hand, an eighth witness; and all shall subscribe and seal. — Though, I say, these and far more things, which we here pass over with dry foot, be known to someone from Civil Law alone, yet it remains most true that these matters either are themselves causes or arguments on the side of necessary requisite causes, which cannot be felicitously and accurately understood by one who has not — or has not well — ascertained *what and how many causes* obtain in forensic matters, and hence also in testaments. For as we said: the full science of any forensic thing consists in the fitting cognition of all its causes. How, then, could one destitute of the Metaphysical and Analytic doctrine (whose chief and most eminent part is that which treats of *causes*) discern the true and full science of any forensic thing from a proletarian or rude or imperfect notice of the same? — I rejoice that men of the greatest Judgment fully agree in this.

**§ 47.** For the rest, we must not entirely pass over this: that the very ***first principle proper to Theology, absolutely so-called***, can itself be resolved into another ***first principle common to Theology with the other sciences***. For *the principle absolutely first proper to Theology* was: **Whatsoever Canonical Sacred Scripture teaches is infallibly true.** Now there is no doubt but that this can be resolved into that axiom which, considered in its proper subject, is Metaphysical, but in use is common to all disciplines and faculties: ***Impossibile est idem simul esse et non esse*** — "It is impossible that the same thing simultaneously be and not be" — thus: *Unless what Canonical Sacred Scripture teaches were infallibly true, God, who is immutable Truth itself, would simultaneously be and not be. He is — as is manifest not only from Sacred Scripture but is also solidly demonstrated in Metaphysics, both from His infinite science (for the omniscient cannot be deceived) and from His infinite goodness (for He who is of infinite goodness neither wills to deceive, nor can, nor can will to deceive). He would not be, since by His word He would deceive or could deceive — for Sacred Scripture is the word of God; and how would God, who is immutable Truth itself, remain immutable Truth, unless His word were infallibly true?* The whole discourse, as every man easily sees, rests on this axiom: *It is impossible for the same thing simultaneously to be and not to be.* For it is one thing to ask whether Sacred Scripture is canonical and of divine authority; another, whether what Sacred Scripture teaches is infallibly true. The decision of the latter question, dependent on the decision of the former, at last runs into that axiom and is ultimately resolved. — And what need is there of words? Does not the Author of Sacred Scripture Himself, that is, God Himself, make the same analysis and assert the same thing? Let us look at the words of the Epistle to the Romans, c. 11, v. 6: ***If by grace, then is it no more of works: otherwise grace is no more grace. But if it be of works, then is it no more grace: otherwise work is no more work.*** Does not the Holy Spirit assert this truth — *If by grace, not of works? If of works, not of grace?* — and resolve it into this axiom: *It is absolutely absurd and simply impossible that the same thing simultaneously be and not be*: e.g. that it be *grace* and *not be grace*, that it be *work* and *not be work*. — Therefore these two do not fight with one another: *That there is a principle absolutely first of Theology, and that it can be resolved into another principle*. For that is called the *absolutely-first principle of some discipline* which is the proper principle of that discipline; and absolutely remains Sacred Scripture alone the principle of Theology — not those philosophical principles that are *common* to Theology with Philosophy. But those which Sacred Scripture furnishes are the proper and strictly-so-called principles of Theology.

Entirely the same, *mutatis mutandis*, must be said concerning the simply first principle of forensic Jurisprudence. It is beyond doubt that forensic Jurisprudence argues *only from authority*, if this be understood of the absolutely first principle of civil Jurisprudence — which at the same time, in another sense, is most rightly called the *ultimately simplest* principle: yet nothing hinders that this ultimate principle be rightly resolved into the most well-known axiom: ***It is impossible for the same thing simultaneously to be and not to be***. For are not these contradictory — *to be sanctioned and approved by civil law*, and *yet not to have the effect of civil law*? To be at once *sanctioned and approved by civil law*, and *not to be so*, is contradictory.

**§ 48.** Let them, then, who can and will judge, judge whether a man can pronounce — saving truth, and freed from either affected and gross ignorance or malice — who did not blush to write: *"That all metaphysical doctrine, all distinctions and terms metaphysical, and above all those distinctions and terms as they are handed down from Stahl, the Professor of Morals is ignorant of — is no more reason to blush than it is for a courtier not to smell of a goat." Item: "In the whole metaphysical science the chief thing is that he who knows that science alone knows nothing."* As to the first: all metaphysical propositions which enunciate something of every being, if they be restricted to moral persons, actions, and things — likewise to persons, actions, and things that are of civil society — in a way become and remain proper to Moral and Civil Philosophy, and to the discipline of the Law of Nature and of Nations; since the Professor of Ethics and Politics, of Natural Law and the Law of Nations (if he wish to discharge his office rightly, as he needs must) uses in this way the *κοινὰς ἀρχάς* — *common principles* — as Aristotle is wont to speak. Nay, all those things which we have just set out concerning the use of Metaphysics in Theology and Jurisprudence are to be drawn with equal, if not greater, right to Ethics, Politics, and the doctrine of Natural Law and the Law of Nations. For *far more easily* will the use of the metaphysical study be persuaded to students of Philosophy in Morals and Politics (above all in Morals, since this is the doctrine of law absolutely immutable) than the use of Metaphysics in Theology and Jurisprudence will be persuaded to the cultivators of Theology and Jurisprudence. — As to what was asserted in the last place, *that the chief thing in the whole metaphysical science is that he who knows it alone knows nothing* — let others see whether here imprudence rather than impudence must be charged. For here is confounded, in a shameful way, the office of the Professor of Metaphysics (which was indicated above at § 41, p. 27, &c., and which belongs to him in so far as he is a *sapiens* — for in what sense Metaphysics, and only Metaphysics, is wisdom, is clear from §§ 19 and following; in this sense also the Metaphysician, and only he, is a *sapiens*) with the office which he discharges in so far as he is *sciens*, precisely considered — as he is, no less than the Doctors of the other theoretical disciplines. For we have said that Metaphysics is both wisdom and science. By the force of this latter office it belongs to the Doctor of Metaphysics to *demonstrate*, that is — as we define it — syllogistically to deduce the necessary certitude of the propositions set forth, from certain principles as from their causes, to be known with no doubt. Of what kind, moreover, those propositions must be through which Metaphysical demonstrations are made, can and ought to be clear from the Analytic. For what is sought by demonstrations is a *certain, limpid cognition, immune from the fear of error*. Whence, if to a suitable Doctor of Metaphysics there are at hand propositions *truly* truly so, not out of mere concession or hypothesis, but *first* also — such namely that they need no further proof, but by their own evidence merit assent; or at least such that at last they can be reduced to some *first truth*, if the Metaphysician continues the argumentations from a demonstrable proposition up to that point until some first principle is reached; and *immediate*, that is, without interruption or hiatus flowing continuously from themselves; and finally *causes of the conclusion*, so that they contain the reason why in the demonstrable proposition the predicate necessarily belongs to the subject — if, I say, propositions of this kind, *true, first, immediate, and causes of the conclusion*, are at hand to the Doctor of Metaphysics in sufficient number, through which first in his own and afterwards in the minds of others he has been able and still is able to generate the certain, limpid cognition immune from the fear of error — by what colour or pretext can it be said that *he who knows Metaphysics, in so far as he knows it, knows absolutely nothing*? — Next, by the consent of all the most learned Philosophers, whatsoever belongs to the theoretical sciences and disciplines has this in common, that it is to be sought for its own sake and so for the sake of the very knowing — why then would the same not have to be said of the metaphysical science and discipline? — Thirdly, so far is it that the metaphysical science is not to be sought for its own sake and so for the knowing's sake, that rather it stands most certainly, which every illustrious Philosopher has pronounced of Metaphysics alone (and which in Aristotle's judgment is one of the most accurate specific marks of Wisdom or of First Philosophy, which men now call Metaphysics), namely that *it is to be sought maximally for its own sake and so for the sake of the very knowing*. This genuine condition of Metaphysics is more at length explained and demonstrated by Aristotle at *Metaphysics* Book I, c. 2 (which whole chapter, together with the preceding, deserves earnest inspection). The words of the Philosopher are these: *"To know and to know for the sake of knowing itself is most seen in that science which is conversant about the most knowable. He who chooses science for the sake of knowing will embrace most of all that which is most worthy of the name of science; and such is that which is conversant about that thing which is most apt to be known. And most apt to be known are the things which are first and the causes, since by these and from these the rest are known..."* Most truly spoken: for does not Metaphysics expressly claim for herself the whole of *Pneumatics*, as it is called — that is, the doctrine of Spirit, God, angels, and souls, which are of spiritual essence, most of all as these are considered in the separated state? But what nobler or equally noble objects, whose science is to be sought equally or more for its own sake, could be handed down or imagined? — Fourthly, even if (which however we believe to be asserted not only contrary to Aristotle's Philosophy but to truth itself) *Pneumatics* did not pertain directly and *per se* to Metaphysics, yet many other and more things occur in the Metaphysical discipline rightly treated which can be derived from certain principles so that no further room for doubt remains, and the whole Metaphysical science — even setting apart its most noble and most eminent part, which treats of Spirit, God, angels, and the separated soul — rests on such foundations that from them genuine demonstrations, apt to beget solid science, can be deduced. If therefore the chief thing in the Metaphysical science were that *he who knows it alone knows nothing*, this must be said equally, nay far more, of all other theoretical sciences, Physics and the Mathematical Sciences: for Metaphysics is the most exact and the most accurate of all the natural sciences, since it is maximally the science of the *first things*. Rightly says Aristotle (to demonstrate this of Metaphysics, *loc. cit.*): *"Of the sciences, those are the most accurate which are conversant with first things. For those which conclude the thing from fewer are more accurate than those which conclude it with addition — as Arithmetic than Geometry. And Metaphysics is also more apt for teaching, which is more occupied in the consideration of causes. For those teach, who adduce the cause of each thing."* Lastly, we proceed thus: Either Metaphysics as a whole is uniquely and solely for the sake of the other sciences and disciplines, or not at all. If not at all, it follows of itself that he who knows that science alone does know something. If so, then by the greatest ignorance — if not by malice — Metaphysics would be exposed to the mockeries of ignoramuses. For otherwise the same fate would await Analytic, concerning which it has been sufficiently established that she is nothing with all the sciences and disciplines abolished, nor could she be anything if there were no place for sciences and disciplines. But for all that, must we proceed with level foot to the sciences and disciplines themselves without the help and benefit of Analytic — because, with the sciences and disciplines abolished, to wish to know her alone (which indeed we grant) is to wish to know nothing? We must learn Analytic, both because it is the *common propaedeutic* (as noted at § 5, p. 3) without which it is impossible, or certainly most difficult, to get sciences, and because it makes us apt for dealing with auditors, so that what we ourselves have solidly, limpidly, and without any fear of error come to know, we may (let it be said so) *scientifically* transfuse into the auditor also, recalling everything to the Analytic rules. The same would be the judgment of Metaphysics, if we said that a good and most ample part of her — like Analytic — passes over so many times into another science or discipline, as often as to that other it is fitly, learnedly, and becomingly applied; perhaps we would have sinned in nothing. For this sentiment the Greeks most keenly championed; and the same, as being most congruent to truth itself and to the mind of Aristotle, has been embraced also by the most accurate Philosopher of the University of Altdorf, **Philipp Scherb**, and by those who succeeded him in order — the great-named Philosophers **Ernst Soner**, **Michael Piccart**, and **Johannes Kobius**. — But these things in passing, and *ἐκ παρόδῳ* [by way of a parenthesis], let be said. Rather we press on to the matter itself, since we are now setting about to hand down, with the good God's help, the very foundations of this discipline — to which when it has been happily completed we have resolved to add our ***Apparatus Metaphysicus***, that is, a brief disquisition on the suitable and necessary means and aids by which those students must be equipped who in the University would carry off the fruit of metaphysical studies; where there will likewise be opportunity of inspecting *which Commenders, which Momi and Detractors Wisdom (i.e. Metaphysics) has had and now has*. Meantime we leave that man to his own insipid wisdom — a man who thinks so abjectly of wisdom.

**§ 49.** Only this remains to be said concerning the **general use of Metaphysics**. The **special use** we are ready to show in the very progress of this our labour. One thing only may it please me to note in advance: namely, we readily grant that the *posterior part* of Metaphysics, as we defined it in § 36, p. 23, does not promise an equally great use to the cultivators of the other disciplines; yet of great moment is its use in *revealed Theology* and in *Morals*. For to the right perception and demonstration of the duty of *piety* and of the whole *divine cult*, *Natural Theology* contributes much — Natural Theology being an eminent and most noble part, into which, along with others, the *posterior part* is subdivided. For how could it be sufficiently taught or learnt in Morals that *God is to be worshipped above all things*, unless it stood most solidly from Metaphysics that *God is Being of beings, highest and most perfect being, from no other being, but the cause of all other beings*? How could it be demonstrated in Morals that *God alone is to be worshipped above all things*, unless by Metaphysical reason it could be most certainly demonstrated that *God is one in essence*? How could it be inferred from a solid Moral principle that *God is to be worshipped with spiritual worship, and the best mind is the fairest worship of God*, unless it were clear from Metaphysics that *God is spirit*? How could it be deduced by genuine Moral reasons that *God is always and everywhere to be worshipped*, unless from Metaphysics the *eternity* and *omnipresence* of God were demonstrable? And so forth. For altogether twofold is Natural Theology: one *theoretic*, which is the part of First Philosophy or Metaphysics; the other *practical*, which is the part of Moral Philosophy. Of which matter it will be treated *ex professo* in its own place, namely in the posterior part of these Institutes. Meanwhile every man can easily reach the special use which the *posterior part* furnishes in *revealed Theology*, if we would avoid the *faulty leap from genus to genus*, and so take care lest we confound *Natural Theology* with *Revealed Theology* — which confusion is wholly at war with the office of a good cultivator or master of Metaphysics.

**§ 50.** These things, then, be said of the nature, constitution, and use of Metaphysics. More things which could be said of these matters — likewise, *which published writings are most apt for the obtaining of a metaphysical erudition* — the promised ***APPARATUS METAPHYSICUS***, at the close of these Institutes, will perhaps indicate then with better fruit.
