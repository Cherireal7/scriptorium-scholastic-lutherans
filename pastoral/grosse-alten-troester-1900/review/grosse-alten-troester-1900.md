# Translator's Note

*This working translation is at its kick-off state. The paragraphs below record the intended text base, the editorial conventions binding on the eventual body prose, and — with full disclosure — what remains to be verified once the 1900 imprint has been sighted paragraph by paragraph.*

## 1. The text base

The English rendered here follows the **1900 Hermannsburg** edition of Constantin Große's *Die alten Tröster: Ein Wegweiser in die Erbauungslitteratur der evangelisch-lutherischen Kirche des 16. bis 18. Jahrhunderts*, published in commission with the Hermannsburger Missionshandlung. This is Große's own first — and to the best of the bibliographic record only — edition of the work; there is no expanded second edition or posthumous recension, and the text-base question is therefore trivial.

The working text-base is the fully-digitised 1900 imprint held by the **Internet Archive** under identifier `diealtentroeste00grosgoog`, a scan derived from the Google Books digitisation of a copy held in a partner-library collection. The scan comprises vi + [2] + 700 pages + one folding leaf of chronology-table, and is accompanied by a Tesseract 5.0.0 optical-character-recognition text layer, run in German-Fraktur mode and reported by the Internet Archive at a script-detection confidence of 0.9596 — meaningfully higher than the OCR quality typical of seventeenth-century Fraktur imprints, and correspondingly less demanding of paragraph-by-paragraph correction, though the workflow discipline of eye-verifying every paragraph against the facsimile image remains in force.

The bibliographic authority record is **GND 101703465** (Deutsche Nationalbibliothek); the specialist bibliography Enzyklothek indexes the work under entry `4cc90c2c-442a-4744-9b3f-1427b4428440`. VD17 does not apply (the register covers 1601–1700); VD18 does not apply (1701–1800); the 1900 imprint falls outside both registers.

## 2. What the author himself supplies

Große's own hand supplies the entirety of the volume — the front matter, the introductory account of what *Erbauungslitteratur* is and by what criterion he selects the writers he surveys, the biographical-and-bibliographical entries on the individual writers he treats, the folding-table chronology of the whole tradition, and the register that closes the volume. The book is not an anthology and it is not a collection of primary sources; it is Große's own scholarship, from cover to cover.

His entries on individual devotional writers do quote — often at length — from the writers' own works, and those quotations preserve the writers' own citations to scripture, the church fathers, and earlier Lutheran devotional authors. All of these are preserved unchanged in the translation. Modern-critical equivalents (WA / LW for Luther, CR / MSA for Melanchthon, BSLK / *Kolb-Wengert* for confessional documents, PL / PG for the church fathers, ESV numbering for scripture alongside Große's own citation form on first occurrence per major section) are consigned to the endnotes.

One authorial fact deserves early acknowledgement: Große writes from the **Hermannsburg mission-house** in 1900, and the volume is issued by the Missionshandlung — the publishing arm of Louis Harms's Hermannsburg-Mission. Große's stance is that of a late-nineteenth-century confessional-Lutheran churchman, aligned with the missional-confessional Lutheran project of his own century and sympathetic to the American Missouri-Synod trajectory. His evaluations of individual devotional writers — who among them remains theologically sound, who is already tending toward pietism, who veers into mysticism — are theologically located, not neutral. The translation preserves his evaluations as his own; the translator's voice does not adjudicate on his adjudications.

## 3. What the Scholastic Lutherans have added — and where

*Nothing in the body text is our own.* All editorial additions are relegated to the numbered endnotes at the end of the volume. These endnotes supply, and only supply:

- Standard modern citations for the primary Lutheran works Große discusses (WA / LW for Luther; CR / MSA for Melanchthon; standard modern-critical editions and, where they exist, cross-references to the *Scholastic Lutherans* translation program's own working translations of the same writers)
- Standard modern citations for the church fathers and confessional documents Große cites (PL / PG / CCSL / SC volume:column; BSLK / *Kolb-Wengert*; ESV numbering for scripture with Große's own citation form retained on first occurrence per section)
- Biographical dates on first named mention of each Lutheran devotional writer, in the standard scholarly convention (e.g. **Philipp Nicolai** [1556–1608])
- Modern place-names and modern spellings for the toponyms and personal names Große spells in his 1900 Fraktur convention (Große spells *Chytraeus* as *Chyträus*, and so on — his spelling on first occurrence in italic; the modern editorial form thereafter in the English body)
- Brief historical-theological notes where the *modern* historiographical judgement on a writer differs from Große's late-nineteenth-century confessional-Lutheran assessment, and where preserving Große's judgement in the body without a note would risk misrepresenting the state of subsequent scholarship — always flagged as an editorial addition, never inserted into the body prose
- Cross-references to the *Scholastic Lutherans* corpus proper: where Große treats a writer whose primary work is itself a translation target of this program (e.g. Musculus in Dogmatics, Balduin in Pastoral, Selnecker in Dogmatics, Chytraeus in Catechetical), the endnote directs the reader to the corpus volume — making explicit the function of Große's *Wegweiser* as a reference companion to the whole program

Body prose contains no modern scholarship, no editorial voice, no bracketed insertions. Everything is either Große or Große's cited authorities.

## 4. Editorial conventions

- **Register.** Große's register is measured late-nineteenth-century scholarly German — expository, biographical, evaluative, missional-pastoral in cadence, not archaising and not partisan-polemical. The English mirrors that register: scholarly and clear, reverent where the subject-matter is reverence, evaluative where Große evaluates. This is *not* the "reverent-but-not-archaising" register of the primary-source volumes in the *Scholastic Lutherans* program (Chytraeus, Flacius); it is the register appropriate to a Wegweiser — a survey, a guide, a reference work.
- **Latin technical terms** — *pietas*, *Erbauung*, *Andacht*, *Trost*, *Betrachtung*, *Übung*, *Anfechtung* — are preserved on first occurrence in each major section in italic, glossed briefly, and rendered in English thereafter, unless the term is itself the subject under discussion, in which case the German or Latin form is retained.
- **Greek** appears where Große quotes patristic sources; preserved in polytonic script, with parenthetical transliteration and English gloss on first substantive occurrence.
- **Hebrew** appears rarely; when it does, it is preserved in pointed Hebrew script with transliteration and gloss on first occurrence.
- **Scripture.** ESV for the English rendering. Große's own citation form (Luther-Bible numeration, occasionally Vulgate) is noted parenthetically on first citation per section.
- **Fraktur normalisation.** Long-*s* (ſ) is silently modernised to round *s*. Umlauts and *ß* are preserved in German source citations (not in the English body). Große's own 1900-convention spellings of 16th–17th-century proper names (*Chyträus* for *Chytraeus*, *Aegidius Hunnius* variants) are preserved in italic on first occurrence; the standard modern editorial spelling is used in the English body thereafter.
- **Named writers.** For each Lutheran devotional writer to whom Große devotes a substantial entry, life-dates are supplied in the endnote on first named mention — never in the body. Where the writer is the subject of a *Scholastic Lutherans* corpus volume, the endnote cross-references the corpus.
- **Confessional restraint.** The translator's voice does not adjudicate on Große's confessional evaluations. His judgements — sympathetic to some writers, sharp with others, drawing the pietism-boundary at specific places — are preserved as his own. Modern historiographical revisions live in endnotes flagged as editorial additions.
- **Rubrication, typeface, layout** — locked via `shared/reference-custom.docx` and identical to the Praetorius trilogy, the completed Chytraeus volume, and the in-flight Flacius volume.

## 5. Scope disclosure

The 1900 Hermannsburg imprint comprises six pages of Roman-numeraled front matter, two unnumbered pages, seven hundred pages of body, and one folding leaf of chronology-table. Its internal arrangement — chronological by century, or thematic by devotional-genre (prayer-books, hymn-collections, meditations, funeral-consolations, catechetical exposition), or biographical-portrait-by-author — is the first structural question the translation must resolve against Große's own sighted table of contents at the opening of Tranche 1. The provisional tranche plan in the working notes assumes chronological-by-century as the most probable arrangement, with re-cutting expected once the table of contents is sighted.

At this working-translation state, the scaffolding is complete: the text base is fixed, the editorial conventions are set, the source has been sighted at the level of catalogue-and-facsimile-availability, and the initial tranche plan is drawn. Body prose accrues tranche by tranche.

Two corrections to the parallel-work coordination memo are recorded transparently in the working notes and are surfaced here for the reader's benefit: the author is **Constantin Große** (not Alexander Grosse — a different person, an English Puritan divine of a different century), and the year of the imprint is **1900** (not 1614 — the coordination memo appears to have taken the earliest date of the *subject-matter* for the year of the *work itself*). The genre-shift consequent on these corrections is from a putative patristic-consolation anthology to a Lutheran-confessional scholarly guide-to-the-tradition. The volume's function inside the *Scholastic Lutherans* corpus is correspondingly recast: not a primary source in the pastoral bucket, but a reference companion to the whole corpus, mapping the terrain the program's other volumes translate piece by piece.

## Vorwort

*The Author's Foreword. Signed simply* Der Verfasser *(the author) at the close.*

In Number 45 of the *Sächsisches Kirchen- und Schulblatt* for 1896, on the occasion of an inquiry concerning Schmolck's *Kommunionbüchlein*, the wish was expressed for a "Register of the Prayer-Literature of Our Old Ones."² In Numbers 47 and 49 there followed a reply from Herr Deacon Pastor König of Hirschfelde, giving some particulars on the printing of various Schmolckian books. He closed his reply with these words: "It would surely be very much blessed if the wished-for *Register of the Prayer-Literature of Our Old Ones* were once put together and made public, in order to reawaken the love for them, which stands in danger of dying out. I should gladly lend my own hand to it, were some time only given me, and could I count on the help of brethren experienced in this field."

These expressions accorded with what had been my favourite study from my university years onward. Since I had been in the pastoral office, the thought had from time to time occupied me: whether one might not, with a writing on the old devotional literature of our Church joined with the outlines of its history, round off a subject worthy of nearer treatment — one with which one could serve the brethren in office, and interested laymen with them, both for the care of souls and for their own edification. Then I came upon the remarks quoted above in the *Kirchen- und Schulblatt*. They met with my own thoughts. In a letter I communicated as much to Herr Deacon Pastor König, and he encouraged me to carry my intentions out.

To be sure, a bare register of the older devotional literature has, in my judgement, too small a purpose. Publishers' Christmas-catalogues and the like already serve that end: they all contain, under the headings "Edifying Works," "Prayer-Books," "Prayer- and Devotional-Books," and other such rubrics, tolerably rich compilations, out of which the old devotional literature can be found with ease. If more than the bare register was to be given, then the task was to compose a searching description of the literature according to form and content, physical get-up, and so forth. Whether a book is to be recommended or not, in what measure it is to be recommended, and to whom — the discussion of such questions led of itself onto the history of the book's coming-to-be, the history the book itself has had since, the history of its author, and the piety of the age from which it stems.

So the register widened, under my hand, into a *Guide to the Devotional Literature of the Evangelical-Lutheran Church of the Sixteenth through Eighteenth Centuries*, so far as that literature is again or still accessible in particular through new editions in our own time, in connexion with a searching description of the literature, a portrayal of its history in outline, and of the lives of its authors. In that same connexion a modest attempt was made to give something — by way of examples — of the development of Christian-churchly piety, chiefly of the life of prayer in the Lutheran Church. For that is the soil out of which these writings grew; that is the soil to which they in turn gave nourishment.

Now that I put the book into the hands of the public, I wish only that it may be a welcome help for preaching and for the care of souls to the brethren in the office — to whom the author, in giving it out, has thought first of all — and that it may also serve Christian laymen (and especially the Christian book-trade) in the buying and selling of devotional literature.

To all who have lent the author a hand in his labour, my thanks are here finally set down. Particular thanks are due to the many book-dealers who supported him most kindly with catalogues, inspection-copies, and the like, indeed in some measure with gratis copies, and made possible for him the sighting of the older editions of the literature in question.

May the book work with its little part to show forth the glory of the Lutheran Church and to awaken love toward her! May the Lord grant that many by its means may find the way into the Old Comforters themselves, unto richly-blessed edification!

*The Author.*

## Foreword

*By D. Fr. Hashagen, Rostock. Placed by the author immediately after his own Vorwort and before the Einleitung.*

With hearty joy I greeted and read this work, which offers an introduction to devotional literature — an introduction resting on thorough studies, industriously wrought, warm with life — and, above all, into those churchly-ascetical writings that are of most worth for doctrine and for life; and in doing so it meets a need plainly present. — When a proverb of the East declares that the fruit-bearing palm must have its foot in the water and its head in the fire, then in the case of the "Old Comforters," those palms of God, both conditions are met in unusual measure. They stem for the greater part — as the present work again and repeatedly shows from the sources — from times of the heaviest shakings of public and personal life, and from times of great inward *Anfechtung*. When the earth quaked and the mountains sank into the midst of the sea, those men of God felt the earthquake also in their own hearts, and knew that the creature — every firmest thing on which one had until then relied — no longer stood fast. Then the living God, the God of all patience and of all comfort, became in his eternal Word and Gospel their sole refuge. As surely as they could say, "This is my misery," so surely, by God's grace, could they also bear witness, "This is my comfort; for thy Word quickeneth me" (Ps. 119:50).³ In them is fulfilled: "Those that be planted in the house of the LORD shall flourish in the courts of our God: they shall still bring forth fruit in old age; they shall be fat and flourishing" (Ps. 92:13–14).⁴ — May the present work, so aptly fitted for it, contribute powerfully to this: that the "Old Comforters" find in the study and in the parsonage, as also in the congregations everywhere, ever more open doors, and that their quiet blessed labour may be carried forward in ever richer measure!

*Rostock, on the Feast of the Reformation, 1899.*

*D. Fr. Hashagen.*

# Einleitung — Introduction

## §1. Who the "Old Comforters" Are

The "Old Comforters" — so this book calls itself. Upon them and upon their blessing it will fix the reader's attention. Who, then, are the "Old Comforters"? The name is used for the prayer- and devotional-books of the believing fathers of our Evangelical-Lutheran Church, which still today comfort the faithful; for those devotional writings out of which not only the author himself and his contemporaries sought and found comfort, but which have such worth that many generations, up to the present day, have been and still are edified by them. *Old Comforters* they were called at first often in mockery, but afterwards, held in high honour, unto their high honour — chiefly for the sake of the comfort they granted in the second half of the last century and the first half of the present one to the pious and the quiet in the land,⁵ everywhere, in the midst of an age of dry, empty rationalist faith and of the dreary enlightenment-wisdom of the pulpit. The children of God, who received in the Church stones for bread,⁶ had in these books, next after Scripture, good bread within their houses. Often they set them — wrongly, to be sure — beside, indeed above the Scripture, because the Comforters were easier for them to understand, and in many cases first made the understanding of God's Word possible. In the troubled seasons of the Church they became, among the means through which the old faith was carried over into the new time, no small part — so that the old faith, when it arose again in the *Erweckungszeit*, could catch hold at their hand.⁷ In the believing evangelical circles they are found everywhere. The personal Christian life, which is founded in baptism and preserved by the immediate Word of God in the Bible (and in preaching, and by the holy Supper), is prepared for, carried forward, established, deepened by the mediated Word of God, which in the devotional literature flows richly, pure and clear, out of the pure fount of Scripture. In right Christian houses the Old Comforters travel from generation to generation, from the forefathers down to the grandchildren. Each generation ought to be aware of the spirit that reigned in the family, and to see to it that the old is preserved — or one lets it lapse and vanish. This latter spirit is, alas, the reigning one in our own time. On the one hand, in single houses, [the old books are still passed down, are still received], are replaced by new [editions] when worn. But on the other hand — and there, alas, are the most — [the old books] are no longer used. The old *Erbauungsbuch* was there, but it lay dust-covered on the shelf or in the chest, and wandered at last into the fire or into the rag-heap. But the purchase of a new devotional book of the same content is not made, because one, entangled in this world, thinks himself risen above edification, pretends to have no time for edification, indeed has in fact no desire for it.

Here it is needful that the Christian people be once again reminded of these writings. Both they who prize them and they who despise them must be brought to know that these books, for a good part, are still to be had; that they have not outlived their day; that indeed they cannot outlive their day, in so far as they hold within them the wrought-through Word of God. To that end it is above all needful that **the pastors and teachers of the congregations be brought again into a proper acquaintance with them,** in order to be able to recommend them anew. That they should come rightly to know these Comforters, and to prize them, is of particular importance both for their own sake and for the sake of their congregations.

## §2. Their Value for the Pastor Himself and for the Congregation

They themselves must go deep into them. They will draw from them a rich gain for their inward life. What entrance do these books afford into the conflicts of the heart, into the mysteries of the Word of God and of the pure doctrine, into a true Christian life! The students and candidates especially, who at the universities, over against the demands of scholarship and criticism, or over against the demands of student and social life in fraternities and clubs, so easily neglect the inward life — such find here nourishment for the recovering of their own inner life, fruit ripe and springs fresh. The gifted preacher himself, no less than the one of lesser abilities, can here find blessing, progress, riches, strengthening, comfort, and cheer. There should be no clergyman at all who is not known and well versed in these our rich gardens; and it should long have been provided that those wishing to enter them should have direction and counsel. Would that many younger clergymen might take from what has been said the needful admonition and encouragement, and in their still-youthful years begin a holy, sweet, meditative study — one to which the quiet hours of a whole life might be devoted, without finding either want or weariness in it.⁸

Delitzsch says in his preface to Cober's *Passions- und Osterprediger* (1865, Frankfurt, Brönner, now out of print): "Whoever will become an able preacher and pastor does well to set these old orthodox teachers before his eyes; and whoever will come to know the order of grace of God and the wonderful, strange, but blessed ways along which the Lord recovers the human soul out of ruin and sets it ever more deeply into fellowship with himself — such a one will not err if he betakes himself to the school of these experience-rich servants of God."⁹

Consider also Delitzsch in the new edition of the *Christentumsfragen* of Ahasverus Fritsch, Dresden 1841 (now out of print): "The Church of God cannot advance without taking up into herself what has been won by the earlier Church already, and has been passed on to us as a pound to be traded with. For God grants not all things to any one age. The history of the Church is the history of grace received little by little, unto ever new insights, experiences, and victories. Accordingly, the Church rising up in our time may surpass the Church of the past — but only in the case that she makes over unto herself the good old, and receives a good new out of the hand of the Lord. Therefore must she go first backward, if she will go forward; she has to remember the earlier times up to this hour, and to consider what he has done unto the old Fathers. Ah, that I could with a loud-clanging voice cry into the hearts of Christians the exhortation: Read, read our dear Old Ones, and use them as guide-posts in the Scripture and as counsellors in the affairs of your souls! Let not the plain, unadorned, all so-called geniality-stripped speech in which they speak be strange to you — it is the depth of a clear stream, full of heavenly shapes!"¹⁰

## §3. Delitzsch on the Old Ones as Standards

Likewise, in the preface to the first edition (1844) of his classic *Communion-Book*¹¹ — a book written in the spirit of the Old Ones — Delitzsch sets the Old Ones before us as scarcely-attainable models:

"The confession- and communion-prayers which the following pages contain are not, as it might seem, drawn from the old god-fearing prayer-books out of which many Christians still today edify themselves before and after the holy Supper. They have, however, grown out of that same faith which those old confessors of our Evangelical-Lutheran Church possessed — though not, to be sure, out of the same fulness, strength, and depth of faith. For in the childlike reverence before the Word of God, in the holy zeal for the wholesome doctrine, in the burning love toward the Lord Jesus, the dear Old Ones will remain, no doubt, our models. Their writings have a ripeness of a life rooted in grace, and a depth of an experience that has penetrated into the Holy of Holies, so peculiar to them that the fragrance of their life will nevermore evaporate."¹²

Also from the time of awakening faith-life in this present century, **August Bomhard** († 1869 as Dean and Church-Councillor in Augsburg; compare *Allgemeine luth. Kirchenzeitung* 1897, No. 10, p. 226) gives to the Old Comforters a testimony which shows how they had, through Rationalism, come into evil repute precisely in the reigning circles, but were, to the one who without prejudice busied himself with them, of great blessing:

"There was a time, I confess it, when I, heeding the common cry of the [rationalist] spokesmen, was almost of the same mind — when I held the maligned word (*Mystic*) for a downright term of abuse, and, when I heard of some unknown person that he was a Mystic, I thought he must be an old star-gazer with small blinking eyes and something uncanny about him, whom I should not care to have for my godfather. To be sure, I was in those days still very young and inexperienced, and had need of no godfather. Little by little, as I learned to use eyes and ears better, doubts came to me. I set myself to asking, but got nowhere any adequate answer; I heard only, and always, talk of *Schwärmerei*,¹³ over-feeling and dark feelings, trifling, confusion of concepts, hangdog piety, superstition, nonsense, and all inordinately dangerous. Soon, to my highest astonishment, I had to hear that men who had, for the sake of their spirit, their soul-enlightened Christian understanding, their purity of morals and faithfulness to their calling, become uncommonly dear and honourable to me, were being labelled as Mystics and lovingly pitied. I set about the study of the Mystics. — They told me: *Arndt* is a Mystic. Then I read Arndt, and I found him deep as the abysses of the sea, rich as the gold-mines and diamond-caves of the New World, blooming as the lovely May, lovely as a rose of Sharon, pious as a right Christian. Then I thought: shame upon the undiscerning, who would dishonour us one of the noblest ornaments of German God-learning! Would that they might rather desire to learn something of him! — They told me: *Scriver* is a Mystic. Then I read Scriver, and I found a preacher such as I had not met among the more recent ones — light-filled as the Alpine peaks at the rising of the sun in their splendour, sharp as the sword of Gideon, sweet as honey and the honeycomb, mild as spring-air and moonlight, fruitful as a garden blessed by God, Christian as an apostle. — And many famous preachers of this present time appeared to me, beside the Old Ones, as the thorn-bush beside the cedar of God, or as the poor village-chapel beside the Strasbourg Minster. Then I thought: Get you to school, ye boys, and learn something of the Master, before ye will speak among the men! — They told me: *Schmolck* is a Mystic. Then I read Schmolck, and I found a pray-er such as I know no more among the people of this present race — clothed with power from on high, illumined with the light of grace that shines about the throne of God, anointed with the Holy Ghost — words that press through the clouds and cease not until they arrive, and cease not until the Most High look down upon them. And my whole heart felt itself refreshed, strengthened, uplifted, freshened by a breath from the dwellings of peace. Then I blessed with a thankful tear the memory of this righteous man, and thought: Holy pray-er, pray above for enlightenment for us, and for forgiveness for the folly and for the pride of this race — for it is very great!"¹⁴

Let us finally hear **Frank.**¹⁵ He says in his *System der christlichen Sittlichkeit* (I, 293 f.) on the worth of devotional literature in general: "Through the whole of Christendom — wherever it lives in its faith on the Redeemer, though it be in great weakness and under much error — there pour forth streams and brooklets of the divine Spirit, mediated by the living witness in word and writing. And when we look upon our evangelical Church in particular — [we find] a literature of edification which, even where it is much scorned by the learned, has yet, for the shaping of the piety and inward life of the people, done its silent and lasting work."¹⁶

## §4. Their Value for the Understanding of the Church, and their Standing in Literary History

Yet there is also to be considered the value of the *Alten Tröster* for **literary history in particular.** Beck writes upon this in his introduction to the *religious folk-literature*: "Already as folk-literature it will not be without significance in cultural-historical regard. If literature in general appears as a faithful impression of the life of a people according to its inward conditions and strivings, then this popular literature will be well suited to hand over to us a mirror of the folk-soul according to its religious conditions and strivings. And what nourishment does the spiritual life of a people find in that literary corpus at all! What means of formation does the German people possess of old in its *House- and Hymn-Books*, its *Prayer-Books*, in the *Old Comforters*! Next to Holy Scripture in the German rendering, it is this literature through which the folk cultivates its inner life, through which it forms its speech! What a rich, in many ways still-unraised (because unknown) treasure of vocabulary does this spiritual folk-literature bear within itself; what a yield does it offer to the philologist for the German dictionary!"¹⁷

For the years of candidacy and for the first years of office — to underline the point once more — these books are especially fitted. Where the student has not the time to enter into them, the candidate ought to devote himself to them with real zeal. They will make a good bridge for the passage from academic study into practical office.

With that we come to the other side. The Old Comforters have their high worth for the servant of the Word **for the sake of the congregation, for the sake of the Church at large.** There it is above all the pastoral experience over the hearts of others, and the practical knowledge that these books foster, which comes to the fore. The pastor must know these writings if only for the reason that they are still present in his congregation. He must know how, and how far, he can recommend them. He must be able to judge the writings out of which precisely the best of his parishioners edify themselves — indeed, the writings against which they often measure him and his preaching. He must know them, that he may know to whom he ought to recommend this or that particular work — whether it is fitted more for youth or for age, for the healthy or for the sick, for the merely-stirred or for the deeper-fostered Christian. Often it becomes clear from familiarity with the devotional books that the pastor finds in this or that parishioner precisely a one-sided emphasis on some particular truth in doctrine or in life. To one who has drawn his devotion only from Zschokke or the like, [the pastor knows what will meet the need]; and again, the woman who prays herself back into her old anxiety by using only a single [prayer], he must point to yet other prayers and meditations in the books. She should for once pray *this* prayer — she should busy herself with the other glorious things which stand in the books. And how fair a thing it is when the pastor, out of the *Paradiesgärtlein* of Arndt, or the *Handbuch* of Starck, which he finds at his house-visit or sick-bed-visit, is able forthwith to find the right, fitting prayer, which he then either prays himself, or recommends to the parishioner to pray.

Yet the books can furnish occasion for edifying conversations of quite another kind as well. One finds — sadly — even among the good Christians a monstrous small understanding for the Church, and an unbelievable ignorance of her history. Here the Old Comforters are the handles by which one may reach in, to reawaken **the understanding of the Church, and especially the sense of the glory of the Lutheran Church.** One has occasion to speak of the life, the age, the conflicts of the writer out of which the book grew, and can easily thereby lead into this or that season of the Church's history. Precisely the deeper grasping of what the Church is, both as the *streitende* (militant) and as the *triumphierende* (triumphant), is essentially made easier through occupation with the Old Comforters. Is it not a means of bringing the "I believe a communion of saints" nearer to us — this precious thought: *I draw from this book comfort, or pray in the words with which so many thousands have already done so, since the Holy Spirit first stirred the pray-er's tongue to them*? Then I see, as a disciple of the Lord in the spirit, all the pray-ers who have already so often read and prayed those same prayers, standing with me at the throne of grace, knocking with me at God's door — and I hear how they, just as I, pour out their heart and find the same comfort, the same being-heard. Will the Lord — who heard the anointed pray-er when he first prayed the prayer he left behind for the Christ-believing one — not also grant me the same, upon his prayer that is now become mine? — So, for example, by praying with the words of others, the awareness is deepened of belonging to the *one* invisible Church of all places and all times. — How important is this thought further in connexion with the *triumphierende Kirche*: The pray-ers who first prayed these prayers are already in the fellowship of the perfected righteous. Here on earth I thank God that he sent them to the Church as living witnesses, and I ask him to send yet many more.

## §5. Scope of the Survey and its Chronological Boundaries

For the writings to be treated here, the range of dates yields itself roughly thus. **The literature itself is grasped as belonging to history through to the end of the last [i.e. eighteenth] century** — until, that is, Rationalism stopped up the old wellsprings of the Word and let the old devotional books grow old and become "Old Comforters." From 1800 to 1830 only very few new editions of them were printed. Roughly the year **1850** yields itself as a boundary reaching backward, from which the literature can be counted as newly issued. In some cases, a few books shortly before 1850 — say from 1830 on — are still to be found in print, not out of edition; they will be dealt with in the appropriate place. On the other hand, writings even into the 1880s of our own century are already now to be described as out of edition — for example, Lorenz's *Andachtsbuch aus Luthers Hauspostille* (1887, Max Woywod, Breslau). Such will either not be discussed at all, or expressly noted as out of edition and to be had only from the antiquarian trade. Since the concern here is with a Guide *to the new editions of the older devotional literature*, only the newly-issued books can be given primary treatment; the older, still-extant original editions in the antiquarian trade are, of course, always available in a narrowly-limited number of copies.

Antiquarian editions are only relevant here to the extent that the newly-published editions rest upon them; the concern of this book is with the editions that are still present or being newly-issued.

## §6. What is a *Erbauungsbuch* — the Definition of the Genre

By *Erbauungsbücher* and *Gebetbücher* in the proper, common-usage sense of the word, we understand: all the writings which — although in part they grow out of sermons — set forth the Scripture-truths of doctrine and life, the doctrine of salvation, in a generally graspable form; which treat single questions of the Christian life (say cross, *Anfechtung*, and the like), or the whole of it; which press for repentance and instruction, or in any other way seek to influence the life of sanctification, whether in general or for specific estates and callings. To this belongs, too, whatever nourishes the intellectual and moral side of the soul; and no less do all prayer- and consolation-books nourish the affective side. All prayer-books — be they general summing-up ones, or those for particular situations or seasons — fall under our subject. Only the prayer-literature that serves exclusively liturgical purposes is excluded. Yet church-prayers which — as often — have passed over into the prayer-books composed for private edification, or, conversely, have come over from the prayer-books into the *Agenden*, are duly noticed.

Alongside this description one may set the definition of Beck, our teacher (deceased 1878): "A form of literature adapted to the grasping-power and the understanding of the congregation" — or, more briefly: "a literature which, according to the intellectual side no less than the ethical, has set itself the task of fostering both the congregational life and the individual life of Christians."¹⁸

If we take the concept in its full breadth, then the sermon-literature and its history, together with the hymnody of the Church and its history, would have to be treated here as well. But the latter is so wide, and has been so often and so thoroughly worked over, that we may exclude it. Much as the hymn is a means of edification, yet, through its close bond with divine service, it has its wholly particular standing and its own history and development, which readily set themselves apart. Only in so far as the hymn as *Andachtslied* has found entrance into the prayer- and devotional-books, or as — for example, in Schmolck — the prayer itself appears rhymed, or as (in Hiller's *Liederkästlein*) a hymn-collection has become an essential part of a prayer-book, will hymnody be noticed here as needed. We hold in view, moreover, newly-issued devotional writings; and old hymn-collections of a single author, as such, are (excepting Luther's, Heermann's, Paul Gerhardt's, Gellert's, and a few others') not to be had. The *Gesangbücher* contain always only a compilation of the most various hymns from all periods, and so are not touched by our nearest purpose. It is therefore clear that we have chiefly and only to do with the treasures of edification that are to be found in unrhymed form.

As to the mentioned **sermon-literature,** it belongs together with the Christian folk-literature to this extent — that printed sermon-books, in the *Postillen* and in certain collections of some outstanding preachers, have become undying edification-books of the congregation. These will be treated (though more briefly, to avoid stepping into the related field of the history of preaching).

The **edifying exposition of Scripture,** as a popular presentation of the Scripture-thoughts, falls further under our concept. It will, so far as is possible, be brought here to speech. But here too the treatment will be shorter, in so far as in many cases the edifying exposition passes over into the scholarly, or is bound up with it. When that happens, this literature easily oversteps the grasping-capacity of the congregation, serves only certain scholarly-trained circles, and does not belong here. In the exposition of Scripture of Luther, of Spener, or of the Württemberg pietists of the last century, one often stands, for example, in doubt whether it really belongs to the folk-literature or to the theological.

If, then, the boundary of our concept is to a certain extent indistinct when it comes to **preaching, hymn, and Scripture-exposition,** the same does not hold of the **devotional-books and prayer-books in the proper, common-usage sense of the word.**

## §7. Confessional Scope — Which Church's Writings?

That, in accord with our purpose, only the writings **of the Evangelical-Lutheran Church** are discussed here, is plain out of everything foregoing. Luther and his age make the historical boundary going backward, the end of the last century the boundary going forward. The vanishingly small number of German devotional writings that have come out of the German-Reformed churches or out of the sects need be treated only in so far as they are still today important and fostering for edification, or have taken an essential share in the development of it. The same holds in particular measure of the **German translations of English religious folk-literature,** as well as of **translations or adaptations of medieval Latin or German devotional writings.** These will be duly appraised in an appendix.

## §8. The Title *Alte Tröster* — Its Justification

The title "Die Alten Tröster" is now once more to be justified. It is meant in a transferred sense. For books are also drawn into view that never expressly bore this name, nor perhaps deserved it. So many an old [book…]. — Very fine thoughts on our theme are contained too in the excellent writing of **Oswald Freybe:** *Anleitung zum gesegneten Gebrauch der Erbauungsbücher für lutherische Christen* (Hermannsburg, 1893). An excellent guide, especially into the "Old Comforters."¹⁹

## §9. The Plan of the Book

From these introductory remarks yields itself the following arrangement of the book:

**The main part** contains a history of the Evangelical-Lutheran devotional literature and piety in its outlines from Luther to the age of Rationalism, in single periods answering to the ecclesiastical-historical development. To this presentation, given in overviews, are joined the *Lebensbilder* (life-portraits) of the fathers of the ascetical literature, and to each is added a searching presentation and appraisal of their most important devotional writings, together with an exact description and critique of the new editions of these.

**The First Appendix** takes in a corresponding description of the translations of English (or French) evangelical and medieval-Catholic writings that are still most read in the Lutheran Church, together with a sketch of the prayer-books that draw their prayers out of the *Alten Tröster.*

**The Second Appendix** adds several tabular overviews: the first gives a searching ordering of the literature according to content; the second gives chronological tables for the history of devotional literature.

## Section a) The Time up to 1550

*Central Point: The comfort of the new doctrine, and its power for new life in the world.*

### §1. General Overview

The great Church-reformation of the sixteenth century brought new life into the Christian people. It very soon exercised, on those who let themselves be laid hold of by this movement, a through-and-through renewing effect on the whole way of thinking in moral-religious regard; indeed, indirectly it influenced also those who resisted it. Through the Reformation, the single person, in his standing to God as to the world, was again valued in right measure according to God's Word. Its aim was to give the heart the right comfort over against God, and, by that, to give the man the right standing to the earthly calling in life, in all earthly circles of fellowship. On the one side, therefore, it stressed the justifying faith that calmed the terror-stricken conscience; on the other side, the battle against sin in the world, against the *Anfechtungen* of the devil. Through the Word of God, and through preaching in the mother-tongue, and through the administration of the Sacrament belonging with these, these thoughts conquered hearts for themselves. Naturally, expositions of Scripture were also soon spread. But hand in hand with them went the *Flugschriften* (occasional-tracts) of Luther and his friends, in rhymed and in unrhymed form, in which the new doctrine was always spread out in immediate reference to the practical need.

With that, it emerges that the Reformation became the source of a new devotional literature. This literature is, correspondingly, especially of two kinds. It points, first of all, to the blessing that the preaching of grace in Christ brings, chiefly in outward and inward *Anfechtungen*. Therefore in that age there arose especially **consolation-books** (*Trostbücher*); and therefore Luther and his companions wrote, richly and in all kinds of matters, **letters of consolation**. The Word of God and **prayer** are recommended as the most emphatic means in the battle against Anfechtung. Prayer, according to Luther, is the work of faith towards God. In prayer the man is led over himself into God. Next to preaching, and next to the Word of God, prayer is the highest exercise of the Christian. There is nothing more necessary in Christendom, because we have so much Anfechtung and hindrance. Prayer is, for the Reformers, the pulse of the inner life. It is the Holy Spirit who does nothing else in the Christians than pray without ceasing. Yet prayer has also its being-heard: with prayer the Christians carry through everything they will. God reserves to himself only "measure, aim, manner, place, person." In order to be able rightly to pray, and rightly to plead for comfort, there arise, alongside the *Trostbücher*, the **guides to prayer** (*Anleitungen zum Beten*), the books in which the Lord's Prayer is expounded — and reference is always made anew to the Psalms as the model-prayers of Scripture. Yet intercession is not forgotten. Prayer enters into the service of love for the neighbour. In good works, which come from faith (which is a busy, mighty, active thing), the Christian exercises himself in every calling God has ordained to him. In the prayer of faith, the Christian rises up to God; in the works of love that come out of faith, he goes down at all times. From this arises the other group of devotional writings, that shows itself often enough in Luther's time: **the application of the Haustafel, and of the whole catechism, to life** — writings which lay out for the several estates and callings prayers and Christian-evangelical practice, and rules of conduct accordingly.

The most encompassing look into what was need and what was accomplished in that age is given by the setting-forth of Luther's own significance for the edifying literature. At the same time, Luther is a picture of the evangelical piety — though a picture of the high-point of that piety, one that is to be found only in single men of God. The piety of the people laid hold of by the Gospel reached this height only rarely.

### §2. Luther and his Significance for Devotional Literature — Part One: The Life to 1529

*Source: I b) §2, pp. 19–30. Luther's biographical arc from Eisleben and Erfurt through the Reformer's break, the Wartburg, the return to Wittenberg, the postille and pastoral writings of the 1520s, marriage to Katharina von Bora in 1525, and the climax in the Great and Small Catechism of 1529 — "the crown of all writings from Luther's pen." Große's own account traces the piety (Frömmigkeit) that stands behind and rises within the reformatory works.*

Luther was born, as is well known, at Eisleben on 10 November 1483, of pious parents.²⁰ His father Hans, a miner, had come from Möhra in Thuringia; his mother, Margarethe née Lindemann, was of Eisenach townsfolk. From the parental hearth a strict religious sense was carried over into the youth; on this the further building went on. We may conclude that he was reared and formed in a high reverence for the churchly priesthood. As a student in Erfurt he lived, "as a nimble and youthful fellow," in industry at his work; amid all the joy of his lute he kept himself distant from the coarsenesses of the age. The nurtured anxiety before the heavenly Judge, and the doubt whether one might yet become truly holy, are the inner grounds of his entering the cloister and of his zeal there for the perfect fulfilment of every law and every work of religion. With the most conscientious care he willed to become an honest monk: night-watches, prayers, mortifications — one after another he laid on himself, and did through, again and again. Once for three days he ate no morsel and drank no drop. Yet he did not become as holy as he willed to become, and found not enough to do that he might yet win himself a gracious God. Scripture too he read here, in the Latin tongue. At the university he had come upon it but once, and had read 1 Samuel 3;²¹ he had had the wish to be able to read more of it. Now the wish was fulfilled — but he read it without being able to find its connexion and its centre. The reading of the scholastics Occam and Biel²² influenced his thinking and covered over the truth of Scripture. Peace he did not find; and his doubting, his trembling, his hunted flight grew. To be sure, he prayed without ceasing for help and for grace — but who could assure him of being heard? The invocation of the saints did not let him stand against the terror of the one word: "I, the LORD, thy God, am a jealous God" (Ex. 20:5).²³ Also in confession he found no peace, because absolution was made again and again dependent upon his own merit. So he went sadly about, and the look upon Christ's cross terrified him only.

Known is the way in which a monk, and then Staupitz, pointed him to the forgiveness of sins. That opened Scripture yet further to him. The same Luther who at his priest-ordination in 1507 had grown afraid before the majesty of God at the altar, as university-teacher in Wittenberg in 1508 no longer let go the word: **"The just shall live by his faith"** (Hab. 2:4; Rom. 1:17).²⁴ From then on the Word worked mightily upon his soul. Nevertheless, in 1511, he yet gladly seized the opportunity to travel to the holy city of Rome on the business of his order. He would gladly have made there a general confession, as he had already twice done at Erfurt. As a "mad saint" he ran through all churches and vaults²⁵ and believed everything that was there lied and stinkingly invented. It grieved him at that time that his parents were still living; he would gladly have delivered them out of purgatory with masses and other excellent prayers. But the word of righteousness-by-faith gave him henceforth no rest.

Doctor of Holy Scripture he became in 1512. To the Erfurt Augustinians he made this step known and asked them for their intercession. As such doctor, he was the first who grounded his whole theological teaching upon the Scripture. With the exposition of the Psalms he began, and he took them up again in 1519. His whole life was accompanied by a love for the Psalms. Here he found the best expression for the conflicts he had himself gone through; here the most glorious words of God's friendliness; here also the Saviour, already set forth in the spirit to the believing ones of the Old Covenant. The Psalter remained to Luther always a mirror of his life and a source of admonitions and consolations. After it, he expounded the Epistles to the Romans and to the Galatians, then to the Hebrews and to Titus, up to 1519. The centre of his exposition was **Christ, and faith in God's grace**. Ever clearer became to him the distinction between Law and Gospel, chiefly through the Epistle to the Galatians. Alongside, he was here helped forward by his occupation with Augustine, and from 1516 by his occupation with **Johann Tauler** and with the ***Deutsche Theologie*.**²⁶ The latter he indeed issued in 1516 and again in 1518, and prized it as "the right theology." He expressed that, next to the Bible and Augustine, there was no book from which he had learned more of what God, Christ, man, and all things are. The strange, the un-evangelical, which those writings contain remained at that time hidden from him. He let the writings work upon him, as it were, only from their edifying, Scripture-conformable side, without exercising criticism against them.

For it is plain that the mysticism of those writings stands under the influence of Greek neo-Platonic philosophy, which lets God — as the absolute Being — come to real self-unfolding only in the world and in men. To that extent it is pantheistic. Everything finite appears, over against the Absolute, as nothing. God, who is love, sinks under in the Absolute. Luther we never find within those thought-circles. He knows nothing of the notion that, while he is wholly given-over to God, his own personhood should perish. He knows himself always, in his sturdy piety, as an independent person over against God — the God whose grace he does not find in an empty self-giving-up, but whom in living faith he lays hold of, the God who strengthens him for work and activity in the world. Luther grasps sin, too, more deeply, as bringing about guilt against God — while in the mystics it appears easily only as imperfection, as the weakness of the creature. Also the means of grace the mysticism lets easily fall to one side; Luther, who through the Word has found the grace, remains firm at them. And while the mystics stress more the enduring and the bearing in the following of Christ, he prizes this indeed very highly, but not so that he did not, at the right time, find himself ready for battling and for wrestling. The mysticism sees at the last, in Christ, always only the one who is to *become* in the soul, and then still predominantly a model. To Luther, the significance of the facts of the reconciliation in Christ's living, doing, and dying remains always the centre — and then becomes for him only the well-spring of a new life.

As preacher²⁷ for Simon Heinß aus Brück, Luther began to work edifyingly for the people at the Wittenberg city-church. He preached first on the Ten Commandments, then on the Lord's Prayer, and in the sermons on the pericopes on the faith. Sturdily and plainly, though in part still walking in the ways of medieval learning, he testified — out of innermost life-experience — to the wisdom of the cross. He is not yet wholly evangelical in his grasp of the several works; but already he denies to monkish achievements their particular worth, already he points to the significance of the calling of each man in the world.

In 1517 there appeared, as the first writing composed by himself for the congregation, an edifying exposition of the **Penitential Psalms**, in German after the Vulgate, Jerome's, and Reuchlin's rendering — "not for learned or refinedly-formed spirits, but for my rough Saxons." In 1518, Luther's disciple **Agricola** issued his Lord's-Prayer-sermons, without his knowledge, and in 1519 Luther revised them once more; at the close he set a short dialogue, joining onto the seven petitions, between the heavenly Father and the child drawing near to him.

By then he had become the great Reformer, and stood in the giant conflicts for the pure Word of God. His piety was thereby shaped with might. Whether he had the true faith to lay hold on salvation — this gave him inward trouble; these *Anfechtungen* he himself accounted heavier than the reformatory ones which were manifest to the outer world. There men marked only his confident courage. Fearlessly he stood before Cajetan at Augsburg. "Christ live, Martin die,"²⁸ he said, in the presage that it would not come to a good end for him. Full of joy and full of peace he was after that hearing — although he knew not how long yet he could work unmolested, although he in earnest weighed the thought of going, perhaps, to France. Precisely at that time he issued the above-mentioned exposition of the Lord's Prayer for the simple laity themselves. Shortly before this, he had written in a letter to Lange, dated 6 June [1518]:²⁹ that no cause troubled him for his own salvation more than the newly-embittered conflict. But in the very midst of it he worked still by Word and Writing — by labour on the Epistle to the Galatians, by fresh exposition of the Psalms — giving [rules] for the Christian life. For the "poor, humble, simple" ones there provided, from 1518 to 1520, many small writings: the repeated editions of "the Exposition of the Ten Commandments, with reference to the chief sins, especially serving for confession"; the sermons "on the Contemplation of the Suffering of Christ," "on Prayer and the Procession in Cross-week," "on Usury" — all writings "for the very simple Christians," to give them the Word of Life for particular circumstances. 1520 brought in addition the "Trostschrift *Tessaradekas*," or "the Fourteen Nothelfer" — a consolation-writing for those who are burdened with temporal suffering, occasioned by Spalatin, at the time of the Elector's illness.³⁰ There followed a sermon on the "Preparation for Dying," occasioned by his own death-terrors of the autumn of 1519; it gives comfort in the Anfechtung on account of damnation.

Out of 1520 there are further sermons on "Repentance," "Baptism," "the Supper," "on Good Works," an addition to the "Exposition of the Lord's Prayer," a "Lord's Prayer Exposition for Oneself and Behind Oneself," and one to the "Exposition of the Ten Commandments" — a forerunner of the Catechism: the faith and the Lord's Prayer are added, as the support in which everything that stands in the Scripture and is necessary for a Christian to know is grasped. To this he had leisure and time in the midst of the labour of the great reformatory writings of 1520.

When over the first, "To the Christian Nobility of the German Nation," he did not receive full assent from friends, he stressed²⁸ what is glorious and mighty and has a look of authority. **[Here Große quotes Luther's *Prayer before the Diet of Worms* verbatim; the prayer is set in extenso:]**

> "Ah God, thou my God, do thou stand by me against all the world's reason and wisdom. Thou must do it — it is, in truth, not my cause but thine. For my own person I have here nothing to do, and nothing to settle with these great lords of the world. Gladly, indeed, would I have good, quiet days, and be unentangled. But thine is the cause, LORD — the cause that is righteous and eternal. Stand by me, thou true, thou eternal God; I lean upon no man. It is in vain and empty; everything limps that is fleshly and that tastes after the flesh. O God, O God — hearest thou not, my God? Art thou dead? No, thou canst not die: thou hidest thee only. Hast thou chosen me for this? I ask thee — as, indeed, I certainly know. Well then, so let God rule — I who my life long never thought to be against such great lords. Nor did I take it upon myself. O God, so stand thou by me in the name of thy dear Son Jesus Christ, who shall be my shelter and shield — yea, my strong fortress — through the power and strengthening of thy Holy Spirit. LORD, where art thou? Thou, my God, where art thou? Come, come — I am ready, ready to leave even my life, patient as a little lamb. For righteous is the cause; and so I will not sunder myself from thee eternally. Let this be sealed in thy name. The world must leave me over my conscience unforced; and should my body — which is the work of thy hands — go to shivers therein, the soul is thine and belongeth to thee, and stayeth with thee eternally. Amen. God help me — amen."³¹

Given over to God's will we find him on the Wartburg. Gladly would he have died for his Lord's cause; and now he has, therefore, only the greater hope. He wrote to Cranach on 28 April 1521 from Frankfurt: "I commit you to God. I let myself be shut in and hidden — I know not yet myself where; and although I would rather have suffered death from the tyrants, especially at the hands of the raging Duke George, yet I must not, until his time, despise the counsel of good people. The Jews must one day sing *Io, Io, Io* [on Good Friday, in jubilation]; the Easter-day will also come to us, and then we shall sing *Alleluia*."³²

How he worked for his people in the quiet of his Patmos!³³ Here, again, he had inward *Anfechtungen* — most in particular in the year 1521 during the reading upon the pericopes, from which grew forth the [Wartburg-Postille]. Especially did Luther compose the Epistle-sermon and the Gospel-sermon "for each Sunday." The later editors divided the Postille into a Gospel- and an Epistle-part; in 1527 Stephan Roth, Magister from Zwickau (Docent in Wittenberg from 1528), issued it in full — with some transpositions — though yet without the Epistles. These appear first in the edition of Cruciger, 1543.³⁴

Luther taught now boldly and confidently from the beginning of 1522 — returned from the Wartburg to new reformatory work. Full of fresh strength and fiery spirit, he held for eight days one after the other the famous *Invocavit* sermons on the disorderly doings of the *Schwärmer* in Wittenberg.³⁵ All Luther's labours in the following years aim always at the edification of the Christian people. His strivings for the shape of the evangelical German divine service (with God's Word and prayer), for the rendering of all the books of Scripture (which, gradually, up to their completion in 1534, appeared with the famous prefaces), for the issuing of expositions of certain books (especially 1 Peter and Genesis), and further for the sanctification of the household-estate of the Christians in society and in office, wish nothing else.

Alongside these outstanding known works, still further writings and letters accompany, again and again, to particular edification: in 1522 there appeared anew a short exposition of the Ten Commandments. Now he called it **his** *Betbüchlein.* His chief aim was, therewith, to sweep aside those prayer-books in which "so much misery of the most various sins was recounted, so much un-Christian foolishness in the prayerlets to God and to his saints was driven into the simple ones — and which, nevertheless, with promise of indulgence and with red-lettered title-page, puffed themselves up on high." He wished, instead, to "hold up a simple Christian form and manner, to know the sins and to pray them." Even here he still added the Ave Maria. He joined himself to the customary practice of the folk, but took care that it should be used only as a biblical greeting from Luke 1:28, to God's praise and honour, and not as if by it Christ's Word and Faith should be cursed.

The persecution of the pure Gospel in Holland moved him in 1523 to the consolation-writing "To all the dear Christians in Holland, Brabant and Flanders": he praises God upon the honour of martyrdom. In 1523 he wrote, among other things, a letter of comfort to Herr Bartholomäus von Staremberg on the death of his consort; in 1524, new sermons on merchant-trade and usury.

While the Peasants' War moved him to writings first of admonishing, then of earnestly warning, kind — while he had to strive against Erasmus over the free will — while the strife against Zwingli and all Supper-Schwärmer began — he himself founded (for his own peace) the evangelical model parsonage. Not long before, he had, in his loneliness with many Anfechtungen, sought after intimate, cheering intercourse; he had then borne witness for the still-despised estate of marriage, and had stressed that one must ask God in earnest for a pious spouse, and must not be ashamed of this prayer. Now, upon his prayer, he had found his spouse. For he later wrote to a friend, as counsel — the friend wishing to be married: "Dear fellow, do as I: when I would take my Käthe, I asked the Lord our God in earnest!"

From 13 June 1525 on he now often writes, out of experience, upon the joys and sorrows of the married estate — after twelve years of marriage with these beautiful, but earnest words: "It has, God be praised, gone well with me, for I have a pious, faithful wife, upon whom a man's heart may rely, as Solomon says.³⁶ Ah, dear Lord God — marriage is not a natural, but a divine gift, the sweetest, indeed the chastest life, above all celibacy when it turns out well; when it turns out ill, however, it is [the hell of earth]."³⁷

As a particular writing for devotional literature there is then to be named, in 1526, "Whether men of war can also be in a blessed estate." But the **crown of all writings from Luther's pen** are those which grew out of the Saxon Visitation: the **Great and Small Catechism** of 1529, which was only prepared for by the *Betbüchlein* and kindred writings. The Catechisms will be, through and through, edifying. They contain the child-lore, "so that every Christian should know what is needful — such that whoever knows this not, cannot be counted among the Christians, nor admitted to any Sacrament."³⁸ They were to come first into the hands of the clergy and the teachers, but through them [into every hand].

### §2. Luther and his Significance for Devotional Literature — Part Two: The Life from 1527 to 1546

*Source: I b) §2, pp. 30–38. The years 1527–1546 — the age of the *Ein feste Burg*, the Coburg fortress, the household preacher, the pastoral letters, the *Tischreden*, and the death at Eisleben.*

How especially do the years 1527–1530 show us Luther in his inner life! The strife with Zwingli (out of which in 1528 came the great *Bekenntnis vom Abendmahl*),³⁹ the strife with Henry VIII, with Papists and Sectarians — wherein his easily-flaming spirit went too far in single expressions and images — inwardly troubled him. The plague reigning in 1527 he does not flee. **"Whether one may flee before dying"** he writes,⁴⁰ occasioned by Heß in Breslau; and he remained, as a right pastor, in Wittenberg. Otherwise it stands with his own conflicts: they give him much to bear. In faith, however, he wrestles himself again through; and the prayer and intercession of his friends — whom he industriously calls upon in letters — help him ever again to the victory. In 1526 there had broken out especially his kidney-suffering; in 1527 there followed heavy bodily oppressions and great soul-anguish. On 6 July 1527 he confessed to Bugenhagen and made himself ready to die, took leave of wife and child and asked for intercession, but felt himself soon strengthened. This latter request — and with it the thanks for the same, and for its being heard — runs through many letters of that time.

Up to early 1528 and again at the beginning of 1529 much outward and inward distress burdened him. The sickness passing through Germany in 1529 — the **English sweat** — and likewise the Turkish danger of 1528 and 1529 let him prove himself once more undismayed; the latter drove him to prayer, to which he zealously admonished in word and writing. The hymn **"Ein feste Burg ist unser Gott"** stems well from that time.⁴¹

The year 1530 brought Luther's sojourn on the **Coburg fortress.** As on the Wartburg, so here again Luther lets us look richly into his deep spirit and his strong, zealous prayer-life. To prayer he admonishes the clergy at the Diet of Augsburg, against the Turks and for his own cause; he is one who is sure that everything he asks shall come to pass. In Coburg he wrote also, for his own and others' comfort, "Chosen Scripture-sayings on the cause with which the Christians console themselves in the sufferings that for the sake of the divine Word come upon the believers," further "Short admonition to the bearing of the cross," and "Admonition to the Sacrament of the Body and Blood of our Lord." Despite all this labour and this earnest intercourse with God, he cared also for the innocent recreations of life. Melanchthon he comfortingly urged to playing and singing; and he himself will not have let them alone. He has, for example, exercised himself with Dietrich in crossbow-shooting. Even when at the close of his sojourn earnest thoughts of his life's end trouble him, and he feels himself already, at forty-seven years, as an old man, he yet knows how to return joyful and full of hope to Wittenberg.

There he had further to labour especially on the rendering and exposition of Scripture. Now follow calmer years of his life. His Anfechtungen for a time relatively lessen — even though we hear from 1531–1533 of many kinds of bodily weakness-states. His piety is too firmly grounded that it should lose thereby. Now one may see it in its fairest form take shape in the household circle; and out of small consolation-letters, consolation-writings, and edifying labours it shines forth brightly. His letters give counsel in the most various matters: now he sets forth intercession, now he warns, now he counsels, now he consoles. To his mother he communicates in her sickness: "We must be certain of God in Christ and console ourselves upon his victory," and closes: "There pray for you all my children and my Käthe." To the sick (Kaspar Müller, 1534), to the Anfechtungs-tried, he gives out of his rich experience the counsel that one should give the devil's thoughts of salvation and of God's providence a discharge. To a Herr von Stockhausen, whom suicide-thoughts tormented, he counsels that he not be left alone by his wife, that he should work, be active, cheerful, hear and read new tidings, despise the devil, remember God in Christ and console himself. Widows he consoles at the death of the spouse (1530), a father at the death of the son (1535), wives at the imprisonment of the husband. What he did in letters for others, he showed also to his household. From the spring of 1532 to the end of 1534 he preached as household-father before wife, children, servants, and guests⁴² — an edifying preaching-of-repentance for his own house-church.

His illnesses meanwhile continued. **"God has done wonders on me this night, and does them still, through pious people's intercession,"** he says at Wittenberg after his recovery. A relapse forced him indeed to new death-preparation, but again the way went upward. Still many a conflict was to be gone through, still many a writing was to be written. In 1538 and 1541 it was again necessary to pray against the Turks. In 1539 there appeared a new writing on usury. Consolation-letters and admonition-writings were to be dispatched. So there appeared, in 1542, a consolation-writing "for women to whom it has gone amiss in the pains of childbirth."⁴³ But the longing to die, and death-presentiments, gathered thicker. In 1538 a painful dysentery attacked him for weeks; in 1539 a vertigo. With the expectations of his own end came those of the Last Day. The dark moral conditions, chiefly in Wittenberg, let him long for the dear Last Day; and in view of it he called out, at the 1544 consecration of the castle-church at Torgau, zealously to intercession for the Church.⁴⁴ In 1545 he wished on that account to leave Wittenberg altogether. But however heavily the Anfechtung of the forties again pressed him down, he remained at bottom yet the immovable man of prayer and of faith. Many testimonies to this the *Tischreden* furnish, and particular events in his family and in his circle of friends of that time.

The *Tischreden*⁴⁵ rest upon the notes of **Anton Lauterbach.** After Lauterbach had, from 1529 onward, studied several years in Wittenberg, and there had already been Luther's table-guest, he came in 1537–1539 as Deacon in Wittenberg again to Luther's table, and made in the years 1538 and 1539 continuous notes from the discourses which he heard at Luther's table. With notes of **Veit Dietrich** from the years 1529–1534 and 1540–42, of **Mathesius** from the years 1540–1542, and others, they were later printed and set in order by **Aurifaber**,⁴⁶ and are so up to this day spread in various re-workings. We see through them into Luther's happy family life, into his joy at his Käthe, as at his children. Yet he stresses once, that this against Anfechtungen does not alone help — but that the trust upon the merit of Christ carries out everything. We look further into his sense, opened for friendship, for nature, for music, for noble sociability, and for right humour. Yet we experience again that only his firm faith in his Saviour, and his fervent intercourse with him, brought him to this healthy standing. Therefore he remains also — in the midst of anxiety — one who, in trust upon Christ, knows how to save himself; and the devil-spectre (which to him and to his imagination was actually sensibly perceptible)⁴⁷ has, for him, lost its terror. The other matter is, that according to biblical view it is warranted to assume that Luther, as a particular weapon of God, was especially attacked with inward and outward Anfechtungen by the devil and his host. To him, therefore, the *lords and mighty ones who rule in the darkness of this world, the evil spirits under the heavens* (Eph. 6:12), stepped forth undoubtedly for outward perception more visibly and remarkably than they do to other believers — the "smaller spirits," if I may so speak, in the kingdom of God.

Some few important examples for the manner and might of Luther's believing prayer-life may close this sketch.

**See and hear him in 1541 at Weimar, at Melanchthon's sickbed!** Melanchthon lies there, the eyes already broken, consciousness, speech, hearing seemed vanished. His face was sunken. Luther grew afraid beyond measure, and cried out: "God forbid, how has the devil disgraced me this instrument!" Then he turned himself at once to the window, and prayed. Of this he later said: "There must our Lord God stand by me; for I threw the sack before his door and rubbed his ears with all his promises, that he would hear prayer — those which I knew how to reckon up out of Holy Scripture — that he *must* hear me, if I were otherwise to trust in those promises." Then he grasped Melanchthon by the hand and bade him be of good cheer, for he would not die; although God had cause to slay him, he willed not the sinner's death, but rather that the same should turn and live; Melanchthon must not give room to the mourning-spirit and become a self-murderer, but must trust in the Lord, who can kill and make alive again, wound and again heal. Whereupon Melanchthon began again to draw breath, though he could yet for a long time not speak, then looked Luther straight upon, and asked that he not be held back but let go from hence, since he was on a good journey and nothing better could yet befall him. But Luther replied: "By no means. Thou must serve our Lord God yet further." Then he had something quickly brought for him to eat, and threatened him, when he would not take it: "Listen thou, Philip: shortly, thou *must* eat with me, or I put thee under the ban." Melanchthon recovered. Luther wrote on it to Lange at Erfurt on 2 July: **"We found Melanchthon dead; through a manifest miracle of God, he lives."**⁴⁸

No wonder, that after such experiences, in 1542 Joachim asked for intercession from Luther and Melanchthon for the Turkish war. He received their assent.

An experience of another kind had the pious pray-er upon the death of his little **Magdalena** on 20 September 1542.⁴⁹ When it was sure to him that God would take her to himself, and she lay in the last extremity, he fell down at her bed on his knees, wept bitterly, and asked God to release her. Then she passed away and fell asleep in his hands. To his wife he spoke comfort: "I have sent a saint into heaven. O had I such a death, such a death would I take upon me this hour."

So indeed was it to come. The more of his old friends who went on before him, the more had he desire to depart. The hour came for him, as is well known, at **Eisleben,**⁵⁰ whither he journeyed at the end of January 1546. From there he writes to his wife, when she is anxious about him: "Pray, and let God care!" and communicates, when his mediation between the Mansfeld lords seemed to be turning out well: "So must one grasp, that God is the hearer of prayer." To a Hohensteinian *Rentmeister* he wrote a word he was fond of using — into a *Hauspostille*, John 8:51: "Whosoever keepeth my word shall never see death." In his chamber he did, as usual, at the window earnestly his evening-prayer, and then turned cheerfully for a short conversation with Jonas, Rudtfeld, a servant, and his two sons, and fell peacefully asleep.

On 16 February he spoke at table much of sickness and of dying. On 17 February he prayed often before noon at the window; in the evening after earnest conversations on the seeing-again in the world to come, likewise; and now he passed the last night of his life — as is known — with much sighing and prayer. After 10 o'clock he entered the sleeping-chamber with the words: **"God rule; I go to bed. Into thy hands I commend my spirit; thou hast redeemed me, thou true God."** When he had lain down, he gave the hand for good-night and said: "Dr. Jonas and M. Cölius and you others: pray for our Lord God and for his Gospel, that it may go well with him; for the Council of Trent and the wretched Pope are wroth with him hard." After 1 o'clock, when he had become awake again, he said to Jonas: "Ah, Lord God, how sore am I; ah, dear Dr. Jonas, I reckon I shall stay here at Eisleben, where I was born and baptised." Walking about in the room he repeated: "Into thy hands I commend my spirit." When the town-clerk, his wife, the Counts Albrecht and von Schwarzburg with their consorts had come, and sought to strengthen him, he said: "Dear God, I am so sore and anxious; I journey forth." Directly upon this, already in the sweat of death, he prayed:

> **"O my heavenly Father, God and Father of our Lord Jesus Christ, thou God of all comfort — I thank thee that thou hast revealed to me thy dear Son Jesus Christ, on whom I believe, whom I have preached and confessed, whom I have loved and praised, whom the wretched Pope and all godless ones dishonour, persecute and slander. I pray thee, my Lord Jesus Christ, let my little soul be commended to thee. O heavenly Father, though I must lay down this body, yet I know most surely that I abide with thee eternally, and out of thy hands can none pluck me."**⁵¹

Then he prayed in Latin three times John 3:16 and Psalm 68:21: "We have a God who helps, and the Lord, the Lord, who saves from death." When Cölius offered him medicine, he cried: "I journey forth; my spirit will I give up." And finally he added, three times quickly one after the other: **"Father, into thy hands I commend my spirit; thou hast redeemed me, thou true God."** Then he became still, and said only one more audible *Ja* on the loud question: "*Reverende pater*, will you abide constant on Christ and the doctrine which you have preached?"

So died the great man of faith praying, as he had prayed while he lived.

### §2. Luther and his Significance for Devotional Literature — Part Three: The Writings-Catalogue

*Let us now give an overview of the newly-issued devotional writings of Luther then in print.* To this some remarks are to be sent ahead. Luther's prayers and edifying selections from his writings were, already in his own age and through all the centuries thereafter, gathered and worked over. So something also of the age of the particular editors will need again to be named. In the description of Luther's writings, completeness has not been striven for. What his sermons concern: there are here editions of the more scholarly, and the more edifying, kinds; only in so far as they are meant to serve the congregation directly are they mentioned here. His Scripture-exposition, further, so uniquely binds together the theological-scholarly and the practically-edifying side, that the question presses which [face of it belongs in the *Wegweiser* and which not].⁵²

## A. Luther's Own Devotional Writings, by Category

### The *Betbüchlein* and the Ways to Right Prayer

The *Betbüchlein* of 1522 and its enlargements have already been named above.⁵³ Luther enlarged the *Exposition of the Ten Commandments* by "an Exposition of the Holy Lord's Prayer, forward and backward" and by the further exposition of the Ave Maria. He set out his own method of prayer in the beautiful writing "**How one shall pray, for Master Peter the Barber**" (1534, pp. 126 ff.).⁵⁴ Here he gives the immediately-useful counsels for a right prayer-life: how out of the Ten Commandments one may — through meditation (*Betrachtung*) — arise, and how out of the petitions and words of Scripture and of the faith one may pass on to the prayer, in that one makes of each of these one thing — a doctrine, a thanksgiving, a confession, a prayer.

Also of high worth stands beside these the significant Trostschrift **"Tessaradekas Consolatoria — a Little Book of Fourteen Comforting Means for the Weary and Heavy-Laden"** of the year 1520.⁵⁵ Instead of the "Fourteen Nothelfer" (the fourteen intercessory saints then popularly invoked), Luther will set before the sick person a great painting in two panels, each with seven pictures. On the first panel he shows the evils:

1. the evil in us (sin and hell);
2. the future evil, or the evil before us (death);
3. the evil of the past, or the evil behind us (which points to God's mercy);
4. the evil from below, or the evil under us (hell);
5. the evil at our left, or the evil to our left hand (enemies);
6. the right evil, or the evil to our right hand (the evils of our friends);
7. the evil on high, or the evil above us (which points to Christ the Crucified).

On the second panel he shows the goods, under the like seven headings.⁵⁶

Of consolation-writings there is further to be found in Vol. VI [of the received editions]:

- p. 61 — **"A Sermon on Preparation for Dying"** — to set the holy Sacrament of the Altar, and Christ himself, over against the Anfechtung.⁵⁷
- p. 81 — **"Whether one may flee before dying"** (1527).
- p. 107 — **"Fair chosen sayings of the Holy Scripture, wherewith Luther has comforted himself in great Anfechtungen"** (1530).⁵⁸
- p. 115 — **"Consolation-writing for a person in high Anfechtungen."**

### 5. Wahres Christentum nach Luther — after Statius

There follows in Große's account an edition that gathers from Luther's whole corpus a two-book anthology on *Wahres Christentum* — "true Christianity" — carried out by **Martin Statius,**⁶⁶ the seventeenth-century Brandenburg pastor (of whom Große treats more fully in his own biographical portrait below, in the post-Reformation section). Statius writes in his own preface that he undertook the "rather heavy labour" to gather out of all Luther's works — which he industriously read through from beginning to end — the material on *Wahres Christentum,* so that the false-Christian teachings might be overthrown and the Lutheran Christianity fostered; and that the enemies of the truth, who slander Dr. Luther as founder and beginner of an evil life among the Lutherans, might have their mouths stopped, to God's honour and to many men's benefit.

The first book handles, in fifteen chapters with many subsections, faith (dead, historical, [saving]; origin, properties of the same, etc.). The second book handles, in sixteen chapters, love (or good works) — for example, how faith and works are close of kin; that in the article on justification, faith and love are to be distinguished; which works Luther refuses and which he does not, and the like. The four antique copperplates are portraits of Luther, Melanchthon, Statius, and of the Diet of Augsburg. Whoever will come to know thoroughly what Luther, out of Scripture, has clearly recognised and expressed on faith and works, is to reach for this materially fine-ordered collection.⁶⁷

### 6. Trostschriften — The Consolation-Writings of Luther

### 9. Gebete, Gebetbücher, Andachtssammlungen — Prayer-books after Luther

As to prayers, prayer-books, and prayer-and-devotion collections after Luther, the following books are to be named.

**2. Das Leiden und Sterben unsers HErrn JEsu Christi** — 42 contemplations for household devotion for the several days of Passion-tide, put together out of Luther's Passion-sermons. Issued by the *Christlicher Verein im nördlichen Deutschland,* Eisleben, 1854. IV + 197 pp, 8°, half-cloth 60 pf, cloth 90 pf. — To the contemplations hymns are added, that suit the old grained Luther-language, drawn therefore out of the hymn-treasury of the 16th century up to Joh. Heermann († 1647). To each day comes a contemplation of 4–5 pp.

**[Further collections] — Continuation:** The Perthes-Delius selection (already treated above); Schinmeyer's *Schatzkästlein* — Sayings out of Luther's writings with fuller explanation, put together for the whole year through. **Johann Christoph Schinmeyer** at his own time gave out this *Schatzkästlein,* occasioned by 2 Cor. 3:16,⁷³ after in the Pietist age many had been printed which explained the sayings with hymns; he willed to set his contemporaries upon God's Word and Luther's teaching. He praises "the sound, emphatic, and full-mouthed explanation of Luther" as especially edifying. He himself indicates as his purpose: to show Luther as the *comforter of troubled and faint-hearted consciences* — how Luther there mightily set forth the doctrine of Justification out of Grace, but at the same time pressed for sanctification and good works. "The design of this little booklet is further to show how far the doctrine and the life of our present Lutherans deviates from Luther's doctrine and life, and further, that these testimonies of Luther may serve for the edification, comfort, and grounding of faith in the house, on journeys, in sermons and in letters." This purpose of Schinmeyer is not missed even today, but makes the book useful, for every friend of God's Word and Luther's teaching, as a short collection thereupon.

Schinmeyer at first gave out the explanation of 200 sayings. Little by little the editions were enlarged; so 1769 to 500. On this rest the most recent editions.

That printed at Wilh. Baur is the same as is distributed by A. Schmidt, Anklam (now Altona), with the closest possible tying-in with the [feast-days of the church-year].⁷⁴

Structured as fourteen consolations, evenly divided into seven inward and seven outward consolations, as a Lutheran replacement for the medieval cult of the "Fourteen Holy Helpers" (*vierzehn Nothelfer*) — the intercessory saints whom the pious of the age invoked in illness. Large parts of it are in the WA *Tischreden* (esp. TR 5, no. 5342). Große quotes a devotional-tradition text-form (probably drawing from the collection issued by K. F. Ledderhose or a nineteenth-century Erbauungs-book); a modern-critical text is difficult to fix because of the oral-tradition transmission. Preserved here in extenso in the English rendering as one of the *Trostschriften* of Luther's own life.

Duke George of Saxony (the *raging* one) was the Albertine-Saxon prince who remained Roman Catholic throughout his life. The wordplay *Io, Io, Io* / *Alleluia* is on the Jewish antiphon-form of the Passion-week liturgy.

The **Small Catechism** and the **Large Catechism** (both 1529) grew out of the Saxon visitations of 1528–1529, in which Luther and Melanchthon observed first-hand the theological illiteracy of the ordinary parish and set themselves to write the two catechisms as the theological floor of Lutheran life. In Große's placement, they are "the crown of all writings from Luther's pen" — a judgement inherited from the nineteenth-century confessional-Lutheran revival, for whom the catechisms stood at the centre of Lutheran identity together with the *Augustana* and the *Formula of Concord*.

## From Tranche 2 · The Reformation Century — Luther (Part Two)

³⁹ **Vom Abendmahl Christi. Bekenntnis** (1528) — the *Große Bekenntnis vom Abendmahl* — Luther's final and most extensive Christological-eucharistic statement against the Zwinglian and Reformed rejection of the Real Presence. Text at WA 26:261–509; ET at LW 37:151–372. The confession's closing pages became a source-text of the *Formula of Concord*'s Christological articles.

⁴⁰ **Ob man vor dem Sterben fliehen möge** (1527) — Luther's tract to Johann Heß (Silesian reformer, pastor at St. Maria Magdalena in Breslau) on whether Christians and their pastors may flee the plague. Text at WA 23:323–386; ET at LW 43:113–138 as "Whether One May Flee from a Deadly Plague." One of the foundational writings of Lutheran pastoral casuistry on the pastor's duty of remaining with the flock.

⁴¹ ***Ein feste Burg ist unser Gott*** — Luther's *chorale of the Reformation*, composed some time between 1527 and 1529 (dating disputed; earliest extant printing 1529 in the Klug hymnal). Text at WA 35:455–457. Melodic paraphrase of Ps. 46. Große's placement in the plague-and-Turk period of 1528–29 follows the older biographical tradition; modern scholarship allows the earlier composition-date of 1527 as equally possible.

⁴² Luther's household-preaching (*Hauspostille*) from 1532–1534 was written down by his amanuensis **Veit Dietrich** and issued after Luther's death by Dietrich in 1544 (Nürnberg), and again by Rörer in the Wittenberg Edition. The parallel *Kirchenpostille* is the sermons-on-the-pericopes catalogue. Text at WA 52 (Dietrich-recension); WA 21–22 (Rörer/Cruciger *Kirchenpostille*).

⁴³ *Trostschrift für Frauen, denen es unrichtig in Kindesnöten gegangen* — Luther's brief tract of 1542 on the pastoral care of women whose deliveries have miscarried or ended in stillbirth. Text at WA 53:205–208 ("Ein Trost den Weibern, welchen es ungerade gegangen ist mit Kindergebären"). One of the earliest Protestant treatments of the pastoral question of the *massa perditionis* verdict on the unbaptised infant.

⁴⁴ **Consecration sermon at the Torgau castle-church, 5 October 1544** — Luther's dedicatory sermon for the new-built palace chapel at Schloss Hartenfels, the first Lutheran church consecrated as such (rather than a Roman-Catholic building re-appropriated). Text at WA 49:588–615.

⁴⁵ **Tischreden** (Table-Talk) — the collected transcriptions of Luther's conversations at his own dinner table with students and friends, made across the 1530s and early 1540s. Not "written" by Luther, but by his hearers. Text at WA TR 1–6. The Aurifaber edition of 1566 became the received nineteenth-century standard; the WA edition rests on the direct hearer-manuscripts.

⁴⁶ **Anton Lauterbach** (1502–1569), Wittenberg-trained pastor and deacon; kept the *Colloquia* records of 1538 in Wittenberg (WA TR 3, no. 3465 onwards). **Veit Dietrich** (1506–1549), Luther's *famulus* and later pastor at Nürnberg-St. Sebald, kept records 1529–1534. **Johann Mathesius** (1504–1565), Luther's student and later pastor at Joachimsthal (author of the 1566 *Historien Von... Doct. Martini Luthers*), kept records 1540–1542. **Johann Aurifaber** (Goldschmidt, 1519–1575), Luther's amanuensis in the last years, first editor of the collected *Tischreden* (Eisleben, 1566).

⁴⁷ Große's parenthesis records the mid-nineteenth-century confessional-Lutheran refusal to allegorise Luther's demonology — that is, the refusal to explain Luther's inkpot-throwing episode at the Wartburg (and the many Tischreden references to Satan) as merely psychological. Große takes Luther's Anfechtungen at their sixteenth-century word. The modern historiography (from Karl Holl onward) has taken a more differentiating view: Luther's Anfechtungen are read theologically as *Deus-absconditus*-encounters, not psychologised away, but also not reduced to a simple demonology.

⁴⁸ **Weimar, June–July 1541 — Melanchthon's near-death and Luther's prayer of intercession.** The episode is preserved in Melanchthon's own account, Mathesius's *Historien* (13th sermon), and Rörer's transcript of Luther's report at table (WA TR 5, no. 5565); Luther's letter to Wenzeslaus Linck (Große: "Lange"; but the standard addressee is again Linck) is at WA Br 9, no. 3627, dated 2 July 1541. Large's rendering of the "sack before his door" metaphor is preserved literally in the English; it is Luther's own idiom of wrestling with God in prayer.

⁴⁹ **Magdalena Luther** (1529 – 20 September 1542), Luther's twelve-year-old daughter, whose death Luther chronicles in a heart-tearing letter to Justus Jonas (WA Br 10, no. 3794). The account of Luther kneeling at the bedside is Luther's own report; the phrase *ich habe einen Heiligen in den Himmel geschickt* passes into all subsequent Lutheran hagiography as a mark of the reformed doctrine of the communion of saints against the Roman-Catholic cult.

⁵⁰ **The Eisleben journey and death, January–February 1546** — Luther travelled to Eisleben at the end of January 1546 to mediate between the sons of Count Albrecht IV of Mansfeld over the family mining and inheritance disputes. He preached four sermons there (WA 51:187–194), completed the mediation on 17 February, and died the following morning, 18 February 1546. The deathbed narrative and the last confession-formula ("Reverende pater...") are recorded in the eye-witness accounts of Justus Jonas, Michael Coelius, and Ambrosius Rudtfeld, and printed in the *Historia von des ehrwürdigen Herrn D. Martini Lutheri seligem Abschied* (Wittenberg, 1546). Text of the accounts at WA 54:487–496.

⁵¹ Luther's dying confession — a free-form prayer in Luther's own voice, transcribed from his lips by Justus Jonas or Coelius on the night of 17–18 February 1546. Preserved in the death-day *Historia* of 1546; text at WA 54:487–496; standard modern ET in LW 55 (letters) and in Roland Bainton's *Here I Stand* (New York, 1950), ch. 22. Große quotes it here as one of the founding Trostschriften of Lutheran devotional tradition: it becomes, in the following two centuries of Lutheran devotional-writing, the model of the *evangelischer Sterbegebet* — the evangelical prayer at the point of death.

A charter-document of the Lutheran devotional method: work outward from the Ten Commandments, the Creed, and the Lord's Prayer, treating each item under the four heads of *doctrine, thanksgiving, confession, prayer.* Große cites page-count 126 ff. of an edition not further specified — probably a nineteenth-century Erbauungs-collection.

Große here gives the two-panel iconographic schema by which Luther replaces the medieval Fourteen Nothelfer cult with a scriptural anthropology of the seven evils and seven goods. The schema was intended as an actual painted diptych for the sick-room, though how many such were produced is unknown; the text at WA 6:99–134 preserves the schema in prose.

The reader is not to invoke the saints against distress, but to consider the *Gnadenschau* — the vision of the graces given in Christ, categorised by their coming-to-us.

The counter-move to the medieval *ars moriendi* tradition (Gerson, etc.): the dying man is to look not at his sins but at the Sacrament, at Baptism, and at Christ.
