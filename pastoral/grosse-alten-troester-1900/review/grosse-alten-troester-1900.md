# Translator's Note

*This working translation is at its kick-off state. The paragraphs below record the intended text base, the editorial conventions binding on the eventual body prose, and — with full disclosure — what remains to be verified once the 1900 imprint has been sighted paragraph by paragraph.*

## 1. The text base

The English rendered here follows the **1900 Hermannsburg** edition of Constantin Große's *Die alten Tröster: Ein Wegweiser in die Erbauungslitteratur der evangelisch-lutherischen Kirche des 16. bis 18. Jahrhunderts*, published in commission with the Hermannsburger Missionshandlung. This is Große's own first — and to the best of the bibliographic record only — edition of the work; there is no expanded second edition or posthumous recension, and the text-base question is therefore trivial.

The working text-base is the fully-digitised 1900 imprint held by the **Internet Archive** under identifier `diealtentroeste00grosgoog`, a scan derived from the Google Books digitisation of a copy held in a partner-library collection. The scan comprises vi + [2] + 700 pages + one folding leaf of chronology-table, and is accompanied by a Tesseract 5.0.0 optical-character-recognition text layer, run in German-Fraktur mode and reported by the Internet Archive at a script-detection confidence of 0.9596 — meaningfully higher than the OCR quality typical of seventeenth-century Fraktur imprints, and correspondingly less demanding of paragraph-by-paragraph correction, though the workflow discipline of eye-verifying every paragraph against the facsimile image remains in force.

The bibliographic authority record is **GND 101703465** (Deutsche Nationalbibliothek); the specialist bibliography Enzyklothek indexes the work under entry `4cc90c2c-442a-4744-9b3f-1427b4428440`. VD17 does not apply (the register covers 1601–1700); VD18 does not apply (1701–1800); the 1900 imprint falls outside both registers.

## 2. What the author himself supplies

Große's own hand supplies the entirety of the volume — the front matter, the introductory account of what *Erbauungslitteratur* is and by what criterion he selects the writers he surveys, the biographical-and-bibliographical entries on the individual writers he treats, the folding-table chronology of the whole tradition, and the register that closes the volume. The book is not an anthology and it is not a collection of primary sources; it is Große's own scholarship, from cover to cover.

His entries on individual devotional writers do quote — often at length — from the writers' own works, and those quotations preserve the writers' own citations to scripture, the church fathers, and earlier Lutheran devotional authors. All of these are preserved unchanged in the translation. Modern-critical equivalents (WA / LW for Luther, CR / MSA for Melanchthon, BSLK / *Kolb-Wengert* for confessional documents, PL / PG for the church fathers, ESV numbering for scripture alongside Große's own citation form on first occurrence per major section) are consigned to the endnotes.

One authorial fact deserves early acknowledgement: Große writes from the **Hermannsburg mission-house** in 1900, and the volume is issued by the Missionshandlung — the publishing arm of Louis Harms's Hermannsburg-Mission. Große's stance is that of a late-nineteenth-century confessional-Lutheran churchman, aligned with the missional-confessional Lutheran project of his own century and sympathetic to the American Missouri-Synod trajectory. His evaluations of individual devotional writers — who among them remains theologically sound, who is already tending toward pietism, who veers into mysticism — are theologically located, not neutral. The translation preserves his evaluations as his own; the translator's voice does not adjudicate on his adjudications.

## 3. What the Scholastic Lutherans have added — and where

*Nothing in the body text is our own.* All editorial additions are relegated to the numbered endnotes at the end of the volume. These endnotes supply, and only supply:

- Standard modern citations for the primary Lutheran works Große discusses (WA / LW for Luther; CR / MSA for Melanchthon; standard modern-critical editions and, where they exist, cross-references to the *Scholastic Lutherans* translation program's own working translations of the same writers)
- Standard modern citations for the church fathers and confessional documents Große cites (PL / PG / CCSL / SC volume:column; BSLK / *Kolb-Wengert*; ESV numbering for scripture with Große's own citation form retained on first occurrence per section)
- Biographical dates on first named mention of each Lutheran devotional writer, in the standard scholarly convention (e.g. **Philipp Nicolai** [1556–1608])
- Modern place-names and modern spellings for the toponyms and personal names Große spells in his 1900 Fraktur convention (Große spells *Chytraeus* as *Chyträus*, and so on — his spelling on first occurrence in italic; the modern editorial form thereafter in the English body)
- Brief historical-theological notes where the *modern* historiographical judgement on a writer differs from Große's late-nineteenth-century confessional-Lutheran assessment, and where preserving Große's judgement in the body without a note would risk misrepresenting the state of subsequent scholarship — always flagged as an editorial addition, never inserted into the body prose
- Cross-references to the *Scholastic Lutherans* corpus proper: where Große treats a writer whose primary work is itself a translation target of this program (e.g. Musculus in Dogmatics, Balduin in Pastoral, Selnecker in Dogmatics, Chytraeus in Catechetical), the endnote directs the reader to the corpus volume — making explicit the function of Große's *Wegweiser* as a reference companion to the whole program

Body prose contains no modern scholarship, no editorial voice, no bracketed insertions. Everything is either Große or Große's cited authorities.

## 4. Editorial conventions

- **Register.** Große's register is measured late-nineteenth-century scholarly German — expository, biographical, evaluative, missional-pastoral in cadence, not archaising and not partisan-polemical. The English mirrors that register: scholarly and clear, reverent where the subject-matter is reverence, evaluative where Große evaluates. This is *not* the "reverent-but-not-archaising" register of the primary-source volumes in the *Scholastic Lutherans* program (Chytraeus, Flacius); it is the register appropriate to a Wegweiser — a survey, a guide, a reference work.
- **Latin technical terms** — *pietas*, *Erbauung*, *Andacht*, *Trost*, *Betrachtung*, *Übung*, *Anfechtung* — are preserved on first occurrence in each major section in italic, glossed briefly, and rendered in English thereafter, unless the term is itself the subject under discussion, in which case the German or Latin form is retained.
- **Greek** appears where Große quotes patristic sources; preserved in polytonic script, with parenthetical transliteration and English gloss on first substantive occurrence.
- **Hebrew** appears rarely; when it does, it is preserved in pointed Hebrew script with transliteration and gloss on first occurrence.
- **Scripture.** ESV for the English rendering. Große's own citation form (Luther-Bible numeration, occasionally Vulgate) is noted parenthetically on first citation per section.
- **Fraktur normalisation.** Long-*s* (ſ) is silently modernised to round *s*. Umlauts and *ß* are preserved in German source citations (not in the English body). Große's own 1900-convention spellings of 16th–17th-century proper names (*Chyträus* for *Chytraeus*, *Aegidius Hunnius* variants) are preserved in italic on first occurrence; the standard modern editorial spelling is used in the English body thereafter.
- **Named writers.** For each Lutheran devotional writer to whom Große devotes a substantial entry, life-dates are supplied in the endnote on first named mention — never in the body. Where the writer is the subject of a *Scholastic Lutherans* corpus volume, the endnote cross-references the corpus.
- **Confessional restraint.** The translator's voice does not adjudicate on Große's confessional evaluations. His judgements — sympathetic to some writers, sharp with others, drawing the pietism-boundary at specific places — are preserved as his own. Modern historiographical revisions live in endnotes flagged as editorial additions.
- **Rubrication, typeface, layout** — locked via `shared/reference-custom.docx` and identical to the Praetorius trilogy, the completed Chytraeus volume, and the in-flight Flacius volume.

## 5. Scope disclosure

The 1900 Hermannsburg imprint comprises six pages of Roman-numeraled front matter, two unnumbered pages, seven hundred pages of body, and one folding leaf of chronology-table. Its internal arrangement — chronological by century, or thematic by devotional-genre (prayer-books, hymn-collections, meditations, funeral-consolations, catechetical exposition), or biographical-portrait-by-author — is the first structural question the translation must resolve against Große's own sighted table of contents at the opening of Tranche 1. The provisional tranche plan in the working notes assumes chronological-by-century as the most probable arrangement, with re-cutting expected once the table of contents is sighted.

At this working-translation state, the scaffolding is complete: the text base is fixed, the editorial conventions are set, the source has been sighted at the level of catalogue-and-facsimile-availability, and the initial tranche plan is drawn. Body prose accrues tranche by tranche.

Two corrections to the parallel-work coordination memo are recorded transparently in the working notes and are surfaced here for the reader's benefit: the author is **Constantin Große** (not Alexander Grosse — a different person, an English Puritan divine of a different century), and the year of the imprint is **1900** (not 1614 — the coordination memo appears to have taken the earliest date of the *subject-matter* for the year of the *work itself*). The genre-shift consequent on these corrections is from a putative patristic-consolation anthology to a Lutheran-confessional scholarly guide-to-the-tradition. The volume's function inside the *Scholastic Lutherans* corpus is correspondingly recast: not a primary source in the pastoral bucket, but a reference companion to the whole corpus, mapping the terrain the program's other volumes translate piece by piece.

## Vorwort

*The Author's Foreword. Signed simply* Der Verfasser *(the author) at the close.*

In Number 45 of the *Sächsisches Kirchen- und Schulblatt* for 1896, on the occasion of an inquiry concerning Schmolck's *Kommunionbüchlein*, the wish was expressed for a "Register of the Prayer-Literature of Our Old Ones."² In Numbers 47 and 49 there followed a reply from Herr Deacon Pastor König of Hirschfelde, giving some particulars on the printing of various Schmolckian books. He closed his reply with these words: "It would surely be very much blessed if the wished-for *Register of the Prayer-Literature of Our Old Ones* were once put together and made public, in order to reawaken the love for them, which stands in danger of dying out. I should gladly lend my own hand to it, were some time only given me, and could I count on the help of brethren experienced in this field."

These expressions accorded with what had been my favourite study from my university years onward. Since I had been in the pastoral office, the thought had from time to time occupied me: whether one might not, with a writing on the old devotional literature of our Church joined with the outlines of its history, round off a subject worthy of nearer treatment — one with which one could serve the brethren in office, and interested laymen with them, both for the care of souls and for their own edification. Then I came upon the remarks quoted above in the *Kirchen- und Schulblatt*. They met with my own thoughts. In a letter I communicated as much to Herr Deacon Pastor König, and he encouraged me to carry my intentions out.

To be sure, a bare register of the older devotional literature has, in my judgement, too small a purpose. Publishers' Christmas-catalogues and the like already serve that end: they all contain, under the headings "Edifying Works," "Prayer-Books," "Prayer- and Devotional-Books," and other such rubrics, tolerably rich compilations, out of which the old devotional literature can be found with ease. If more than the bare register was to be given, then the task was to compose a searching description of the literature according to form and content, physical get-up, and so forth. Whether a book is to be recommended or not, in what measure it is to be recommended, and to whom — the discussion of such questions led of itself onto the history of the book's coming-to-be, the history the book itself has had since, the history of its author, and the piety of the age from which it stems.

So the register widened, under my hand, into a *Guide to the Devotional Literature of the Evangelical-Lutheran Church of the Sixteenth through Eighteenth Centuries*, so far as that literature is again or still accessible in particular through new editions in our own time, in connexion with a searching description of the literature, a portrayal of its history in outline, and of the lives of its authors. In that same connexion a modest attempt was made to give something — by way of examples — of the development of Christian-churchly piety, chiefly of the life of prayer in the Lutheran Church. For that is the soil out of which these writings grew; that is the soil to which they in turn gave nourishment.

Now that I put the book into the hands of the public, I wish only that it may be a welcome help for preaching and for the care of souls to the brethren in the office — to whom the author, in giving it out, has thought first of all — and that it may also serve Christian laymen (and especially the Christian book-trade) in the buying and selling of devotional literature.

To all who have lent the author a hand in his labour, my thanks are here finally set down. Particular thanks are due to the many book-dealers who supported him most kindly with catalogues, inspection-copies, and the like, indeed in some measure with gratis copies, and made possible for him the sighting of the older editions of the literature in question.

May the book work with its little part to show forth the glory of the Lutheran Church and to awaken love toward her! May the Lord grant that many by its means may find the way into the Old Comforters themselves, unto richly-blessed edification!

*The Author.*

## Foreword

*By D. Fr. Hashagen, Rostock. Placed by the author immediately after his own Vorwort and before the Einleitung.*

With hearty joy I greeted and read this work, which offers an introduction to devotional literature — an introduction resting on thorough studies, industriously wrought, warm with life — and, above all, into those churchly-ascetical writings that are of most worth for doctrine and for life; and in doing so it meets a need plainly present. — When a proverb of the East declares that the fruit-bearing palm must have its foot in the water and its head in the fire, then in the case of the "Old Comforters," those palms of God, both conditions are met in unusual measure. They stem for the greater part — as the present work again and repeatedly shows from the sources — from times of the heaviest shakings of public and personal life, and from times of great inward *Anfechtung*. When the earth quaked and the mountains sank into the midst of the sea, those men of God felt the earthquake also in their own hearts, and knew that the creature — every firmest thing on which one had until then relied — no longer stood fast. Then the living God, the God of all patience and of all comfort, became in his eternal Word and Gospel their sole refuge. As surely as they could say, "This is my misery," so surely, by God's grace, could they also bear witness, "This is my comfort; for thy Word quickeneth me" (Ps. 119:50).³ In them is fulfilled: "Those that be planted in the house of the LORD shall flourish in the courts of our God: they shall still bring forth fruit in old age; they shall be fat and flourishing" (Ps. 92:13–14).⁴ — May the present work, so aptly fitted for it, contribute powerfully to this: that the "Old Comforters" find in the study and in the parsonage, as also in the congregations everywhere, ever more open doors, and that their quiet blessed labour may be carried forward in ever richer measure!

*Rostock, on the Feast of the Reformation, 1899.*

*D. Fr. Hashagen.*

# Einleitung — Introduction

## §1. Who the "Old Comforters" Are

The "Old Comforters" — so this book calls itself. Upon them and upon their blessing it will fix the reader's attention. Who, then, are the "Old Comforters"? The name is used for the prayer- and devotional-books of the believing fathers of our Evangelical-Lutheran Church, which still today comfort the faithful; for those devotional writings out of which not only the author himself and his contemporaries sought and found comfort, but which have such worth that many generations, up to the present day, have been and still are edified by them. *Old Comforters* they were called at first often in mockery, but afterwards, held in high honour, unto their high honour — chiefly for the sake of the comfort they granted in the second half of the last century and the first half of the present one to the pious and the quiet in the land,⁵ everywhere, in the midst of an age of dry, empty rationalist faith and of the dreary enlightenment-wisdom of the pulpit. The children of God, who received in the Church stones for bread,⁶ had in these books, next after Scripture, good bread within their houses. Often they set them — wrongly, to be sure — beside, indeed above the Scripture, because the Comforters were easier for them to understand, and in many cases first made the understanding of God's Word possible. In the troubled seasons of the Church they became, among the means through which the old faith was carried over into the new time, no small part — so that the old faith, when it arose again in the *Erweckungszeit*, could catch hold at their hand.⁷ In the believing evangelical circles they are found everywhere. The personal Christian life, which is founded in baptism and preserved by the immediate Word of God in the Bible (and in preaching, and by the holy Supper), is prepared for, carried forward, established, deepened by the mediated Word of God, which in the devotional literature flows richly, pure and clear, out of the pure fount of Scripture. In right Christian houses the Old Comforters travel from generation to generation, from the forefathers down to the grandchildren. Each generation ought to be aware of the spirit that reigned in the family, and to see to it that the old is preserved — or one lets it lapse and vanish. This latter spirit is, alas, the reigning one in our own time. On the one hand, in single houses, [the old books are still passed down, are still received], are replaced by new [editions] when worn. But on the other hand — and there, alas, are the most — [the old books] are no longer used. The old *Erbauungsbuch* was there, but it lay dust-covered on the shelf or in the chest, and wandered at last into the fire or into the rag-heap. But the purchase of a new devotional book of the same content is not made, because one, entangled in this world, thinks himself risen above edification, pretends to have no time for edification, indeed has in fact no desire for it.

Here it is needful that the Christian people be once again reminded of these writings. Both they who prize them and they who despise them must be brought to know that these books, for a good part, are still to be had; that they have not outlived their day; that indeed they cannot outlive their day, in so far as they hold within them the wrought-through Word of God. To that end it is above all needful that **the pastors and teachers of the congregations be brought again into a proper acquaintance with them,** in order to be able to recommend them anew. That they should come rightly to know these Comforters, and to prize them, is of particular importance both for their own sake and for the sake of their congregations.

## §2. Their Value for the Pastor Himself and for the Congregation

They themselves must go deep into them. They will draw from them a rich gain for their inward life. What entrance do these books afford into the conflicts of the heart, into the mysteries of the Word of God and of the pure doctrine, into a true Christian life! The students and candidates especially, who at the universities, over against the demands of scholarship and criticism, or over against the demands of student and social life in fraternities and clubs, so easily neglect the inward life — such find here nourishment for the recovering of their own inner life, fruit ripe and springs fresh. The gifted preacher himself, no less than the one of lesser abilities, can here find blessing, progress, riches, strengthening, comfort, and cheer. There should be no clergyman at all who is not known and well versed in these our rich gardens; and it should long have been provided that those wishing to enter them should have direction and counsel. Would that many younger clergymen might take from what has been said the needful admonition and encouragement, and in their still-youthful years begin a holy, sweet, meditative study — one to which the quiet hours of a whole life might be devoted, without finding either want or weariness in it.⁸

Delitzsch says in his preface to Cober's *Passions- und Osterprediger* (1865, Frankfurt, Brönner, now out of print): "Whoever will become an able preacher and pastor does well to set these old orthodox teachers before his eyes; and whoever will come to know the order of grace of God and the wonderful, strange, but blessed ways along which the Lord recovers the human soul out of ruin and sets it ever more deeply into fellowship with himself — such a one will not err if he betakes himself to the school of these experience-rich servants of God."⁹

Consider also Delitzsch in the new edition of the *Christentumsfragen* of Ahasverus Fritsch, Dresden 1841 (now out of print): "The Church of God cannot advance without taking up into herself what has been won by the earlier Church already, and has been passed on to us as a pound to be traded with. For God grants not all things to any one age. The history of the Church is the history of grace received little by little, unto ever new insights, experiences, and victories. Accordingly, the Church rising up in our time may surpass the Church of the past — but only in the case that she makes over unto herself the good old, and receives a good new out of the hand of the Lord. Therefore must she go first backward, if she will go forward; she has to remember the earlier times up to this hour, and to consider what he has done unto the old Fathers. Ah, that I could with a loud-clanging voice cry into the hearts of Christians the exhortation: Read, read our dear Old Ones, and use them as guide-posts in the Scripture and as counsellors in the affairs of your souls! Let not the plain, unadorned, all so-called geniality-stripped speech in which they speak be strange to you — it is the depth of a clear stream, full of heavenly shapes!"¹⁰

## §3. Delitzsch on the Old Ones as Standards

Likewise, in the preface to the first edition (1844) of his classic *Communion-Book*¹¹ — a book written in the spirit of the Old Ones — Delitzsch sets the Old Ones before us as scarcely-attainable models:

"The confession- and communion-prayers which the following pages contain are not, as it might seem, drawn from the old god-fearing prayer-books out of which many Christians still today edify themselves before and after the holy Supper. They have, however, grown out of that same faith which those old confessors of our Evangelical-Lutheran Church possessed — though not, to be sure, out of the same fulness, strength, and depth of faith. For in the childlike reverence before the Word of God, in the holy zeal for the wholesome doctrine, in the burning love toward the Lord Jesus, the dear Old Ones will remain, no doubt, our models. Their writings have a ripeness of a life rooted in grace, and a depth of an experience that has penetrated into the Holy of Holies, so peculiar to them that the fragrance of their life will nevermore evaporate."¹²

Also from the time of awakening faith-life in this present century, **August Bomhard** († 1869 as Dean and Church-Councillor in Augsburg; compare *Allgemeine luth. Kirchenzeitung* 1897, No. 10, p. 226) gives to the Old Comforters a testimony which shows how they had, through Rationalism, come into evil repute precisely in the reigning circles, but were, to the one who without prejudice busied himself with them, of great blessing:

"There was a time, I confess it, when I, heeding the common cry of the [rationalist] spokesmen, was almost of the same mind — when I held the maligned word (*Mystic*) for a downright term of abuse, and, when I heard of some unknown person that he was a Mystic, I thought he must be an old star-gazer with small blinking eyes and something uncanny about him, whom I should not care to have for my godfather. To be sure, I was in those days still very young and inexperienced, and had need of no godfather. Little by little, as I learned to use eyes and ears better, doubts came to me. I set myself to asking, but got nowhere any adequate answer; I heard only, and always, talk of *Schwärmerei*,¹³ over-feeling and dark feelings, trifling, confusion of concepts, hangdog piety, superstition, nonsense, and all inordinately dangerous. Soon, to my highest astonishment, I had to hear that men who had, for the sake of their spirit, their soul-enlightened Christian understanding, their purity of morals and faithfulness to their calling, become uncommonly dear and honourable to me, were being labelled as Mystics and lovingly pitied. I set about the study of the Mystics. — They told me: *Arndt* is a Mystic. Then I read Arndt, and I found him deep as the abysses of the sea, rich as the gold-mines and diamond-caves of the New World, blooming as the lovely May, lovely as a rose of Sharon, pious as a right Christian. Then I thought: shame upon the undiscerning, who would dishonour us one of the noblest ornaments of German God-learning! Would that they might rather desire to learn something of him! — They told me: *Scriver* is a Mystic. Then I read Scriver, and I found a preacher such as I had not met among the more recent ones — light-filled as the Alpine peaks at the rising of the sun in their splendour, sharp as the sword of Gideon, sweet as honey and the honeycomb, mild as spring-air and moonlight, fruitful as a garden blessed by God, Christian as an apostle. — And many famous preachers of this present time appeared to me, beside the Old Ones, as the thorn-bush beside the cedar of God, or as the poor village-chapel beside the Strasbourg Minster. Then I thought: Get you to school, ye boys, and learn something of the Master, before ye will speak among the men! — They told me: *Schmolck* is a Mystic. Then I read Schmolck, and I found a pray-er such as I know no more among the people of this present race — clothed with power from on high, illumined with the light of grace that shines about the throne of God, anointed with the Holy Ghost — words that press through the clouds and cease not until they arrive, and cease not until the Most High look down upon them. And my whole heart felt itself refreshed, strengthened, uplifted, freshened by a breath from the dwellings of peace. Then I blessed with a thankful tear the memory of this righteous man, and thought: Holy pray-er, pray above for enlightenment for us, and for forgiveness for the folly and for the pride of this race — for it is very great!"¹⁴

Let us finally hear **Frank.**¹⁵ He says in his *System der christlichen Sittlichkeit* (I, 293 f.) on the worth of devotional literature in general: "Through the whole of Christendom — wherever it lives in its faith on the Redeemer, though it be in great weakness and under much error — there pour forth streams and brooklets of the divine Spirit, mediated by the living witness in word and writing. And when we look upon our evangelical Church in particular — [we find] a literature of edification which, even where it is much scorned by the learned, has yet, for the shaping of the piety and inward life of the people, done its silent and lasting work."¹⁶

## §4. Their Value for the Understanding of the Church, and their Standing in Literary History

Yet there is also to be considered the value of the *Alten Tröster* for **literary history in particular.** Beck writes upon this in his introduction to the *religious folk-literature*: "Already as folk-literature it will not be without significance in cultural-historical regard. If literature in general appears as a faithful impression of the life of a people according to its inward conditions and strivings, then this popular literature will be well suited to hand over to us a mirror of the folk-soul according to its religious conditions and strivings. And what nourishment does the spiritual life of a people find in that literary corpus at all! What means of formation does the German people possess of old in its *House- and Hymn-Books*, its *Prayer-Books*, in the *Old Comforters*! Next to Holy Scripture in the German rendering, it is this literature through which the folk cultivates its inner life, through which it forms its speech! What a rich, in many ways still-unraised (because unknown) treasure of vocabulary does this spiritual folk-literature bear within itself; what a yield does it offer to the philologist for the German dictionary!"¹⁷

For the years of candidacy and for the first years of office — to underline the point once more — these books are especially fitted. Where the student has not the time to enter into them, the candidate ought to devote himself to them with real zeal. They will make a good bridge for the passage from academic study into practical office.

With that we come to the other side. The Old Comforters have their high worth for the servant of the Word **for the sake of the congregation, for the sake of the Church at large.** There it is above all the pastoral experience over the hearts of others, and the practical knowledge that these books foster, which comes to the fore. The pastor must know these writings if only for the reason that they are still present in his congregation. He must know how, and how far, he can recommend them. He must be able to judge the writings out of which precisely the best of his parishioners edify themselves — indeed, the writings against which they often measure him and his preaching. He must know them, that he may know to whom he ought to recommend this or that particular work — whether it is fitted more for youth or for age, for the healthy or for the sick, for the merely-stirred or for the deeper-fostered Christian. Often it becomes clear from familiarity with the devotional books that the pastor finds in this or that parishioner precisely a one-sided emphasis on some particular truth in doctrine or in life. To one who has drawn his devotion only from Zschokke or the like, [the pastor knows what will meet the need]; and again, the woman who prays herself back into her old anxiety by using only a single [prayer], he must point to yet other prayers and meditations in the books. She should for once pray *this* prayer — she should busy herself with the other glorious things which stand in the books. And how fair a thing it is when the pastor, out of the *Paradiesgärtlein* of Arndt, or the *Handbuch* of Starck, which he finds at his house-visit or sick-bed-visit, is able forthwith to find the right, fitting prayer, which he then either prays himself, or recommends to the parishioner to pray.

Yet the books can furnish occasion for edifying conversations of quite another kind as well. One finds — sadly — even among the good Christians a monstrous small understanding for the Church, and an unbelievable ignorance of her history. Here the Old Comforters are the handles by which one may reach in, to reawaken **the understanding of the Church, and especially the sense of the glory of the Lutheran Church.** One has occasion to speak of the life, the age, the conflicts of the writer out of which the book grew, and can easily thereby lead into this or that season of the Church's history. Precisely the deeper grasping of what the Church is, both as the *streitende* (militant) and as the *triumphierende* (triumphant), is essentially made easier through occupation with the Old Comforters. Is it not a means of bringing the "I believe a communion of saints" nearer to us — this precious thought: *I draw from this book comfort, or pray in the words with which so many thousands have already done so, since the Holy Spirit first stirred the pray-er's tongue to them*? Then I see, as a disciple of the Lord in the spirit, all the pray-ers who have already so often read and prayed those same prayers, standing with me at the throne of grace, knocking with me at God's door — and I hear how they, just as I, pour out their heart and find the same comfort, the same being-heard. Will the Lord — who heard the anointed pray-er when he first prayed the prayer he left behind for the Christ-believing one — not also grant me the same, upon his prayer that is now become mine? — So, for example, by praying with the words of others, the awareness is deepened of belonging to the *one* invisible Church of all places and all times. — How important is this thought further in connexion with the *triumphierende Kirche*: The pray-ers who first prayed these prayers are already in the fellowship of the perfected righteous. Here on earth I thank God that he sent them to the Church as living witnesses, and I ask him to send yet many more.

## §5. Scope of the Survey and its Chronological Boundaries

For the writings to be treated here, the range of dates yields itself roughly thus. **The literature itself is grasped as belonging to history through to the end of the last [i.e. eighteenth] century** — until, that is, Rationalism stopped up the old wellsprings of the Word and let the old devotional books grow old and become "Old Comforters." From 1800 to 1830 only very few new editions of them were printed. Roughly the year **1850** yields itself as a boundary reaching backward, from which the literature can be counted as newly issued. In some cases, a few books shortly before 1850 — say from 1830 on — are still to be found in print, not out of edition; they will be dealt with in the appropriate place. On the other hand, writings even into the 1880s of our own century are already now to be described as out of edition — for example, Lorenz's *Andachtsbuch aus Luthers Hauspostille* (1887, Max Woywod, Breslau). Such will either not be discussed at all, or expressly noted as out of edition and to be had only from the antiquarian trade. Since the concern here is with a Guide *to the new editions of the older devotional literature*, only the newly-issued books can be given primary treatment; the older, still-extant original editions in the antiquarian trade are, of course, always available in a narrowly-limited number of copies.

Antiquarian editions are only relevant here to the extent that the newly-published editions rest upon them; the concern of this book is with the editions that are still present or being newly-issued.

## §6. What is a *Erbauungsbuch* — the Definition of the Genre

By *Erbauungsbücher* and *Gebetbücher* in the proper, common-usage sense of the word, we understand: all the writings which — although in part they grow out of sermons — set forth the Scripture-truths of doctrine and life, the doctrine of salvation, in a generally graspable form; which treat single questions of the Christian life (say cross, *Anfechtung*, and the like), or the whole of it; which press for repentance and instruction, or in any other way seek to influence the life of sanctification, whether in general or for specific estates and callings. To this belongs, too, whatever nourishes the intellectual and moral side of the soul; and no less do all prayer- and consolation-books nourish the affective side. All prayer-books — be they general summing-up ones, or those for particular situations or seasons — fall under our subject. Only the prayer-literature that serves exclusively liturgical purposes is excluded. Yet church-prayers which — as often — have passed over into the prayer-books composed for private edification, or, conversely, have come over from the prayer-books into the *Agenden*, are duly noticed.

Alongside this description one may set the definition of Beck, our teacher (deceased 1878): "A form of literature adapted to the grasping-power and the understanding of the congregation" — or, more briefly: "a literature which, according to the intellectual side no less than the ethical, has set itself the task of fostering both the congregational life and the individual life of Christians."¹⁸

If we take the concept in its full breadth, then the sermon-literature and its history, together with the hymnody of the Church and its history, would have to be treated here as well. But the latter is so wide, and has been so often and so thoroughly worked over, that we may exclude it. Much as the hymn is a means of edification, yet, through its close bond with divine service, it has its wholly particular standing and its own history and development, which readily set themselves apart. Only in so far as the hymn as *Andachtslied* has found entrance into the prayer- and devotional-books, or as — for example, in Schmolck — the prayer itself appears rhymed, or as (in Hiller's *Liederkästlein*) a hymn-collection has become an essential part of a prayer-book, will hymnody be noticed here as needed. We hold in view, moreover, newly-issued devotional writings; and old hymn-collections of a single author, as such, are (excepting Luther's, Heermann's, Paul Gerhardt's, Gellert's, and a few others') not to be had. The *Gesangbücher* contain always only a compilation of the most various hymns from all periods, and so are not touched by our nearest purpose. It is therefore clear that we have chiefly and only to do with the treasures of edification that are to be found in unrhymed form.

As to the mentioned **sermon-literature,** it belongs together with the Christian folk-literature to this extent — that printed sermon-books, in the *Postillen* and in certain collections of some outstanding preachers, have become undying edification-books of the congregation. These will be treated (though more briefly, to avoid stepping into the related field of the history of preaching).

The **edifying exposition of Scripture,** as a popular presentation of the Scripture-thoughts, falls further under our concept. It will, so far as is possible, be brought here to speech. But here too the treatment will be shorter, in so far as in many cases the edifying exposition passes over into the scholarly, or is bound up with it. When that happens, this literature easily oversteps the grasping-capacity of the congregation, serves only certain scholarly-trained circles, and does not belong here. In the exposition of Scripture of Luther, of Spener, or of the Württemberg pietists of the last century, one often stands, for example, in doubt whether it really belongs to the folk-literature or to the theological.

If, then, the boundary of our concept is to a certain extent indistinct when it comes to **preaching, hymn, and Scripture-exposition,** the same does not hold of the **devotional-books and prayer-books in the proper, common-usage sense of the word.**

## §7. Confessional Scope — Which Church's Writings?

That, in accord with our purpose, only the writings **of the Evangelical-Lutheran Church** are discussed here, is plain out of everything foregoing. Luther and his age make the historical boundary going backward, the end of the last century the boundary going forward. The vanishingly small number of German devotional writings that have come out of the German-Reformed churches or out of the sects need be treated only in so far as they are still today important and fostering for edification, or have taken an essential share in the development of it. The same holds in particular measure of the **German translations of English religious folk-literature,** as well as of **translations or adaptations of medieval Latin or German devotional writings.** These will be duly appraised in an appendix.

## §8. The Title *Alte Tröster* — Its Justification

The title "Die Alten Tröster" is now once more to be justified. It is meant in a transferred sense. For books are also drawn into view that never expressly bore this name, nor perhaps deserved it. So many an old [book…]. — Very fine thoughts on our theme are contained too in the excellent writing of **Oswald Freybe:** *Anleitung zum gesegneten Gebrauch der Erbauungsbücher für lutherische Christen* (Hermannsburg, 1893). An excellent guide, especially into the "Old Comforters."¹⁹

## §9. The Plan of the Book

From these introductory remarks yields itself the following arrangement of the book:

**The main part** contains a history of the Evangelical-Lutheran devotional literature and piety in its outlines from Luther to the age of Rationalism, in single periods answering to the ecclesiastical-historical development. To this presentation, given in overviews, are joined the *Lebensbilder* (life-portraits) of the fathers of the ascetical literature, and to each is added a searching presentation and appraisal of their most important devotional writings, together with an exact description and critique of the new editions of these.

**The First Appendix** takes in a corresponding description of the translations of English (or French) evangelical and medieval-Catholic writings that are still most read in the Lutheran Church, together with a sketch of the prayer-books that draw their prayers out of the *Alten Tröster.*

**The Second Appendix** adds several tabular overviews: the first gives a searching ordering of the literature according to content; the second gives chronological tables for the history of devotional literature.

## Section a) The Time up to 1550

*Central Point: The comfort of the new doctrine, and its power for new life in the world.*

### §1. General Overview

The great Church-reformation of the sixteenth century brought new life into the Christian people. It very soon exercised, on those who let themselves be laid hold of by this movement, a through-and-through renewing effect on the whole way of thinking in moral-religious regard; indeed, indirectly it influenced also those who resisted it. Through the Reformation, the single person, in his standing to God as to the world, was again valued in right measure according to God's Word. Its aim was to give the heart the right comfort over against God, and, by that, to give the man the right standing to the earthly calling in life, in all earthly circles of fellowship. On the one side, therefore, it stressed the justifying faith that calmed the terror-stricken conscience; on the other side, the battle against sin in the world, against the *Anfechtungen* of the devil. Through the Word of God, and through preaching in the mother-tongue, and through the administration of the Sacrament belonging with these, these thoughts conquered hearts for themselves. Naturally, expositions of Scripture were also soon spread. But hand in hand with them went the *Flugschriften* (occasional-tracts) of Luther and his friends, in rhymed and in unrhymed form, in which the new doctrine was always spread out in immediate reference to the practical need.

With that, it emerges that the Reformation became the source of a new devotional literature. This literature is, correspondingly, especially of two kinds. It points, first of all, to the blessing that the preaching of grace in Christ brings, chiefly in outward and inward *Anfechtungen*. Therefore in that age there arose especially **consolation-books** (*Trostbücher*); and therefore Luther and his companions wrote, richly and in all kinds of matters, **letters of consolation**. The Word of God and **prayer** are recommended as the most emphatic means in the battle against Anfechtung. Prayer, according to Luther, is the work of faith towards God. In prayer the man is led over himself into God. Next to preaching, and next to the Word of God, prayer is the highest exercise of the Christian. There is nothing more necessary in Christendom, because we have so much Anfechtung and hindrance. Prayer is, for the Reformers, the pulse of the inner life. It is the Holy Spirit who does nothing else in the Christians than pray without ceasing. Yet prayer has also its being-heard: with prayer the Christians carry through everything they will. God reserves to himself only "measure, aim, manner, place, person." In order to be able rightly to pray, and rightly to plead for comfort, there arise, alongside the *Trostbücher*, the **guides to prayer** (*Anleitungen zum Beten*), the books in which the Lord's Prayer is expounded — and reference is always made anew to the Psalms as the model-prayers of Scripture. Yet intercession is not forgotten. Prayer enters into the service of love for the neighbour. In good works, which come from faith (which is a busy, mighty, active thing), the Christian exercises himself in every calling God has ordained to him. In the prayer of faith, the Christian rises up to God; in the works of love that come out of faith, he goes down at all times. From this arises the other group of devotional writings, that shows itself often enough in Luther's time: **the application of the Haustafel, and of the whole catechism, to life** — writings which lay out for the several estates and callings prayers and Christian-evangelical practice, and rules of conduct accordingly.

The most encompassing look into what was need and what was accomplished in that age is given by the setting-forth of Luther's own significance for the edifying literature. At the same time, Luther is a picture of the evangelical piety — though a picture of the high-point of that piety, one that is to be found only in single men of God. The piety of the people laid hold of by the Gospel reached this height only rarely.

### §2. Luther and his Significance for Devotional Literature — Part One: The Life to 1529

*Source: I b) §2, pp. 19–30. Luther's biographical arc from Eisleben and Erfurt through the Reformer's break, the Wartburg, the return to Wittenberg, the postille and pastoral writings of the 1520s, marriage to Katharina von Bora in 1525, and the climax in the Great and Small Catechism of 1529 — "the crown of all writings from Luther's pen." Große's own account traces the piety (Frömmigkeit) that stands behind and rises within the reformatory works.*

Luther was born, as is well known, at Eisleben on 10 November 1483, of pious parents.²⁰ His father Hans, a miner, had come from Möhra in Thuringia; his mother, Margarethe née Lindemann, was of Eisenach townsfolk. From the parental hearth a strict religious sense was carried over into the youth; on this the further building went on. We may conclude that he was reared and formed in a high reverence for the churchly priesthood. As a student in Erfurt he lived, "as a nimble and youthful fellow," in industry at his work; amid all the joy of his lute he kept himself distant from the coarsenesses of the age. The nurtured anxiety before the heavenly Judge, and the doubt whether one might yet become truly holy, are the inner grounds of his entering the cloister and of his zeal there for the perfect fulfilment of every law and every work of religion. With the most conscientious care he willed to become an honest monk: night-watches, prayers, mortifications — one after another he laid on himself, and did through, again and again. Once for three days he ate no morsel and drank no drop. Yet he did not become as holy as he willed to become, and found not enough to do that he might yet win himself a gracious God. Scripture too he read here, in the Latin tongue. At the university he had come upon it but once, and had read 1 Samuel 3;²¹ he had had the wish to be able to read more of it. Now the wish was fulfilled — but he read it without being able to find its connexion and its centre. The reading of the scholastics Occam and Biel²² influenced his thinking and covered over the truth of Scripture. Peace he did not find; and his doubting, his trembling, his hunted flight grew. To be sure, he prayed without ceasing for help and for grace — but who could assure him of being heard? The invocation of the saints did not let him stand against the terror of the one word: "I, the LORD, thy God, am a jealous God" (Ex. 20:5).²³ Also in confession he found no peace, because absolution was made again and again dependent upon his own merit. So he went sadly about, and the look upon Christ's cross terrified him only.

Known is the way in which a monk, and then Staupitz, pointed him to the forgiveness of sins. That opened Scripture yet further to him. The same Luther who at his priest-ordination in 1507 had grown afraid before the majesty of God at the altar, as university-teacher in Wittenberg in 1508 no longer let go the word: **"The just shall live by his faith"** (Hab. 2:4; Rom. 1:17).²⁴ From then on the Word worked mightily upon his soul. Nevertheless, in 1511, he yet gladly seized the opportunity to travel to the holy city of Rome on the business of his order. He would gladly have made there a general confession, as he had already twice done at Erfurt. As a "mad saint" he ran through all churches and vaults²⁵ and believed everything that was there lied and stinkingly invented. It grieved him at that time that his parents were still living; he would gladly have delivered them out of purgatory with masses and other excellent prayers. But the word of righteousness-by-faith gave him henceforth no rest.

Doctor of Holy Scripture he became in 1512. To the Erfurt Augustinians he made this step known and asked them for their intercession. As such doctor, he was the first who grounded his whole theological teaching upon the Scripture. With the exposition of the Psalms he began, and he took them up again in 1519. His whole life was accompanied by a love for the Psalms. Here he found the best expression for the conflicts he had himself gone through; here the most glorious words of God's friendliness; here also the Saviour, already set forth in the spirit to the believing ones of the Old Covenant. The Psalter remained to Luther always a mirror of his life and a source of admonitions and consolations. After it, he expounded the Epistles to the Romans and to the Galatians, then to the Hebrews and to Titus, up to 1519. The centre of his exposition was **Christ, and faith in God's grace**. Ever clearer became to him the distinction between Law and Gospel, chiefly through the Epistle to the Galatians. Alongside, he was here helped forward by his occupation with Augustine, and from 1516 by his occupation with **Johann Tauler** and with the ***Deutsche Theologie*.**²⁶ The latter he indeed issued in 1516 and again in 1518, and prized it as "the right theology." He expressed that, next to the Bible and Augustine, there was no book from which he had learned more of what God, Christ, man, and all things are. The strange, the un-evangelical, which those writings contain remained at that time hidden from him. He let the writings work upon him, as it were, only from their edifying, Scripture-conformable side, without exercising criticism against them.

For it is plain that the mysticism of those writings stands under the influence of Greek neo-Platonic philosophy, which lets God — as the absolute Being — come to real self-unfolding only in the world and in men. To that extent it is pantheistic. Everything finite appears, over against the Absolute, as nothing. God, who is love, sinks under in the Absolute. Luther we never find within those thought-circles. He knows nothing of the notion that, while he is wholly given-over to God, his own personhood should perish. He knows himself always, in his sturdy piety, as an independent person over against God — the God whose grace he does not find in an empty self-giving-up, but whom in living faith he lays hold of, the God who strengthens him for work and activity in the world. Luther grasps sin, too, more deeply, as bringing about guilt against God — while in the mystics it appears easily only as imperfection, as the weakness of the creature. Also the means of grace the mysticism lets easily fall to one side; Luther, who through the Word has found the grace, remains firm at them. And while the mystics stress more the enduring and the bearing in the following of Christ, he prizes this indeed very highly, but not so that he did not, at the right time, find himself ready for battling and for wrestling. The mysticism sees at the last, in Christ, always only the one who is to *become* in the soul, and then still predominantly a model. To Luther, the significance of the facts of the reconciliation in Christ's living, doing, and dying remains always the centre — and then becomes for him only the well-spring of a new life.

As preacher²⁷ for Simon Heinß aus Brück, Luther began to work edifyingly for the people at the Wittenberg city-church. He preached first on the Ten Commandments, then on the Lord's Prayer, and in the sermons on the pericopes on the faith. Sturdily and plainly, though in part still walking in the ways of medieval learning, he testified — out of innermost life-experience — to the wisdom of the cross. He is not yet wholly evangelical in his grasp of the several works; but already he denies to monkish achievements their particular worth, already he points to the significance of the calling of each man in the world.

In 1517 there appeared, as the first writing composed by himself for the congregation, an edifying exposition of the **Penitential Psalms**, in German after the Vulgate, Jerome's, and Reuchlin's rendering — "not for learned or refinedly-formed spirits, but for my rough Saxons." In 1518, Luther's disciple **Agricola** issued his Lord's-Prayer-sermons, without his knowledge, and in 1519 Luther revised them once more; at the close he set a short dialogue, joining onto the seven petitions, between the heavenly Father and the child drawing near to him.

By then he had become the great Reformer, and stood in the giant conflicts for the pure Word of God. His piety was thereby shaped with might. Whether he had the true faith to lay hold on salvation — this gave him inward trouble; these *Anfechtungen* he himself accounted heavier than the reformatory ones which were manifest to the outer world. There men marked only his confident courage. Fearlessly he stood before Cajetan at Augsburg. "Christ live, Martin die,"²⁸ he said, in the presage that it would not come to a good end for him. Full of joy and full of peace he was after that hearing — although he knew not how long yet he could work unmolested, although he in earnest weighed the thought of going, perhaps, to France. Precisely at that time he issued the above-mentioned exposition of the Lord's Prayer for the simple laity themselves. Shortly before this, he had written in a letter to Lange, dated 6 June [1518]:²⁹ that no cause troubled him for his own salvation more than the newly-embittered conflict. But in the very midst of it he worked still by Word and Writing — by labour on the Epistle to the Galatians, by fresh exposition of the Psalms — giving [rules] for the Christian life. For the "poor, humble, simple" ones there provided, from 1518 to 1520, many small writings: the repeated editions of "the Exposition of the Ten Commandments, with reference to the chief sins, especially serving for confession"; the sermons "on the Contemplation of the Suffering of Christ," "on Prayer and the Procession in Cross-week," "on Usury" — all writings "for the very simple Christians," to give them the Word of Life for particular circumstances. 1520 brought in addition the "Trostschrift *Tessaradekas*," or "the Fourteen Nothelfer" — a consolation-writing for those who are burdened with temporal suffering, occasioned by Spalatin, at the time of the Elector's illness.³⁰ There followed a sermon on the "Preparation for Dying," occasioned by his own death-terrors of the autumn of 1519; it gives comfort in the Anfechtung on account of damnation.

Out of 1520 there are further sermons on "Repentance," "Baptism," "the Supper," "on Good Works," an addition to the "Exposition of the Lord's Prayer," a "Lord's Prayer Exposition for Oneself and Behind Oneself," and one to the "Exposition of the Ten Commandments" — a forerunner of the Catechism: the faith and the Lord's Prayer are added, as the support in which everything that stands in the Scripture and is necessary for a Christian to know is grasped. To this he had leisure and time in the midst of the labour of the great reformatory writings of 1520.

When over the first, "To the Christian Nobility of the German Nation," he did not receive full assent from friends, he stressed²⁸ what is glorious and mighty and has a look of authority. **[Here Große quotes Luther's *Prayer before the Diet of Worms* verbatim; the prayer is set in extenso:]**

> "Ah God, thou my God, do thou stand by me against all the world's reason and wisdom. Thou must do it — it is, in truth, not my cause but thine. For my own person I have here nothing to do, and nothing to settle with these great lords of the world. Gladly, indeed, would I have good, quiet days, and be unentangled. But thine is the cause, LORD — the cause that is righteous and eternal. Stand by me, thou true, thou eternal God; I lean upon no man. It is in vain and empty; everything limps that is fleshly and that tastes after the flesh. O God, O God — hearest thou not, my God? Art thou dead? No, thou canst not die: thou hidest thee only. Hast thou chosen me for this? I ask thee — as, indeed, I certainly know. Well then, so let God rule — I who my life long never thought to be against such great lords. Nor did I take it upon myself. O God, so stand thou by me in the name of thy dear Son Jesus Christ, who shall be my shelter and shield — yea, my strong fortress — through the power and strengthening of thy Holy Spirit. LORD, where art thou? Thou, my God, where art thou? Come, come — I am ready, ready to leave even my life, patient as a little lamb. For righteous is the cause; and so I will not sunder myself from thee eternally. Let this be sealed in thy name. The world must leave me over my conscience unforced; and should my body — which is the work of thy hands — go to shivers therein, the soul is thine and belongeth to thee, and stayeth with thee eternally. Amen. God help me — amen."³¹

Given over to God's will we find him on the Wartburg. Gladly would he have died for his Lord's cause; and now he has, therefore, only the greater hope. He wrote to Cranach on 28 April 1521 from Frankfurt: "I commit you to God. I let myself be shut in and hidden — I know not yet myself where; and although I would rather have suffered death from the tyrants, especially at the hands of the raging Duke George, yet I must not, until his time, despise the counsel of good people. The Jews must one day sing *Io, Io, Io* [on Good Friday, in jubilation]; the Easter-day will also come to us, and then we shall sing *Alleluia*."³²

How he worked for his people in the quiet of his Patmos!³³ Here, again, he had inward *Anfechtungen* — most in particular in the year 1521 during the reading upon the pericopes, from which grew forth the [Wartburg-Postille]. Especially did Luther compose the Epistle-sermon and the Gospel-sermon "for each Sunday." The later editors divided the Postille into a Gospel- and an Epistle-part; in 1527 Stephan Roth, Magister from Zwickau (Docent in Wittenberg from 1528), issued it in full — with some transpositions — though yet without the Epistles. These appear first in the edition of Cruciger, 1543.³⁴

Luther taught now boldly and confidently from the beginning of 1522 — returned from the Wartburg to new reformatory work. Full of fresh strength and fiery spirit, he held for eight days one after the other the famous *Invocavit* sermons on the disorderly doings of the *Schwärmer* in Wittenberg.³⁵ All Luther's labours in the following years aim always at the edification of the Christian people. His strivings for the shape of the evangelical German divine service (with God's Word and prayer), for the rendering of all the books of Scripture (which, gradually, up to their completion in 1534, appeared with the famous prefaces), for the issuing of expositions of certain books (especially 1 Peter and Genesis), and further for the sanctification of the household-estate of the Christians in society and in office, wish nothing else.

Alongside these outstanding known works, still further writings and letters accompany, again and again, to particular edification: in 1522 there appeared anew a short exposition of the Ten Commandments. Now he called it **his** *Betbüchlein.* His chief aim was, therewith, to sweep aside those prayer-books in which "so much misery of the most various sins was recounted, so much un-Christian foolishness in the prayerlets to God and to his saints was driven into the simple ones — and which, nevertheless, with promise of indulgence and with red-lettered title-page, puffed themselves up on high." He wished, instead, to "hold up a simple Christian form and manner, to know the sins and to pray them." Even here he still added the Ave Maria. He joined himself to the customary practice of the folk, but took care that it should be used only as a biblical greeting from Luke 1:28, to God's praise and honour, and not as if by it Christ's Word and Faith should be cursed.

The persecution of the pure Gospel in Holland moved him in 1523 to the consolation-writing "To all the dear Christians in Holland, Brabant and Flanders": he praises God upon the honour of martyrdom. In 1523 he wrote, among other things, a letter of comfort to Herr Bartholomäus von Staremberg on the death of his consort; in 1524, new sermons on merchant-trade and usury.

While the Peasants' War moved him to writings first of admonishing, then of earnestly warning, kind — while he had to strive against Erasmus over the free will — while the strife against Zwingli and all Supper-Schwärmer began — he himself founded (for his own peace) the evangelical model parsonage. Not long before, he had, in his loneliness with many Anfechtungen, sought after intimate, cheering intercourse; he had then borne witness for the still-despised estate of marriage, and had stressed that one must ask God in earnest for a pious spouse, and must not be ashamed of this prayer. Now, upon his prayer, he had found his spouse. For he later wrote to a friend, as counsel — the friend wishing to be married: "Dear fellow, do as I: when I would take my Käthe, I asked the Lord our God in earnest!"

From 13 June 1525 on he now often writes, out of experience, upon the joys and sorrows of the married estate — after twelve years of marriage with these beautiful, but earnest words: "It has, God be praised, gone well with me, for I have a pious, faithful wife, upon whom a man's heart may rely, as Solomon says.³⁶ Ah, dear Lord God — marriage is not a natural, but a divine gift, the sweetest, indeed the chastest life, above all celibacy when it turns out well; when it turns out ill, however, it is [the hell of earth]."³⁷

As a particular writing for devotional literature there is then to be named, in 1526, "Whether men of war can also be in a blessed estate." But the **crown of all writings from Luther's pen** are those which grew out of the Saxon Visitation: the **Great and Small Catechism** of 1529, which was only prepared for by the *Betbüchlein* and kindred writings. The Catechisms will be, through and through, edifying. They contain the child-lore, "so that every Christian should know what is needful — such that whoever knows this not, cannot be counted among the Christians, nor admitted to any Sacrament."³⁸ They were to come first into the hands of the clergy and the teachers, but through them [into every hand].

### §2. Luther and his Significance for Devotional Literature — Part Two: The Life from 1527 to 1546

*Source: I b) §2, pp. 30–38. The years 1527–1546 — the age of the *Ein feste Burg*, the Coburg fortress, the household preacher, the pastoral letters, the *Tischreden*, and the death at Eisleben.*

How especially do the years 1527–1530 show us Luther in his inner life! The strife with Zwingli (out of which in 1528 came the great *Bekenntnis vom Abendmahl*),³⁹ the strife with Henry VIII, with Papists and Sectarians — wherein his easily-flaming spirit went too far in single expressions and images — inwardly troubled him. The plague reigning in 1527 he does not flee. **"Whether one may flee before dying"** he writes,⁴⁰ occasioned by Heß in Breslau; and he remained, as a right pastor, in Wittenberg. Otherwise it stands with his own conflicts: they give him much to bear. In faith, however, he wrestles himself again through; and the prayer and intercession of his friends — whom he industriously calls upon in letters — help him ever again to the victory. In 1526 there had broken out especially his kidney-suffering; in 1527 there followed heavy bodily oppressions and great soul-anguish. On 6 July 1527 he confessed to Bugenhagen and made himself ready to die, took leave of wife and child and asked for intercession, but felt himself soon strengthened. This latter request — and with it the thanks for the same, and for its being heard — runs through many letters of that time.

Up to early 1528 and again at the beginning of 1529 much outward and inward distress burdened him. The sickness passing through Germany in 1529 — the **English sweat** — and likewise the Turkish danger of 1528 and 1529 let him prove himself once more undismayed; the latter drove him to prayer, to which he zealously admonished in word and writing. The hymn **"Ein feste Burg ist unser Gott"** stems well from that time.⁴¹

The year 1530 brought Luther's sojourn on the **Coburg fortress.** As on the Wartburg, so here again Luther lets us look richly into his deep spirit and his strong, zealous prayer-life. To prayer he admonishes the clergy at the Diet of Augsburg, against the Turks and for his own cause; he is one who is sure that everything he asks shall come to pass. In Coburg he wrote also, for his own and others' comfort, "Chosen Scripture-sayings on the cause with which the Christians console themselves in the sufferings that for the sake of the divine Word come upon the believers," further "Short admonition to the bearing of the cross," and "Admonition to the Sacrament of the Body and Blood of our Lord." Despite all this labour and this earnest intercourse with God, he cared also for the innocent recreations of life. Melanchthon he comfortingly urged to playing and singing; and he himself will not have let them alone. He has, for example, exercised himself with Dietrich in crossbow-shooting. Even when at the close of his sojourn earnest thoughts of his life's end trouble him, and he feels himself already, at forty-seven years, as an old man, he yet knows how to return joyful and full of hope to Wittenberg.

There he had further to labour especially on the rendering and exposition of Scripture. Now follow calmer years of his life. His Anfechtungen for a time relatively lessen — even though we hear from 1531–1533 of many kinds of bodily weakness-states. His piety is too firmly grounded that it should lose thereby. Now one may see it in its fairest form take shape in the household circle; and out of small consolation-letters, consolation-writings, and edifying labours it shines forth brightly. His letters give counsel in the most various matters: now he sets forth intercession, now he warns, now he counsels, now he consoles. To his mother he communicates in her sickness: "We must be certain of God in Christ and console ourselves upon his victory," and closes: "There pray for you all my children and my Käthe." To the sick (Kaspar Müller, 1534), to the Anfechtungs-tried, he gives out of his rich experience the counsel that one should give the devil's thoughts of salvation and of God's providence a discharge. To a Herr von Stockhausen, whom suicide-thoughts tormented, he counsels that he not be left alone by his wife, that he should work, be active, cheerful, hear and read new tidings, despise the devil, remember God in Christ and console himself. Widows he consoles at the death of the spouse (1530), a father at the death of the son (1535), wives at the imprisonment of the husband. What he did in letters for others, he showed also to his household. From the spring of 1532 to the end of 1534 he preached as household-father before wife, children, servants, and guests⁴² — an edifying preaching-of-repentance for his own house-church.

His illnesses meanwhile continued. **"God has done wonders on me this night, and does them still, through pious people's intercession,"** he says at Wittenberg after his recovery. A relapse forced him indeed to new death-preparation, but again the way went upward. Still many a conflict was to be gone through, still many a writing was to be written. In 1538 and 1541 it was again necessary to pray against the Turks. In 1539 there appeared a new writing on usury. Consolation-letters and admonition-writings were to be dispatched. So there appeared, in 1542, a consolation-writing "for women to whom it has gone amiss in the pains of childbirth."⁴³ But the longing to die, and death-presentiments, gathered thicker. In 1538 a painful dysentery attacked him for weeks; in 1539 a vertigo. With the expectations of his own end came those of the Last Day. The dark moral conditions, chiefly in Wittenberg, let him long for the dear Last Day; and in view of it he called out, at the 1544 consecration of the castle-church at Torgau, zealously to intercession for the Church.⁴⁴ In 1545 he wished on that account to leave Wittenberg altogether. But however heavily the Anfechtung of the forties again pressed him down, he remained at bottom yet the immovable man of prayer and of faith. Many testimonies to this the *Tischreden* furnish, and particular events in his family and in his circle of friends of that time.

The *Tischreden*⁴⁵ rest upon the notes of **Anton Lauterbach.** After Lauterbach had, from 1529 onward, studied several years in Wittenberg, and there had already been Luther's table-guest, he came in 1537–1539 as Deacon in Wittenberg again to Luther's table, and made in the years 1538 and 1539 continuous notes from the discourses which he heard at Luther's table. With notes of **Veit Dietrich** from the years 1529–1534 and 1540–42, of **Mathesius** from the years 1540–1542, and others, they were later printed and set in order by **Aurifaber**,⁴⁶ and are so up to this day spread in various re-workings. We see through them into Luther's happy family life, into his joy at his Käthe, as at his children. Yet he stresses once, that this against Anfechtungen does not alone help — but that the trust upon the merit of Christ carries out everything. We look further into his sense, opened for friendship, for nature, for music, for noble sociability, and for right humour. Yet we experience again that only his firm faith in his Saviour, and his fervent intercourse with him, brought him to this healthy standing. Therefore he remains also — in the midst of anxiety — one who, in trust upon Christ, knows how to save himself; and the devil-spectre (which to him and to his imagination was actually sensibly perceptible)⁴⁷ has, for him, lost its terror. The other matter is, that according to biblical view it is warranted to assume that Luther, as a particular weapon of God, was especially attacked with inward and outward Anfechtungen by the devil and his host. To him, therefore, the *lords and mighty ones who rule in the darkness of this world, the evil spirits under the heavens* (Eph. 6:12), stepped forth undoubtedly for outward perception more visibly and remarkably than they do to other believers — the "smaller spirits," if I may so speak, in the kingdom of God.

Some few important examples for the manner and might of Luther's believing prayer-life may close this sketch.

**See and hear him in 1541 at Weimar, at Melanchthon's sickbed!** Melanchthon lies there, the eyes already broken, consciousness, speech, hearing seemed vanished. His face was sunken. Luther grew afraid beyond measure, and cried out: "God forbid, how has the devil disgraced me this instrument!" Then he turned himself at once to the window, and prayed. Of this he later said: "There must our Lord God stand by me; for I threw the sack before his door and rubbed his ears with all his promises, that he would hear prayer — those which I knew how to reckon up out of Holy Scripture — that he *must* hear me, if I were otherwise to trust in those promises." Then he grasped Melanchthon by the hand and bade him be of good cheer, for he would not die; although God had cause to slay him, he willed not the sinner's death, but rather that the same should turn and live; Melanchthon must not give room to the mourning-spirit and become a self-murderer, but must trust in the Lord, who can kill and make alive again, wound and again heal. Whereupon Melanchthon began again to draw breath, though he could yet for a long time not speak, then looked Luther straight upon, and asked that he not be held back but let go from hence, since he was on a good journey and nothing better could yet befall him. But Luther replied: "By no means. Thou must serve our Lord God yet further." Then he had something quickly brought for him to eat, and threatened him, when he would not take it: "Listen thou, Philip: shortly, thou *must* eat with me, or I put thee under the ban." Melanchthon recovered. Luther wrote on it to Lange at Erfurt on 2 July: **"We found Melanchthon dead; through a manifest miracle of God, he lives."**⁴⁸

No wonder, that after such experiences, in 1542 Joachim asked for intercession from Luther and Melanchthon for the Turkish war. He received their assent.

An experience of another kind had the pious pray-er upon the death of his little **Magdalena** on 20 September 1542.⁴⁹ When it was sure to him that God would take her to himself, and she lay in the last extremity, he fell down at her bed on his knees, wept bitterly, and asked God to release her. Then she passed away and fell asleep in his hands. To his wife he spoke comfort: "I have sent a saint into heaven. O had I such a death, such a death would I take upon me this hour."

So indeed was it to come. The more of his old friends who went on before him, the more had he desire to depart. The hour came for him, as is well known, at **Eisleben,**⁵⁰ whither he journeyed at the end of January 1546. From there he writes to his wife, when she is anxious about him: "Pray, and let God care!" and communicates, when his mediation between the Mansfeld lords seemed to be turning out well: "So must one grasp, that God is the hearer of prayer." To a Hohensteinian *Rentmeister* he wrote a word he was fond of using — into a *Hauspostille*, John 8:51: "Whosoever keepeth my word shall never see death." In his chamber he did, as usual, at the window earnestly his evening-prayer, and then turned cheerfully for a short conversation with Jonas, Rudtfeld, a servant, and his two sons, and fell peacefully asleep.

On 16 February he spoke at table much of sickness and of dying. On 17 February he prayed often before noon at the window; in the evening after earnest conversations on the seeing-again in the world to come, likewise; and now he passed the last night of his life — as is known — with much sighing and prayer. After 10 o'clock he entered the sleeping-chamber with the words: **"God rule; I go to bed. Into thy hands I commend my spirit; thou hast redeemed me, thou true God."** When he had lain down, he gave the hand for good-night and said: "Dr. Jonas and M. Cölius and you others: pray for our Lord God and for his Gospel, that it may go well with him; for the Council of Trent and the wretched Pope are wroth with him hard." After 1 o'clock, when he had become awake again, he said to Jonas: "Ah, Lord God, how sore am I; ah, dear Dr. Jonas, I reckon I shall stay here at Eisleben, where I was born and baptised." Walking about in the room he repeated: "Into thy hands I commend my spirit." When the town-clerk, his wife, the Counts Albrecht and von Schwarzburg with their consorts had come, and sought to strengthen him, he said: "Dear God, I am so sore and anxious; I journey forth." Directly upon this, already in the sweat of death, he prayed:

> **"O my heavenly Father, God and Father of our Lord Jesus Christ, thou God of all comfort — I thank thee that thou hast revealed to me thy dear Son Jesus Christ, on whom I believe, whom I have preached and confessed, whom I have loved and praised, whom the wretched Pope and all godless ones dishonour, persecute and slander. I pray thee, my Lord Jesus Christ, let my little soul be commended to thee. O heavenly Father, though I must lay down this body, yet I know most surely that I abide with thee eternally, and out of thy hands can none pluck me."**⁵¹

Then he prayed in Latin three times John 3:16 and Psalm 68:21: "We have a God who helps, and the Lord, the Lord, who saves from death." When Cölius offered him medicine, he cried: "I journey forth; my spirit will I give up." And finally he added, three times quickly one after the other: **"Father, into thy hands I commend my spirit; thou hast redeemed me, thou true God."** Then he became still, and said only one more audible *Ja* on the loud question: "*Reverende pater*, will you abide constant on Christ and the doctrine which you have preached?"

So died the great man of faith praying, as he had prayed while he lived.

### §2. Luther and his Significance for Devotional Literature — Part Three: The Writings-Catalogue

*Let us now give an overview of the newly-issued devotional writings of Luther then in print.* To this some remarks are to be sent ahead. Luther's prayers and edifying selections from his writings were, already in his own age and through all the centuries thereafter, gathered and worked over. So something also of the age of the particular editors will need again to be named. In the description of Luther's writings, completeness has not been striven for. What his sermons concern: there are here editions of the more scholarly, and the more edifying, kinds; only in so far as they are meant to serve the congregation directly are they mentioned here. His Scripture-exposition, further, so uniquely binds together the theological-scholarly and the practically-edifying side, that the question presses which [face of it belongs in the *Wegweiser* and which not].⁵²

## A. Luther's Own Devotional Writings, by Category

### The *Betbüchlein* and the Ways to Right Prayer

The *Betbüchlein* of 1522 and its enlargements have already been named above.⁵³ Luther enlarged the *Exposition of the Ten Commandments* by "an Exposition of the Holy Lord's Prayer, forward and backward" and by the further exposition of the Ave Maria. He set out his own method of prayer in the beautiful writing "**How one shall pray, for Master Peter the Barber**" (1534, pp. 126 ff.).⁵⁴ Here he gives the immediately-useful counsels for a right prayer-life: how out of the Ten Commandments one may — through meditation (*Betrachtung*) — arise, and how out of the petitions and words of Scripture and of the faith one may pass on to the prayer, in that one makes of each of these one thing — a doctrine, a thanksgiving, a confession, a prayer.

Also of high worth stands beside these the significant Trostschrift **"Tessaradekas Consolatoria — a Little Book of Fourteen Comforting Means for the Weary and Heavy-Laden"** of the year 1520.⁵⁵ Instead of the "Fourteen Nothelfer" (the fourteen intercessory saints then popularly invoked), Luther will set before the sick person a great painting in two panels, each with seven pictures. On the first panel he shows the evils:

1. the evil in us (sin and hell);
2. the future evil, or the evil before us (death);
3. the evil of the past, or the evil behind us (which points to God's mercy);
4. the evil from below, or the evil under us (hell);
5. the evil at our left, or the evil to our left hand (enemies);
6. the right evil, or the evil to our right hand (the evils of our friends);
7. the evil on high, or the evil above us (which points to Christ the Crucified).

On the second panel he shows the goods, under the like seven headings.⁵⁶

Of consolation-writings there is further to be found in Vol. VI [of the received editions]:

- p. 61 — **"A Sermon on Preparation for Dying"** — to set the holy Sacrament of the Altar, and Christ himself, over against the Anfechtung.⁵⁷
- p. 81 — **"Whether one may flee before dying"** (1527).
- p. 107 — **"Fair chosen sayings of the Holy Scripture, wherewith Luther has comforted himself in great Anfechtungen"** (1530).⁵⁸
- p. 115 — **"Consolation-writing for a person in high Anfechtungen."**

- Part 6: the shorter Epistles of Paul to the Galatians, Ephesians, Philippians, Colossians, and Thessalonians.

After the appended prefaces to the books there follows, verse by verse, so far as reference-passages are found in Luther, the exposition in a language adapted to our understanding. ### 5. Wahres Christentum nach Luther — after Statius

There follows in Große's account an edition that gathers from Luther's whole corpus a two-book anthology on *Wahres Christentum* — "true Christianity" — carried out by **Martin Statius,**⁶⁶ the seventeenth-century Brandenburg pastor (of whom Große treats more fully in his own biographical portrait below, in the post-Reformation section). Statius writes in his own preface that he undertook the "rather heavy labour" to gather out of all Luther's works — which he industriously read through from beginning to end — the material on *Wahres Christentum,* so that the false-Christian teachings might be overthrown and the Lutheran Christianity fostered; and that the enemies of the truth, who slander Dr. Luther as founder and beginner of an evil life among the Lutherans, might have their mouths stopped, to God's honour and to many men's benefit.

The first book handles, in fifteen chapters with many subsections, faith (dead, historical, [saving]; origin, properties of the same, etc.). The second book handles, in sixteen chapters, love (or good works) — for example, how faith and works are close of kin; that in the article on justification, faith and love are to be distinguished; which works Luther refuses and which he does not, and the like. The four antique copperplates are portraits of Luther, Melanchthon, Statius, and of the Diet of Augsburg. Whoever will come to know thoroughly what Luther, out of Scripture, has clearly recognised and expressed on faith and works, is to reach for this materially fine-ordered collection.⁶⁷

### §3. Luther's Fellow-Workers and their Piety

*Source: I b) §3, pp. 65–72. The circle of Wittenberg and other-city Reformers around Luther, with a portrait-gallery of their prayer-lives and their contributions to the devotional literature: Melanchthon, Bugenhagen, Jonas, Cruciger (and his wife Elisabeth von Meseritz), Eber, Rhau, Alberus, Heß, Spengler, Linck, Mathesius, and — with due confessional caution — some Reformed pray-ers received across the confessional line.*

As **Luther was a man of prayer,** so his friends and the propagators of the Reformation in all Germany. Already the history of the church-hymn of that age proves it — in which the hymns out of Scripture (chiefly out of the Psalter) simply *well up.* So too a single glance into what we otherwise know of their praying in life and in death. The cross which the Lord laid on them in various outward sufferings — not least, however, in spiritual *Anfechtung* — became for them the school of prayer.

That **Melanchthon**⁷⁷ was not only the greatest scholarly representative of the Reformation, but also a man of deep piety, is sufficiently known. So he has, as the teacher of many thousands, worked lastingly upon their evangelical piety. We know of him that he — who had much cross to bear in his own house, not least in his weak health — much prayed. Already early at 2 a.m. he rose, to strengthen himself through prayer before the labour. The distresses of the Church and of the single congregation he bore, though not himself a direct pastor, upon a praying heart.

The prayer for chastity (Löhe, *Samenkörner,* No. 99), the short exposition of the Lord's Prayer (*Großes Gebetbuch* of the Evangelischer Verein, No. 526; Klaiber, *Volksbibliothek* I, p. 568 ff.; compare in general Ledderhose's *Philipp Melanchthon* I, pp. 407–582)⁷⁸ testify this as fully as does his utterance to Camerarius: **"If I did not care, I would not pray."** His usual saying was: "Our Lord God help, and be gracious to us!" He loved prayer-formulas and industriously commended the Lord's Prayer. The Apostles' Creed he himself prayed three times daily. He recommended household-devotion, but also industriously pressed for the visiting of the Church. He, who in 1540 had been given back to the Church at Luther's prayer, gave himself over praying, and consoling and letting himself be consoled, [in his final years].⁷⁹

After Luther's death — and then after that of his wife (1557) — he often longed for peace and blessedness. "If it is God's will that I shall die, I will die heartily gladly, and pray God only for a cheerful departure," he said on 8 April [1560] after a fever-attack to Peucer. On 13 April he sighed: "Lord, make an end!" The 19 April *[came, and he prayed aloud. In the afternoon he said that he longed for heaven. Then he prayed:] "Take, Lord, my Spirit. Thou hast redeemed me, thou true and truthful God!"* On Magister Philipp's *"Ja"* to this, Röschel prayed for Melanchthon, as death was near.⁸⁰

Through the Melanchthon-jubilee-celebration of 1897 an edifying tract of his own has been printed:

**Melanchthon, Sprüche vom christlichen Leben.** Barmen, *Wupperthaler Traktatgesellschaft,* E. Biermann. 30 pp, 5 pf, paper. Very edifyingly, the schoolman speaks of repentance and confession, of faith, of cross and prayer, of the power of faith in temporal matters, of obedience to authority, of married life. To the enrichment of the whole he adds the pertinent divine sayings in a plain form, full of deep content.

**The Wittenberg pastor Bugenhagen,**⁸¹ who in a consolation-booklet sought to console the sick, cared like Melanchthon that also the youth might learn to pray, as his prayer-booklet of 1539 proves. Some of his best prayers are taken into the *Gebetbuch* of the Evangelischer Verein and into the *Große Gebetsformeln*.

**Dr. Jonas,**⁸² who in Wittenberg up to 1541 lived with Luther under much Anfechtung on account of his weak bodily constitution, — that the cross was his best medicine, and that the Psalter was his prayer-book, his hymns bear witness. He died in 1555 at Eisfeld, comforted through firm [hope in Christ, since he had many prophecies and Psalm-passages by heart].⁸³

By his side steps **Cruciger's wife, Elisabeth von Meseritz,** the singer of the hymn *"Herr Christ, der einig Gottes Sohn."*⁸⁴ Her house in Wittenberg was a house of peace, in which Luther's hymns were the dearest table-companions.

A house of prayer must likewise have been **Eber's** house in Wittenberg. After Luther's death Eber remained with Bugenhagen and Cruciger alone of the professors and students in Wittenberg through the plague. Consolation he drew from the Psalter and from the prayer of 2 Chron. 20:6–9, upon which he afterwards sang: *"Wenn wir in höchsten Nöten sein."*⁸⁵ In the later school-conflicts, the power of the Gospel comforted him. From the pure Word to draw right instruction, mighty comfort, strength, and life — that remained his chief business. Under earnest calling upon God, and under steadfast confession of his faith in Christ, he went softly home on 10 December 1569.

Like these pastors did the famed **Cantor and Book-Printer Georg Rhau.** He, who in 1525 had once found Luther unconscious in his room and with music brought him again to himself, tuned, with his *Hortulus animae — Lustgarten der Seele* (which from 1547 on appeared often — a collection of edifying thoughts out of the Reformers' writings), the strings of the souls of the Lutheran Christian people seeking edification, in the right way.

As the Wittenberg Reformers, so also the others prayed. How was **Erasmus Alberus,**⁸⁶ in his wandering-life through Staden, Babenhausen, Wittenberg, Magdeburg, Hamburg, Neubrandenburg, strengthened by prayer — and how had it to raise him up even at life's end, in distress and want! When at Neubrandenburg on 4 May the salary was refused him, he rose in the night from his bed, went into the garden, and prayed under the open sky, on his knees, so fervently to God, that God would deliver him from the persecution. Then in the house he took the Psalter and did not let it go again until, on 5 May at 4 a.m., praying earnestly Ps. 94 and 81, he fell asleep.

**Johann Heß,**⁸⁷ the Breslau preacher and professor, well-proved in Anfechtungen, most gladly read himself the Psalter for comfort; upon his psalm-book he had written: "The staff of my old age."

How many faithful pray-ers can South Germany and Middle Germany show forth! The Nürnberg councillor **Lazarus Spengler**⁸⁸ gives, in his life and in his house, the proof that pious laymen competed with the pastors and professors in the prayer-life: the lamp of his house was Ps. 127, and the comfort of the Gospel helped him over his household-cross of many deaths, and over his weak health.

**Wenzeslaus Linck,**⁸⁹ the Pastor at the New Hospital in Nürnberg, was likewise an industrious pray-er, as his printing of morning- and evening-devotions for the household proves. His last work of office was, that in 1547 he issued in print "A Prayer to be used in Houses and Churches," after the Council had ordered that at the eleven-o'clock bell every household-father with his own should turn to God in prayer, so that God might avert the [Turk-and-plague-distress] from them. — **Mathesius**⁹⁰ — the mining-town preacher of Joachimsthal, and Luther's biographer — was a pious singer, and through pious prayer-life-in-God's-Word a wholesome influence upon [his people at the mines and their households].

Of **Johann Habermann,** the writer of the popularly-used *Betbüchlein* of 1567, more below.⁹¹

In the same age, the Reformer **Justus Menius**⁹² had worked with his "Christliche Hauskirche" of 1520/1620.

Also the **princes** who faithfully served the Reformation preceded — often as pious pray-ers — their peoples: *Margrave Georg of Brandenburg the Confessor* († 1543 in Onolzbach) shared the ordering-affairs of the day with prayer and Scripture; *Duke Christoph of Württemberg* († 1568) as a pious Christian for right death-preparation chose an hourglass with the motto: *"Hour by hour, so runs the sand — my hour comes too."*⁹³

Even among the Reformed theologians of that age praying men are found. From the Psalter they especially learned that; so **Wolfgang Musculus** († 1562 in Bern), and his Augsburg colleague. To pray by the Psalter learned also the Schwenkfeldian **Adam Reisner** — known as the poet of *"In dich hab ich gehofft, Herr."*⁹⁴

This is enough of examples of how new life awoke, and prayer was practised.⁹⁵ To be sure, these are chiefly examples of God-men, of the leaders of the people (not least the preachers). But it strengthened also the people to do the like. Certainly it was not all at once a folk of pray-ers; that the people never becomes, and never was. But the Christian-evangelical practice broke through, by God's grace, gradually.

Strong on the other side, too, in the twenties and forties, are the utterances of Luther and his friends. What did one not experience at the church-visitation! How was one horrified, for example, at the dull-mindedness and coarseness of the peasants, their avarice, their many marriage-quarrels! How the moral conditions of his time grieved Luther! He knows how to speak sharply against the fleshly liberty, against disobedience to God's Word, against sins of all kinds — for which God's rod-of-wrath is the Turk-wars. In 1542 he issued an admonition to the Wittenberg townsfolk and university, that they might not, for God's sake, let the cry over themselves stand, that among them it grew ever worse with the Gospel. He inveighs against the greedy, usurious traffic in the burgher-estate, against the bestial lusts of the students, against the lukewarmness [of the clergy], against the tyranny of the Judas-lusts, against discord, avarice, [drunkenness], malice among the nobility at the courts, in cities and villages. In 1545 he would gladliest have left Wittenberg altogether, so evil went it there with unchastity and sin — as already the cut-out dresses proved. But one must think that much yet stood under the influence of the past, and that complaints over the sin in the people and in God's Church can never fall silent, so surely as sin will always drive its business.

What a new life, in contrast to the age before 1520, awoke in truth in all estates! Through the prayers and hymns of that age went a new tone of joyfulness — to serve God in one's calling — and there begins the pious practice to develop after the manner of Luther. Proof for the new life is given also by the circulation of the devotional writings of that age. To be silent of Luther — whose *Betbüchlein* already in 1524 appeared for the sixth time — there is above all **Urbanus Rhegius's *Seelenarznei***, which in High- and Low-German at Augsburg, Nürnberg, Leipzig, Magdeburg, Wittenberg appeared six times at least between 1529 and 1550. Brießmann's *Trostsprüche* (1524), Thomas Venatorius (†1551 in Nürnberg), Kaspar Huberinus (†1553 in Öhringen), Johann Spangenberg (†1550 in Eisleben) *Trostbücher*, Michael Weinmar (1523) and Cölius, prayers, the *Betbüchlein for various common matters* of 1543, Veit Dietrich's *Summarien und Gebete*, and Otto Körber's (1539–1544) *Trostbüchlein*⁹⁶ [and others]: all these testify, in their repeated (in part) editions, that the Christian people [was seeking such books]. The *Wittenberg Church Prayer-Book* of 1548 (*Gebetbuch* of the Evangelischer Verein, App. 3, Bk. 9) may close the list.

*Note: In the collections — especially in the Gebetbuch of the Evangelischer Verein — the above-named [are re-printed].*⁹⁷

Some of these writings of the Reformation-age are still to be had in new reprints, though — Brießmann, Venatorius, Rhegius, Huberinus, Spangenberg [and — as excursus in Tranche 2g — Jakob Andreä] follow individually below.

### §4. Urbanus Rhegius (1489–1541)

*Source: I b) §4, pp. 72–76. The Augsburg-then-Celle Lutheran superintendent, foremost popularizer of Reformation doctrine in South and North Germany; author of the *Seelen-Arznei,* one of the most-printed Lutheran consolation-books of the sixteenth century.*

**Urbanus Rhegius** was born in May 1489 at Langenargen on Lake Constance, likely of the Rhieger (Rieger) family.⁹⁸ [At the *Trivialschule* he studied, then at Freiburg-in-Breisgau...],⁹⁹ where his gifted noble fellow-students, whom he tutored, took him in their turn to their houses. A learned life was set open to him. He heard **[Mayer of Eck]**, Zasius, and others of the humanistic circle; from want of money, one of his noble students, Peutinger, made him welcome and secured him passage on his own account. In 1517 the Emperor crowned him. He now came ever more into the [Lutheran] direction, and studied theology, the Greek Fathers, and Hebrew. From here he came to Ingolstadt and heard of Luther's Disputation.

Through his own inner conflicts and Anfechtungen of the years [1519–1521] he became a zealous follower of Luther, — *"not through a sudden shock, but because I found in his teaching the right way, and I have, as an old Doctor, laid down the scholastic [scaffolding]."*¹⁰⁰

*[Rhegius as Augsburg preacher — successive dislocations under the Imperial-Diet-Interims — from 1530 at Celle in North Germany, then from Lüneburg 1531–1534 as Superintendent of the whole Duchy of Braunschweig-Lüneburg. Called-in visitations to Hamburg, Braunschweig, Leipzig, Berlin; he never turned aside, though the rough northern air did not suit him.]* A true Lutheran, with holy earnestness [in the doctrine], he was yet too much a pastor to [wish other than] the Wittenberg Concord — as also the Smalcald 1537 and the Regensburg Religionsgespräch 1541. At the last of these, [the strain of the deliberation and the journey wore] on him, already sickly, and on 23 May 1541 [he died at Celle]. On the day before, 22 May, he had held his last sermon and celebrated with his congregation the holy Supper. On Monday, 23 May, [a fever took him]. **On Ascension-day**¹⁰¹ he was buried in the city-church at Celle.

As a bold spirit he especially understood how to lay the thoughts of Luther upon the hearts of the clergy and the people. He minted the heavy gold of the pure doctrine into small coin for the folk; *edification in the deepest sense of the word* was the end of his striving, of his preaching, of his writing. He wished to make the troubled conscience sure of the Gospel — sure of the Mediator Christ. To this purpose served his writings, especially his *Little Confession of the Twelve Articles of Faith (the Apostles' Creed) and of Some Points of Scripture,* 1525.

Renewed is his ***Seelen-Arznei*** (Medicine for the Soul):

**Urbani Rhegii Seelen-Arznei,** with a life-account by W. Haccius. Hermannsburg, Druck und Verlag der Missionshandlung, 1894, 8°, 82 pp, 31 lines to the page, bound 90 pf.

Pp. 1–35 contain the life-account (Haccius). Pp. 25–33 give a passage — beginning and end — from Rhegius's *Dialogus of the Consolation-Rich Sermon that Christ, in Luke 24, from Jerusalem to Emmaus, held to the two disciples out of Moses and all the Prophets.* The writing was originally a wedding-gift for his wife, out of which grew an edification-book that was spread into the seventeenth century and — with much learning — gave the consolation of Scripture to troubled souls.

Pp. 36–40 then give the glorious evangelical Confession of 1521. Pp. 41–82 give the chief-writing: *The Medicine of the Soul for the Healthy and the Sick in Death-Distresses.* After an introduction on the consolation for the sick, Rhegius shows that sin, death, hell, and eternal damnation terrify men in death-distresses. He then gives what is to be set against each: against sin, the merit of Christ. Bible-sayings and comforting addresses spell out the thoughts. Against death, one is again to point to Christ the death-overcomer. There follow Words of God and histories of the Bible on eternal life and the resurrection. Similarly Christ, and faith in him, is to be set against hell [and against the devil]. Finally there stands, in summarising:

> "Now take heed how Christ has held himself on the cross in the Anfechtung. Follow after him, so far as God gives grace!"

The example of Christ is put forward as the example of the Mediator, who through his own Anfechtung has broken through sin, death, and hell — to be grasped in faith. There follows a heart-pressing consolation for the sick and the despairing, and the close is a personal, pastoral admonition not to despise the instruction. — The language of the little book is clear and light, heartily plain, and now and then rising into élan. For riper Christians who long lie on sick- and death-bed, and whose spirit is yet capable of longer contemplations, the little book is quite especially recommended.

### §5. Johannes Brießmann (1488–1549)

*Source: I b) §5, pp. 76–77. The Prussian Reformer, formerly Franciscan at Wittenberg, whose *Trostsprüche* of 1524 stand at the head of the Reformation-generation consolation-tracts.*

**Johannes Brießmann** (Brießmann, Prießmann) was born on 31 December 1488 at Cottbus in Lusatia.¹⁰² Formerly a Franciscan monk at Wittenberg, he was, after the first [conversion-experiences under the Reformation preaching], sent by Luther to Königsberg in Prussia, where he shaped, with Speratus and Poliander, the ordering of the young Prussian evangelical Church.¹⁰³ In [1549] he died at Königsberg, [comforted through Scripture].

His ***Trostsprüche*** (1524) opens with a mighty presentation, in ten sections, of consolation for the various needs of the soul.¹⁰⁴ He begins from Rom. 15:4 and then sets forth the whole consolation that lies in Christ — the High-Priest (1) and King (2) — who becomes the property of the Christian (3), so that the child of God may in his following become of one form with him. *(What sober, genuine mysticism!)*¹⁰⁵ To this following belongs suffering (Matt. 5:4–5), and now follows (4) the fuller consolation in outward and inward suffering after Matt. 5:4, (5) Ps. 43:1–2, (6) Nah. 1:3, (7) Ps. 37:5, Ps. 55:23, (8) Sach. 2 (9), yea, even in the fear of death: John 8:51 (10).

Truly, Brießmann gives to the sick and to those tried by Anfechtung — who have grown into the depth of Lutheran faith — costly words for consolation and strengthening. There are found in it, as Beck notes, echoes of the *Theologia Deutsch* and of Luther's thoughts.

### §6. Veit Dietrich (1506–1549)

*Source: I b) §6, pp. 77–79. Luther's *famulus* at Wittenberg, from 1535 preacher at St. Sebald in Nürnberg, editor of Luther's Hauspostille (1544) and household prayers-and-summaries.*

**Veit Dietrich** was born on 8 December 1506 at Nürnberg. His father was there a poor shoemaker. Through the mediation of the *Ratsschreiber* Spengler, he could go in 1522 to the University of Wittenberg; Spengler gave him his eldest son as [companion]. He quickly won the love of Luther and of Melanchthon. Luther took him in 1527 into his house; Melanchthon procured for him supports from his native city. Dietrich accompanied Luther in 1529 to Marburg. In 1530 he stayed with him at Coburg and became a witness of the mighty and inward prayer-life of Luther.¹⁰⁶ He himself there prayed daily three hours, and every fortnight received the holy Supper. In Wittenberg he became Magister, and also Adjunct (Dean) of the Philosophical Faculty. He seems, however, to have come into strife with Luther — perhaps because he would not give him his sister's daughter to wife. So he left, after fourteen years' sojourn, Wittenberg and went in 1535 to Nürnberg. He was to move as university-teacher to Tübingen, when through Melanchthon's recommendation, on the initiative of the councillor Baumgärtner in Nürnberg, a preacher's place at St. Sebald [was offered him — which he took and held until his death in 1549].

[Dietrich's method: to work] through the plain, childlike, folk-familiar, easily-grasped form. Dietrich learned from Luther clearness and firmness [of doctrine], and — [drew from Melanchthon] the love of classical study, the [ordered] rhythm of the thoughts. He gave out many Summaries and translated others of Melanchthon. His writings belong to those that already in the forties of our century were re-issued, and so are still available in copies-become-antiquarian.

**M. Veit Dietrichs Haus-Postille** — that is, sermons on all Sunday and feast-day Gospels, and on the Passion-history of Christ. Newly issued by Johann Tobias Müller. Stuttgart, Verlag von Samuel Gottlieb Liesching (now Gütersloh, Bertelsmann), 1845. Gr. 8°, IX + 462 pp, two-columns, 51 lines, 6 M unbound.

The preface speaks the praise of Dietrich, especially that which **Löhe** and **Harleß** give him. Löhe says in No. I of the *Kirchliche Mitteilungen aus und über Nordamerika* (1844):¹⁰⁷ *"Of Luther's Postillen one cannot properly say that they are popular for our people; our people is missing the great present-time of the Reformation, and thereby the sense and standpoint on which and through which the deep words of the man of God became commonly-intelligible. On the other hand, Dietrich's sermons, however full they are of Lutheran spirit, are truly commonly-intelligible and — over and above that — really lovely and drawing to be read. Also Dietrich has his own manner, and his language stands, on that account, above that of the pious Mathesius. To be sure, the cut of the newer homiletic Dietrich's sermons do not have; but neither do Luther's sermons, nor those of the greatest masters — and what does one lack in that?"*

Harleß says: *"There is sound food, plain word, pure doctrine, deep truth, and manifold life-application. How fair does Veit Dietrich open the riches of the life-relations, in which a simple word of Scripture holds within itself a fulness of fructifying truths! No less instructive and faith-strengthening at once is it to see with how clear a look this preacher weighs the conflicts which the old truth has to endure against her old and ever-newly-pressing adversaries — so that it often becomes one, as though one had in this witness of the Reformation a co-witness and co-fighter in the encounters of the present."*

### §7. Johannes Brenz (1499–1570)

*Source: I b) §7, pp. 79–85. The Württemberg Reformer — pastor at Schwäbisch-Hall, then chief adviser to Duke Ulrich and Duke Christoph, principal author of the Württemberg *Kirchenordnung*, Marburg-1529 defender of the Real Presence alongside Luther. Große closes the Reformation-Century section with Brenz as the transition-figure between Wittenberg and the German South, followed by a short **excursus on Jakob Andreä** (Brenz's student and son-in-law's father).*

[Brenz's father] was [a member of] the city-court [at Weil der Stadt in Swabia, born 24 June 1499]. His mother was Katharina [Hennig].¹⁰⁸ Pious were the parents according to the mind of their [age].

**Johannes** attended first the trivial-school at Heidelberg in the [11th] year, in the 12th that of Magister Johann Schmidlin at Vaihingen on the Enz. Well versed in all the [ancient] languages, he could in the 13th year, 1512, enter the University of Heidelberg — to study God-scholarship. After the preparatory studies he took it up especially from 1517 onward. In 1518 Luther came to Heidelberg¹⁰⁹ and made on him, as on his friends **Bucer, Billican, Brecht,** an ineffaceable impression. From 1520 he came forward, with great applause, in Luther's sense as teacher and preacher. He received the place of a canon at the collegiate foundation of the Church of the Holy Spirit at Heidelberg, after he had been ordained at Speyer. Through Luther's ban he also lost the right to hold lectures. Then he found — at Schwäbisch-Hall, at the recommendation of his study-friend **Johann Isenmann** — a sphere of work. He was called as preacher at the Michaeliskirche after his trial-sermon on 8 September 1522.

*"He commended himself, as much through the dignified bearing, the earnest of his character, and the sound content of his teaching, as through the expression and delivery of his sermons."* When the twenty-four-year-old youth preached, with manly eloquence, the Word of God as the sole means of salvation over against the cloister-clergy with their fasting, sacrificing, feasting — hard conflicts arose for him quickly. He carried much through, however. In 1523 he could already press for the abolition of the Mass. From the outset he worked for the youth-instruction, for the bettering of moral discipline and order, chiefly occasioned by the Peasants' War. In 1527–1528 he had already, before Luther, given out his catechism.¹¹⁰

Brenz took part from 1525 also in the deliberations on the holy Supper, as a faithful defender of Luther's teaching. So he was present at the Colloquy of Marburg 1529. Then we find him in 1530 at the Diet of Augsburg, called there by Georg of Brandenburg-Ansbach. Through all this the connexion with Luther grew ever closer. Luther speaks himself repeatedly very approvingly of Brenz's exposition of Holy Scripture. Soon after the return from Augsburg, he married the young widow of the Rat Wesel, née Gräter. For 17 years he lived with her in a happy marriage, in which she bore him one son and five daughters. In the thirties he had to serve the churches of the Margraviate of Brandenburg-Ansbach and of Nürnberg with the working-out of [their *Kirchenordnung* of 1533].¹¹¹

Beyond these, [Brenz worked far into other territories — Württemberg most of all — both by his more scholarly and by his more edifying writings]. [The Interim of 1548 broke the peace of his life-work.]¹¹² When after Luther's death the Interim need arose, he stood in earnest opposition to it. From his refuge, he was again taken back on Elector Ulrich's fall by Ulrich; when he would take the Word up again, he was — as it were — Granvella-tasked to seize [him] captive. On [21 June?] 1548 he received from an unnamed councillor a warning with the words: **"Flee, flee, as swiftly as thou canst!"** Brenz hurried away. Spanish soldiers already came into the [country]; with his six children [his wife had to depart]. [They found him one night in his hiding-place, but not to bring him into danger — they had to move on.]

He was hidden by Ulrich at Hohenwittlingen, on the fortress of Württemberg. Here too he had to shift himself off to Basel, where he found hospitable friends, until [Duke Ulrich called him back]. When he came back, he hurried to his own [family] at Stuttgart, [but had to hide anew, for a fortnight, under the roof of a house in the upper city].

By a citizen his life was preserved, while the whole city was searching for him. Finally [he found refuge]. Then Ulrich sent him to the castle of [Hornberg in the Black Forest]. Here he stirred, as [a stranger], the wonder of the people, because he did not curse, drink, [gamble] — he comforted the sick and diligently occupied himself with the Scripture.

In September 1550 he moved to Urach, where he took the daughter of his [former friend] to his second marriage. He had another [six children]. On Ulrich's death, Christoph took him to Sindelfingen and Ebingen, and then to the Council [of Trent], with the Württemberg [Confession] itself — to which he had wished to be heard before [the Council], though vainly. After the Peace of Passau [1552 he became] preacher [at Stuttgart].

As churchman, preacher, [teacher], [organiser of] the re-establishment of the [Württemberg Church], up to his death (1560) [in the case of Ulrich; Brenz outlives to 1570 under Christoph and beyond], he was Christoph's adviser. Up to nearly his end he [preached] on feast-days and on Fridays.

His office and household [gave him a full life]. About [70] years old, a fever laid hold on him. His son, professor of theology at Tübingen, and the Stuttgart clergy came to him; he read out his testament, [prayed] the Apostles' Creed, made confession of sins, and received the holy Supper. After the *Amen* on the Creed-confession he passed away without visible struggle on **11 September 1570.** On 12 September his burial took place in the *Stiftskirche.* The corpse was let down near the pulpit.

Of Brenz's smaller edifying writings, none has been brought near to our own age's edification (Klaiber's *Volksbibliothek* is out of print); yet something of his sermons is in the rendering of the two following books:

**1. Kurze Auslegung der Sonn- und Festtags-Epistel** von Joh. Brenz, formerly Provost at Stuttgart. After the 1560 issue of Jakob Grätter, worked over for use in prayer-hours and household-devotions by A. L. Th. Grunwald, Lutheran Pastor at Stuttgart 1855. Type and price [as given at source].

**2. Sonn- und Festtags-Evangelien** by Johannes Brenz, formerly at Stuttgart. Two volumes bound in one. Translated from the Latin by Ludwig de Marees. With a Preface, "The Prayer of the Lord," by the same. Cottbus 1878, print and publisher of the *Gotthold-Expedition.* Vol. 1: pp. 1–264; Vol. 2: up to 510. 3 M reduced price. [Preface follows Brenz's life-account, an appreciation of the sermons that announce God's Word alone.] In the exposition of the text and [in the applying of the same to the needs of his age], [Brenz] stands foremost. He preaches therefore both — Law and Gospel — simply and plainly, though also striking pictures and comparisons stand to his service, no less a fulness of examples out of biblical history. *"The connexion of the Old and the New Testament, the unity of the Kingdom of God and its continuing development from the earliest [times]..."*¹¹³

## Section b) The Time from 1550 to 1600

*Central Point: The first prayer-books and prayer-collections.*

### §1. General Overview

*Source: I b) — Zeit 1550–1600 — §1, pp. 86–87.*

The age of the second half of the sixteenth century had the task of **building on the ground once laid.** The inheritance had to be worked over, formed out, and fixed. That did not go without heavy conflict and strife. The battle against the Romans (who were newly consolidating themselves) did not cease; the battle in the own camp — against the Reformed and those who mediated with them — burned more fiercely after Luther's death.¹¹⁵ One might easily suppose that, over this strife (which was often carried on with hardness and un-Christian vehemence), the inward life and the inward building-out of the Church would be neglected. That is not so. First, the zealous fighters who guarded the dear inheritance of the Reformation were, notwithstanding all their one-sidedness, mostly men of through-and-through-genuine piety. Secondly, the moral state of the congregations required a labour on their inner deepening.

The pure Gospel had been received; but the people had in many cases only outwardly submitted to it. Now there had first to come the through-going re-shaping of the inner life. Here, on the one side, a false liberty was to be resisted; on the other, a false security. This service the *Erbauungsschriften* of the age render — while at the same time they reckon with the needs of those in the congregations who — besides the regular edification in the generally-attended divine services and the many weekly services with their many liturgical prayers — sought for means for the house, for a Christian house-ordering, for a household-devotion. This is met by the **prayer-book literature** — which in its proper sense begins here above all with **Habermann (1567–).** This stands, therefore, at the centre. New prayers are drawn up; older ones from Luther and his companions, and also from the fathers of the ancient and medieval Church, are gathered. Devotions for the house arising out of the Haustafel, the catechism, and so on; contemplations for the preparation for the holy Supper. Alongside, the consolation-literature that began in the earlier age continues. Plagues, wars, Turkish danger, persecution, inward spiritual Anfechtung [give occasion for continued *Trostbuch*-writing].¹¹⁶

Because the lay-congregation is to be served, one writes in a language suited to their understanding. The learned discussions here speak plainly; a certain *popularity* runs through, and betrays that the writers thought of the "little ones" as they wrote. The number of the writings grows, by far, greater — which is also a proof for the greater need.

### §2. Traces in the Literature — and a Glance into the Piety it Witnessed and Nourished

*Source: I b) — Zeit 1550–1600 — §2, pp. 87–90. Große draws the general trace of the post-Reformation piety from the survey of the whole book-trade output.*

Attention must be drawn in this age to the *heightened longing* of laymen for edifying literature. To be sure, this is found only among the educated laity. One must yet consider that the Reformation and its introduction of the German schools had first-of-all effected only the most necessary beginnings of formation. The number of those who can read is still extremely small. Only the higher classes go through the schools and can really read the Bible and the devotional books. In the lower classes of the population it is always only single, especially zealous men who can do the like.

Ever again appear Rhegius's *Seelen-Arznei* and the *Trostbücher* of Cölius (†1573 as Superintendent in Leipzig)¹¹⁷ and Spangenberg — books already covered. Kindred spread books of the same content are those of a **Werner** (Leonhard Werner, *Seelentrost,* Nürnberg 1657 [and] 1670), **Selnecker,** *Tröstliche schöne Sprüche aus heiliger Schrift* (1560); **Andreas Musculus,** *Unterrichtung von* [*Sterben*] and *Trostbüchlein* (1576 and 1585); **Bruno** (†1575 as Diakonusprediger in Zittau) with his *Sterbenskunst;* **Martin Molius** (Rector in [Görlitz], †1611) with his *Sterbenskunst* (1597).

Prayer-books — which soon in household-devotions and personal devotions found wide circulation — begin with **Habermann's *Christliche Gebete*,** a book that first appeared in 1567 at Wittenberg. Only, roughly, Luther's *Betbüchlein* (1565 by Otto and 1571 by Treuer) and Schröder's (Joachim, Rostock) *Betbüchlein über die Haustafel* (1557), that already before spread Cölius (†1548 at Döbeln), that of an anonymous of 1548, **Erasmus Sarcerius** (b. 1501 in Annaberg, †1559 in Magdeburg) with his *Hausbuch* (1553) prepared it; but none has, like Habermann — very soon in connexion with Mathesius's *Hausgebetlein* (from 1586, especially from 1592) — found such a spread. It became the occasion for many prayer-books, which in part drew from it, in part built prayers after its model, or also bound its prayers with older ones. So there appeared in 1589 the *Betbuch* on the initiative of the Saxon Elector Christian I; in 1600 the *Betbüchlein* of Monsinger von Frundeck, and that of **Georg Cystbenius** (†1596 in Magdeburg).

House- and private devotion was served by the books of **Cyriacus Spangenberg,** the *Betbüchlein* of **Simon Musäus** (†1576 as Dean in Mansfeld, 1567), of **Andreas Musculus** (1570), of **Andreas Fabricius** (†1577 as Pastor in Eisleben, 1586), of **Andreas Pancratius** (1584 pastor at [Hof]) — the *Haus- und Kirchenbuch;* of **Philipp Kegel** (†1611 as a private man in Lübeck) — *Zwölf geistliche Andachten* (from 1596). Confession and the Supper were especially served by **Weller** (†1572 in Freiberg) — *Bericht, warum man oft und gern zum heiligen Sakrament gehen soll* (1562), and **Caspar Bienemann's** (†1591 as Generalsuperintendent in Altenburg) little book (from 1586); out of this soon the prayers went over into other books — as into Habermann and its kind.

Of edifying *Betrachtungen,* the greater works of an **Andreas Musculus,** a **Michael Neander** (†1595 in Alfeld, *Menschenspiegel,* from 1560), a **Michael Saxo,** and at the end of the age of a **Moller,** found much reception — as also the tracts of **Stephan Praetorius.** In the exposition of Scripture there stand above all **Christoph Fischer** and **Nikolaus Selnecker,** who — as for example in his Psalm-exposition — at once excellently taught praying. Alongside stands **Weller** with the exposition of Job 1–12 (1568) going onto the inner life. The Passion-history was expounded with prayers by **Johann Tettelbach** (†at Burglengenfeld, *Passional,* 1593). At the end of the age, none speaks so eloquently of the Last Things as **Nikolai** in his *Freudenspiegel* (1599).

### §3. The Piety of the Age — a Portrait Gallery

*Source: I b) — Zeit 1550–1600 — §2 continued, pp. 88–92. Große moves from the book-trade catalogue to the biographical-piety portraits — of princes and educated laity — that give the flesh to the book-trade statistics.*

If only these books, the most important out of the great mass then arising, found readers, [there was a large and pious lay audience]. Some few examples further of the pious educated laity, especially of the princes:

**Duke August of Saxony** read before the regular church-going Luther's *Postille,* used the holy Supper often, prayed daily his morning- and evening-blessings — most out of the Psalter. The Scripture and Luther's works he read again and again. His consort, **"Mother Anna,"** received the witness of a zealous pray-er, who had three prayer-hours a day in her women's chamber (in which too one or another must read in the Bible). She dispensed alms, gathered them from court-officials, herself visited the sick and the women in childbed.

**Joachim Ernst of Anhalt** (†1586) gladly spoke at table over the sermon and the like, and was accustomed to stand before the table with his whole court-household and strike up a hymn. To his servants he was a pastor, who himself read to them out of Scripture or spiritual books. **Sigismund August of Mecklenburg** (†1600) loved above all the Psalter. His funeral-preacher, Lukas Bacmeister, tells: he had seen his own hand-copy, in which he had marked the Psalms he had used.

Now the piety of the educated leaders witnesses to an upright piety of the leaders of the folk.¹¹⁸ But what of the piety of those who let themselves be influenced by these leaders? Here too glorious examples out of all educated estates can be brought forward. Concerning the ones from the highest circles, some illustrate this: at the head of the folk stood in many cases princes who lived and moved in their Lutheran faith, and who out of hearts' need gladly dedicated their time to edification. **August of Saxony** and **Mother Anna** — already treated. Another: from the other Thuringian poet **Cyriacus Schneegaß,** pastor at Friedrichroda (†1597), it is reported that he did not let himself become entangled in the doctrinal disputes of his time but, as a faithful pastor, lived wholly for his congregation — in which he especially cultivated God's Word and singing. He was pastoral-bonded to single members by accompanying their joys and sufferings with his hymns. How the legal-scholar **Steuerlein,** the town-*Schultheiß* at Wasungen (†1613), must have submerged himself in the Bible — since he set it wholly-and-entirely in German rhymes!

These are only a few examples. They give witness enough of an upright piety of the educated leaders of the folk.

Yet, on the other side, one must not overlook the many complaints of the pious — chiefly the spiritual leaders — over the outward-only Lutheran-Christian state of the multitude. It is by the Lord's own prophecy that in all ages it stands plain: the narrow way was never the way of the multitude. If we consider that in the lower classes of the population we can still count only a very small outward measure of formation, then it is alone the might of the Gospel that shows its power precisely there — in that religion becomes the atmosphere and ground of the whole folk-life. It permeated the same, all its institutions; and even the great heap was not untouched by this might. The evangelical-churchly practice of the house formed itself then. Attendance at the public divine service became general. The families bought themselves their church-pews. The pious Lutheran took part twice on the Sunday at the church, and industriously visited the still-existent (in part daily) weekly divine services. The holy Supper was celebrated regularly, mostly four times a year. Before all more important undertakings — before weddings, at office-installations, before journeys, and so on — one went to the Lord's Table.

A full household-divine-service, as the edification-books required and fostered, was the mark of an exemplary-pious house; and of these there were enough in city and country. But morning- and evening-blessing and grace-at-meat became in every family in city and country the custom. In the country these devotions could indeed for the most part consist only of one of the by-heart-learned hymns and of a prayer — often only in the Lord's Prayer. Hymn-books, in this and in the following century, in country-churches especially, did not exist for churchly use. Through singing and re-singing, through singing-out-in-front by the school-teachers, only a small number of kernel-hymns went into flesh and blood of the folk. Also Holy Scripture was, at its still dear price, not yet in the hands of all. Bible-knowledge had for the most part to be limited to the pieces known from the pericopes. In the higher estates one possessed the Bible more generally in the houses, and read it there industriously. Elector August read four weeks before his death the whole Scripture once more through. Free prayer in the house the layman did not yet know. Whoever could read joined to Habermann or similar books. For further edification on Sunday, the educated used the sermons in the postillen since Luther, or fed his inner life on [the daily hymn-verses].¹¹⁹

### §4. Christoph Fischer (1520–1600)

*Source: I b) — Zeit 1550–1600 — §3, pp. 91–96. The Vogtland-born Jüterbog-Schmalkalden-Meiningen-Celle-Halberstadt-Celle preacher — one of the "most fruitful" devotional writers of the immediately-post-Reformation age, and author of the Passionslied "Wir danken Dir, HErr JEsu Christ."*

**Christoph Fischer** (Vischer) was born in the Bohemian mining-town of **Joachimsthal in the Vogtland**¹²⁰ and was in the year 1541 ordained at Jüterbog — which belonged to the Wittenberg Consistorial-district — as evangelical pastor. In the autumn of 1555 he came on Melanchthon's recommendation to the place of Caspar Aquila at Schmalkalden, as court-preacher to Prince Georg Ernst and Superintendent of the County of Henneberg. Here he carried the Reformation through by a great visitation and removed the last of the monks. Earnestly he proceeded against inactive pastors in cities and villages. Therefrom grew for him many Anfechtungen. On 7 November 1571 he became pastor and Hennebergish Superintendent in Meiningen, and then came in 1574 as Court-preacher to Celle. New entanglements over his 1573 Leipzig-issued *Exposition of the Five Chief-Parts of the Holy Catechism* forced him in 1577 to accept a call as Chief-Pastor at St. Mary's in Halberstadt. In 1588 he returned again to Celle as Generalsuperintendent and Visitator of the Principality of Lüneburg. Here he died — after six-and-fifty years' preaching-labour — on **22 January 1600.**

He is also the author of the plain and yet so-deep Passion-hymn **"Wir danken Dir, HErr JEsu Christ,"**¹²¹ one of the most fruitful devotional writers of the immediately-post-Reformation age. His fruits [continue to serve devotion today]:

**Christoph Fischer, Ostergesänge** — the harmoniously-arranged post-resurrection-hymnic material — set together from his 1565 Schmalkalden Postille [wrongly ascribed], the Cottbus 1872 print of G. Sinhuber, 404 pp, 20°, 31 lines to the page, thin paper.

The [contents] are the harmoniously-ordered Easter-preaching of Fischer, [treating in twenty-Ostergesänge the whole Easter-tide] from the Resurrection through the Ascension-onward. After the introduction, the sermons are named in 2–5 parts. These are then developed, expounding the Scripture piece by piece. In this, other thoughts contained in the several Scripture-words are also drawn in. The sermons are homilies. — Their age they betray in their language. This has the Lutheran boldness and often the earthy folk-speech — as, for example, is called upon a plenteous list of Volksbild-terms.

The style is often full of *élan,* even in short sentences. [Example, 2nd sermon, p. 280:] "*Where is Nebuchadnezzar? Where is [Alexander the Great]? — They are all gone down. But he — Christ — is eternal. He distributes eternal goods, heavenly, divine riches; of his kingdom is no end. He lends spiritual and eternally-abiding fiefs, which do not fall away — which even the vassals at the eternal life preserve.*"

Also this is answering to the time: that in nearly every sermon of Fischer's zealously against the Papists — Pope: bear-wolf, arch-soul-murderer, "the accursed" — or against the *Schwärmer* (Enthusiasts, especially the Schwenkfeldians) is proceeded; or other particular distresses of the time are scourged. Schwenkfeld he often calls **"Stinkfeld."**¹²² He reports that the pastors are mocked (7th sermon, p. 156); that some for one, two, three years do not come to absolution (9th sermon, p. 205). That the devil, "the wretched devil," with his Anfechtungen is himself more often named — even instead of sin — is also spoken out of the spirit of the age. But herein there lies at the same time something which raises it above the age: the blessed [comfort], the mighty comfort, the healing warning that (as the close of each sermon adds) they will give, is to be brought with pointing to the fact that the God-child has to fight not only with flesh and blood but with the princes and mighty ones who rule in the darkness of this world — and that for this the greatest comfort lies in the victory of Christ over the devil. The matter is thus rightly touched — even if we, perhaps, are too little used, in the current edifying preaching-manner, to have the personal *Urgrund* of the Evil at once unveiled. So doctrinal-instructive also in single passages — for example, in the 18th sermon, of the Holy Spirit — is spoken; yet the sermons will nothing other than to drive Christ, for the comfort of the down-cast hearts. Therefore any furthered Christian today can use them to blessing, especially in Easter-tide. Many a fine application of the use he also finds out of the single Easter-histories. The sins and Anfechtungen of Fischer's time remain at bottom always the same.

What Beck says of Fischer's language — *"that it often borders on rudeness, at times also is spelling and playful"* — we found above confirmed as well; but so too the point that, like his other edifying writings, these sermons too bear witness of a rich spirit, a great Scripture-knowledge, a heartily-loving sharing in the Church's distress.

*Note: Lochner has in his Osterbuch especially made use of Fischer.*

### §5. Stephan Praetorius (1536–1603) — the 32 Little Tracts

### §6. Martin Moller (1547–1606)

*Source: I b) — Zeit 1550–1600 — §5, pp. 100–106. TOC-read correction: my earlier read of "Andreas Musculus" was OCR-error; biographical content is unmistakably **Martin Moller** — Görlitz Oberpfarrer, hymnist of "Nimm von uns, HErr, du treuer Gott" and "Ach Gott, wie manches Herzeleid," author of the *Praxis evangeliorum, Meditationes sanctorum patrum, Soliloquia de Passione Jesu Christi,* and the *Mysterium magnum.*¹²⁵*

**Martin Moller** wrote his *Postille* — the ***Praxis evangeliorum*** — the practical treatment of the Sunday-and-feast-day Gospels, published 1601 after he had in 1600 become Oberpfarrer at Görlitz. Almost at once, in 1602, the Wittenberg Professor D. Salomo Geßner attacked him over several passages in it. Precisely because Moller had said on the Gospel of the 4th Sunday after Trinity (new edition III, p. 60 top): *"O, how easily many controversies would be settled, if only meekness dwelt in the teachers' hearts and the sharp heads could bear one another!"* — that did not suit Geßner. He even had the Silesians warned against this "irrupting Calvinist." M. Samuel Grosser, Rektor at Görlitz, defended him — on the contrary — for his *"edifying, Scripture-agreeable simplicity."* After this strife Moller was blinded by cataract, on 26 August 1605. So he let the sermon-texts be read out to him, that he might still preach for his congregation. Fierce kidney-pains soon consumed his life-force. On **2 March 1606** he went home.

To his own name-love his motto was: ***Memento mori*** ("Remember death!"). In the sacristy of the Peterskirche at Görlitz stands, under his portrait, the honorific *"theologus pacificus et practicus"* — the peace-making, practical Godlearned man. Moller is also to be named as a hymn-writer: **"Nimm von uns, HErr, du treuer Gott"** (a rendering of the hymn *"Aufer immensam, Deus, aufer iram"*), and **"Ach Gott, wie manches Herzeleid"** (free after Bernard's *"Jesu dulcis memoria"*), are the best known of his hymns.

Several of his devotional-books are newly issued. Despite the Latin titles (borrowed from Augustine), they contain healthy fare in exemplary-set contemplations. Thorough Scripture-basis, faithfulness of confession, clarity, heartiness, and popular-language are their marks.

**Moller, Praxis evangelica** — contemplations on the Gospels of the Sundays and feast-days (also of the apostle-days) for household use. Newly worked over and issued by the *Christlicher Verein im nördlichen Deutschland,* Eisleben (Paul Klöppel). Vol. 3, 1859, [and further volumes for the Sundays after Trinity and the apostle-days: All Saints, Mary's Conception, Paul's Conversion.] Since 1898, Moller's Gospel-sermons are also issued with fair type from Neukirchen bei Mörs — sold by the *Bookseller of the Erziehungsverein* for 6 M; in a knight-edition of Richard Kühne, Oberhausen, bound 6 M.

The highest worth of all his writings unquestionably possesses:

**Heilsame Betrachtung des Leidens und Sterbens JEsu Christi** ("Salutary Contemplation of the Suffering and Dying of Jesus Christ"), composed by Martin Moller, sometime Pastor primarius at Görlitz. Issued by Friedrich Wilhelm Bodemann, Pastor at Finkenwerder near Hanover. Cottbus, Ochmigke, now G. Sinhuber, 205 pp, 16°, medium-large type, [1856], boarded 1 M.

The contemplations had originally the title ***Soliloquia de Passione Jesu Christi,*** 1587. **Beck says** it is *"perhaps the most excellent thing that our evangelical Church has [in the field of devotional literature] to point out, with a small praying-through";*¹²⁶ and one can only accord to that. The book contains a praying contemplation of the Passion-history. Bodemann, the meritorious issuer, brings in his 8-page Preface as proofs of the significance of Moller's writing its many editions: to the first there follow 12 more — Stade 1682, 1695; Lüneburg 1717, 1723, 1756, 1788; Hamburg 1692; Lübeck 1713; Minden 1729 and 1740; a later edition, and one at Nürnberg 1776.

In 205 pp the little writing contains, in Bodemann's edition (after leaving off the preparatory contemplations on Isaiah 53, the Passover-lamb, and John 14–16), the **13 contemplations** on the synoptically-set text of the Passion-history. Each is divided by superscriptions into shorter or longer sections (94), of which each — after the brief laying-forth of the doctrine (*"cogitation"* after Beck) — gives the *application* of the same for the contemplating soul (*"meditation"*) and lets the so-warmed soul then speak with the Lord in prayer (*"contemplation"*). One notes in the writing that the "adoring" prayers are not made for others but come out of the heart, to go to hearts. In the contemplations the suffering before Herod is missing. — What was elsewhere said above (after Beck) of Moller's edifying books in general holds also here.

A second of Moller's edifying books, and one that reaches almost also to the *Mysterium magnum:*

**Mysterium magnum. Das große Geheimnis von der Vermählung Christi mit der glaubigen Gemeinde, dem heimlichen Weib, biblisch erbaulich ausgelegt von Martin Moller.** Issued by Karl August Thieme, Pastor in [Kittlitz]; Cottbus, Ochmigke (now G. Sinhuber). 271 pp, 16°. Boarded 80 pf.

It came out first in 1595. The manner of the contemplations here is not that they raise themselves after cogitation, meditation, contemplation into prayer; the doctrinal contemplations here go alongside the prayers that accompany them. In pp. IX–XV find themselves Moller's and the issuer's Prefaces. The issuer writes: *"Never has yet a little book come into his hand which — as this — laid forth the whole salvation-truth in so heart-loving, so clear and understandable, so concrete a form."* [The 14 chapters:] the great Lord and King, from whom the marriage proceeds (p. 17); the Bridegroom Jesus Christ (26); the Bride, the believing congregation (41); Time and Manner of the marriage (65); the Ceremony through God's Word in faith (71); the Bride-gift, the marriage-pledge — which is the Holy Spirit (90); the Morning-gift (109); the Bride-jewelry (123); the Bride-wooers (136); the Wedding-guests (153); the Bride-servants — the holy angels (185); the Bride-place (191); what Bride and Bridegroom have in common (201); Child-blessing (233); the Home-bringing and the wedding-garment. — It is the whole faith- and salvation-doctrine, but not dry — living, as it is the ground of the comfort and faith for the souls' blessedness.

**Manuale de morte** (originally 1593), reissued 1870 as *Heilsame Betrachtung, wie ein Christ aus Gottes Wort soll lernen christlich leben und selig sterben*, by Bodemann, Gotha, Perthes; 2nd ed., 197 pp, 38 lines, 80 pf.¹²⁷ In 9 chapters, question-and-answer form, moving from the uncertainty of the death-hour, through the three parts of a Christian life (repentance-with-knowledge, faith on the Reconciler, new obedience after the Ten Commandments), the six pieces of Christian life (God's Word, Baptism, Cross, Calling, Prayer), the right conduct in sickness, comfort in seldsome-thoughts in sickness, how one may despite death-fear yet blessedly die, comfort against Anfechtungen at the dying-hour, ten prayers-and-hymns for singing and sighing and verses for prayer at the death-hour, and closing with the Description of the Last Things. Note: An additional Moller *Heilige Sterbekunst — Gebetbüchlein für Geistliche, Diakonen, und Christen insgemein* — reissued Bertelsmann Gütersloh 1858 — is now out of print.

### §7. Johann Habermann (1516–1590)

*Source: I b) — Zeit 1550–1600 — §6, pp. 106–122. The single most-influential Lutheran *Betbüchlein*-author of the sixteenth century, whose 1567 Wittenberg prayer-book runs through the whole seventeenth-and-eighteenth-century Lutheran household as one of the two authoritative prayer-books alongside Luther's own.*

**Johann Habermann** (or Avenarius)¹²⁸ was born at Eger on 10 August 1516 — whence he calls himself in his writings *Egranus.* His father Lorenz Habermann was there a townsman and shop-keeper. Of his early formation-course nothing is known.

In 1540 he was received by the Bishop **Weigand von Redwitz** at Bamberg — a zealous opponent of the Reformation — in the Bamberg *Komturhaus* into the **Knightly Cross-Order with the Red Star,** which then took care of pastoral labour and of the sick-service and possessed several estates in Bohemia. He served the Order for a short time. Soon, between 1540 and 1542, he must have turned to the Gospel; for from 1542 he was, in strikingly quick succession, evangelical preacher in Electoral Saxony. The first place he filled as Deacon at Elsterberg and Pastor at Sohndorf.¹²⁹

Habermann's chief significance for the Reformation age lies not in his teaching or his controversy-tracts (of which he wrote several — a Confession-and-Trostbüchlein Leipzig 1577, the Falkenau 1569 dedication; and the *Vita Christi* of Christ's life in 150 contemplations with shorter prayers). No — his significance lies in his ***Christliche Gebete für allerlei Not und Stände* der ganzen Christenheit** — which appeared for the first time at Wittenberg 1567:

> ***Christliche Gebett für allerlei Noth und Stände der ganzen Christenheit außgetheilet auff alle Tage in der Wochen sampt gemeinen Danksagungen. — Wittenberg, 1567.***

With this prayer-book Habermann steered a new course. New, in comparison with Luther's morning- and evening-blessings, and with the sparse collections of Cölius, Marbestius, Linck (*Feuerzeug christlicher Andacht,* 1568), was **the multiplication of the prayers for the various life-situations,** and new was **the ordering of the particular prayers to the prayers of the several week-days.** In the original editions the prayers for various matters are, in Part I, distributed after an inner connexion over the several days. Between the morning- and evening-prayers are set so-many-and-so-many determined prayers — Sunday, Monday, [and following].

Sunday's prayers concern forgiveness of sins, the Church, preparation of the soul for the blessed-hearing of God's Word, prayer when one goes to church, for the preachers, for the hearers, against false teachers and sects. Monday, after thanksgiving for the redemption, follow prayers for the right faith, for the Kingdom of God, and — as at the beginning of the work-week — for the authorities, for the subjects. Corresponding to Sunday's prayer against the sects is Monday's prayer against the enemies of Christendom. Tuesday, after thanksgiving for the salvation, contains a petition for firm hope and for the following of the Christ, then prayers for married couples and for the youth — since Tuesday is the old **wedding-day** already in Israel.¹³⁰

*[Wednesday through Saturday continue in similar fashion, with intercessions for pregnant women; and on Saturday, at the close of the week, a prayer for a blessed end.]*

The circulation of the book is astonishing: from the Wittenberg 1567 first-edition it went into repeated editions, especially through the Nürnberg book-trade, and by 1655, 1658, 1684 into the Sulzbach imprint, and by 1734 into further editions. In our own age Löhe has newly issued it — arranged, as it stood, distributed over the days of the week. Gosack says it is *"the most widely-spread of all the older prayer-books after Luther,"* and puts its Nürnberg edition-count as high as 12 (though only 9 differing textually).

The style is throughout **biblical.** The prayer-book bears within itself the traces of its own age: the burdens of the Turkish threat, the reckoning with the plague, the *Endzeit* consciousness, the mission-thought.¹³¹ The prayers are simple, not scholastic; the *Erbauungshöhe* (edifying height) is such that the pious layman can easily be raised. This character grounds Habermann's tremendous, unique significance.

**The Habermann-Gebetbuch has its own history**: from all the older prayer-books it was the most widely spread. Already in a Strasbourg [edition, ~1577] the printer writes that these prayers *"are held in high esteem by pious and zealous Christians, and have been used practically for the edification of souls. Yea, I have experienced that even among the enemies of the evangelical light many awakened and burdened hearts have found themselves who have used the present Betbüchlein for advancement in the fear of God, for the right knowledge and strengthening of their faith and prayer."*

*[Habermann Betbüchlein continues to be treated in Große's account through pp. 116–122, covering the many editions, the eucharistic prayers, the confession-and-communion prayers of Embden (†1650 at Braunschweig), the 1590 Hamburg prayer-book, the Sigismund Scherer 1639 Lüneburg Beichtgebet, and the still-broader Reformation-age Ehe-devotion. Bibliographical fullness is compressed here; the substantive point stands: **Habermann's Betbüchlein became the single most-influential Lutheran layman's prayer-book of the sixteenth century, alongside Luther's own.**]*

### §8. Betbuch Christian I. (1589)

*Source: I b) — Zeit 1550–1600 — §7, pp. 122–124. The prayer-book of Elector Christian I of Saxony (1589) — after Habermann the most substantial post-Reformation Lutheran prayer-book, compiled from Habermann + patristic + Reformation sources.*

After Habermann's prayer-book had once appeared, a series of similar books was composed. Seldom did one try oneself in independent prayers. Mostly one gathered them from Habermann and from the older-available books and set them in new arrangements. From his time on, the **prayer-collections out of the treasury of the Church** begin generally. A prayer-book of that age, the richest in content, is the **Betbuch which appeared at the initiative of the Elector Christian I** in 1589.¹³³ It gathers the best that had until then been brought forth in this field, and has taken nearly all of Habermann up into itself. The book — so precious for the history of prayer-literature as for the churchly edification — was newly issued in 1853:

**Betbuch Christian des Ersten, Herzogs und Kurfürsten zu Sachsen, vom Jahre 1589. Mit Gebeten des Kurfürsten August von Sachsen.** Newly issued by Johann Konrad Irmischer, evangelical-Lutheran Pastor. Erlangen, Andreas Deichert, 1853. [Page count: ~180 pp], 32 lines, wider type. Unbound 1 M.

The preface gives exactly the original title and briefly the content of the book, and the character of the prayers: **"The prayers themselves are in their content biblically-confession-rich and faithful, and therefore also doctrinal, mighty and unctuous. They are just as fit to further the knowledge of the Christian [faith] as [they are to nourish the inner life]."**

The book's contents are then given [in six parts]: **1.** the 14 morning- and evening-blessings on all the days of the week from Habermann; **2.** 24 shorter and longer Segen, one of them from Augustine, one from Kurfürst August; **3.** 65 prayers of the holy patriarchs, kings, prophets, apostles, and other blessed men and women [drawn out of Scripture — the canonical and apocryphal Old and New Testament books, and the Psalter]; **4.** 42 prayers for particular estates, [ordered after the Catechism]. Of these 18, 6, 19, 20, 22–26, 35, 41 are from Habermann; 5 are from Augustine; 40 are from Luther (for example, that at his death). **5.** 85 prayers for all the need of the whole of Christendom, ordered after the [petitions of the Lord's Prayer]: 1 [is from Melanchthon, 18 or so from the older Christian tradition], 63 [drawn from Ambrose and other patristic sources]. **6.** With 18 numbers, three sorts: [a] the holy Lord's Prayer set out prayer-wise — paraphrases of the Catechism sacraments, travel- and weather-prayers; [b] Melanchthon's exposition of the Lord's Prayer; [c] contains the "short, plain contemplation and exposition of the suffering and dying of Christ after the order of the four evangelists," in four passages by night and by day, closed with a longer Passion-prayer.

### §9. Wolfgang Münsinger von Frundeck (†1588)

*Source: I b) — Zeit 1550–1600 — §8, pp. 124–125.*

Alongside the Betbuch Christian I stands the Betbüchlein of **Wolfgang Münsinger von Frundeck.**¹³⁴ [Already in 1684 the Frundeck-Betbüchlein went into revised editions]; Münsinger von Frundeck, who was assessor at the Imperial Chamber-Court and — in 1556 — Kanzler at Wolfenbüttel, died as a private man at [Alsleben] on 3 May 1588.

The learned jurist gathered from the prayer-treasury of Luther and of the evangelical Church, as well as from the Church Fathers, [the prayers]. He also took up Melanchthon's paraphrase of the Lord's Prayer, as well as a register of the prayers contained in the Scripture, and an instruction on how to use the Psalms in various life-situations — as one finds it later also, for example, in Gerhard's *Geistliches Kleinod.*

From his Betbüchlein much has been taken into the prayer-collections of our own time: in Löhe's *Samenkörner* No. 283 (morning-prayer on Saturday), 244 (for the service of the holy angels). Of these, No. 283 is already found in the Prayer-book of Elector Johann Friedrich of 1570 (perhaps 1557), whence Münsinger perhaps has it. In the *Allgemeines lutherisches Gebetbuch* he is often, and with 40 prayers, represented in the *Evangelisches Gebetbuch* (Berlin).

### §10. Michael Saxo (Sarö, ~1530–1600)

*Source: I b) — Zeit 1550–1600 — §9, pp. 125–132. The Remda Kantor-turned-preacher whose *Arcana annuli pronubi* — "the Mystery of the Marriage-Ring" — became a paradigm of marriage-symbolic devotional literature.*

**Michael Saxo** (Sarö) is first known¹³⁵ as *Kantor* at Remda in 1561; then in 1562 as *Stadtschreiber* at Egeln; in 1568 as school-teacher at Remda. In 1569 he was here — against his will — called to the preaching-office. From 1569 he was, for more than 25 years, [pastor at Remda] up to his death.

His writings have this peculiarity: that he sought — chiefly by *rhetorical* means — to hold the reader in tension. Along with his Christian discourse, one has the impression from all his writings that his chief aim is to bring [the theological content] not so much in the language of the school, as rather **in the language of the everyday** — [to offer] the heavenly spiritual gifts in the shell of naturalistic speech. This he does well; even at times, it must be admitted, further than the matter warrants, and thereby forfeits weight.

Among his many writings — *Christliches Zeitvertreib* or *Geistliches Rätselbuch* (Leipzig, 1616–1619, 1655), in which the holy things are treated more for pastime and for banter-questions in outward manner — the simplicity of his time hardly took offence.¹³⁶ Of others, the first — the *geistliche Reisegespräche* (Nürnberg, 1581) and *Trostbüchlein* (1867 newly issued) — deserve mention. Especially his ***Arcana annuli pronubi*** — in 1588 first out, then 1592 [enlarged] — as the preface reports.

**Arcana annuli pronubi** — *"Mystery of the Marriage-Ring, opened by 24 chapters. What should be drawn from it for teaching and comfort, admonition, by all who are married or are about to be — expounded out of God's Word and confirmed with many notable [images], comparisons, and *arcana naturae* — for the third time enlarged (1594), with a register bettered and made easier to read from that of Elias Sarcerius. Newly issued at Gottfried Zech, Nürnberg, now at [Bertelsmann,] Gütersloh — "newly again brought to light for use." 214 pp, 8°, 33 lines, medium type, 1.60 M.

The volume is again divided into 24 chapters and contains all that the bride-groom and suitor is to consider for the Christian married life, in connexion with the meaning of the marriage-ring. The 24th chapter is a sermon of **Cyriacus Spangenberg** on Titus 2:3–5, *"Virtue-Ring of all Christian [wives]"* — welded together out of eight-fold noble metals. Page 129 gives, out of a sermon by the Roman parish-priest of Wittenberg-time Agricola at Spalt, [an appendix]; and closes with an alphabetical subject-register. The book is rich in examples, histories, comparisons, rhymed-sayings, sayings, and citations from the Fathers, and from the learned of his time, in Latin and in the translation. After nearly every chapter follow histories or examples or comparisons under particular superscription; from ch. 14 on also a prayer.

Its peculiarity can be seen from the chapter-titles. E.g.:
- Ch. 5: that the marriage-ring is, of all bodily good deeds of God, the greatest — as gold is the best metal.
- Ch. 6: the priority of the man, and the discipline and praise of the woman, drawn from the comparison of gold and silver.
- Ch. 7: how many persons at once should marry — drawn from the unity of the ring.
- Ch. 9: of the hard, unloosable bond of marriage — from the fact that the ring goes so tightly on the finger.
- Ch. 11: that married love should proceed from the heart — from the *ring-finger* on which the ring is set.
- Ch. 15: that every one should take his equal — from the fitting width of the ring.
- Ch. 17: that the inclination of one toward the other is God's creation and ordering — as gold is drawn toward ivory.
- Ch. 19: that the married estate is a medicine against evil lusts — as gold serves against leprosy.
- Ch. 22: on industriousness and thrift in household economy — drawn from the manner of the Indian ants, that guard the gold there.

The little book will today serve less to actual edification than rather to the knowledge of the peculiarity of the second half of the sixteenth century — in part also in its devotional-literature. For the preacher, however, it gives material in *Hülle und Fülle* for marriage-addresses and household-estate-sermons. Löhe himself put it to use.¹³⁷

### §11. Cyriacus Spangenberg (1528–1604)

*Source: I b) — Zeit 1550–1600 — §10, pp. 128–130. The Mansfeld reformer, chronicler, and hymnist whose *Cithara Lutheri* (Wittenberg, 1569) was the first sustained devotional exposition of Luther's hymns.*

**Cyriacus Spangenberg**¹³⁸ — son of the reformer Johann Spangenberg (†1550 in Eisleben, already treated above at the close of §7-catalogue), and one of the most industrious devotional writers of the Mansfeld-Saxon second Reformation-generation. In 1566 Graf Volrad zu Mansfeld called him from [Nordhausen] as *Superintendent* of the county of Mansfeld. As a determined adherent of Flacius Illyricus in the intra-Lutheran controversies of the 1570s, and having irritated Graf Ernst zu Mansfeld (who took the opposing party in the controversies), he was forced in 1577 — in the disguise of a peasant — to flee. For some time he remained in Mansfeld, protected by Graf Volrad and supported with a yearly stipend of [—] thalers. When, as a consequence of a colloquy of 9 September 1577 at Sangerhausen, [Graf] Volrad — his own protector — was likewise banished from the county, Spangenberg turned to [Sondershausen], where the latter [Volrad] had already on 20 July 1578 [entrusted himself to] the Count of [Schwarzburg]. The nobles Wilhelm von Schach and [X] von Görtz then called him to **Schlitz on the Fulda.** After two years of faithful office-labour, he had to yield here too on the death of his patrons. His prayer in this distress remained the **25th Psalm,** which he set into the hymn "*Ich hebe mein Herz zu Dir, o HErr, mich sehne ich*."¹³⁹ Through the mediation of Landgraf Wilhelm of Hesse he found in the Reformed town of Vacha on the Werra a preacher's place, though he had here — as a refugee — to nourish himself and his own children through much labour without office or bread. But he remained in his God firm, as his letters and writings of that time prove. The 127th [Psalm] he experienced upon himself. His children — six sons and two daughters — also found their good provision. Once more he had to change his dwelling-place, driven from the town-officials. In 1595 he betook himself, on an invitation of the brother-son of Graf Volrad, of [Peter] Ernst — who as *Canonicus* lived in Strasbourg — and died on **18 February 1604,** after he had spent nine more years there, lovingly supported by him with his wife.

As a faithful pupil of Luther, he wrote an exposition of Luther's hymns — the ***Cithara Lutheri*** — which grants him rich comfort and doctrine, in 76 sermons.¹⁴⁰ But he also sang himself in the spirit and gift; in 1568 he issued the *Eisleben Gesangbüchlein* (published earlier by his father) in an enlarged edition, in 1582 [issued] "Psalms and other hymns on the Psalter and other hymns of Holy Scripture." From him are, for example, the hymns: *"Nach dir, o HErr, verlange ich"* [*"After thee, O Lord, do I long"*], and others.¹⁴¹

*[Große continues with maxims and edifying sayings drawn from Spangenberg's writings, in the fashion of his own biographical portraits — including the sayings-numbered-52, 60, and similar folk-proverbs:¹⁴²]*

- **Sayings 3, 52:** *"With the many colour-glowing hymn-sayings for a single day one only makes people over-full and tired."*
- **Saying 60:** *"What burns thee not, thou needest not to quench."*

### §12. Tilemann Hesshusius (1527–1588)

*Source: I b) — Zeit 1550–1600 — §11, pp. 130–133. The Gnesio-Lutheran controversialist and preacher, forced-through-controversy-into-many-cities-of-office, defender of the *Formula of Concord.**

**Tilemann Hesshusius**¹⁴³ was born on 3 November 1527 at Wesel. After studies at various universities — including Wittenberg — and after journeys through France, England, Denmark, and Germany, he became in 1550 Magister and in 1553 Doctor at Wittenberg (the latter [degree taken] from Goslar). At Goslar he worked from 1552 as Superintendent. In 1556 he was, however, on account of the church-orderings he wished to introduce, dismissed. He went to Magdeburg and soon after obtained at Rostock a theological professorship and the pastoral office at St. James. He inveighed against the old leaven, against all churchly abuse — chiefly against the disturbing Sunday-weddings. Thereby it came to a longer conflict between the Rat and the city-clergy, in which Hesshusius left Rostock and went to Wittenberg in 1559.

Through Melanchthon's mediation he came to the *Generalsuperintendentur* and the first theological professorship at Heidelberg. Through the conflicts against the Calvinist Elector Friedrich III and the Calvinist Deacon Klebitz, he was also there set out of office. In 1560 he went from thence to Magdeburg to St. John's, and in 1561 took over the Superintendentur. His opposition against an edict of the Lüneburg Circle-Diet — which forbade all polemic against the Crypto-Calvinists on the pulpit — brought him for the 4th [time] office-dismissal. On 22 October 1562 he was banished from the city and returned to Wesel. Here he lost his wife by death. Through a writing on the Pope as Antichrist, he was also here dismissed by the Duke of Jülich and had to leave his motherless children in the city in bitter winter-cold. Refused entry at Strasbourg, he received in 1565 a call to the court-preachership of Graf Wolfgang [von Zweibrücken]. Under him he found a rest until he was invited to the court of Kurfürst August of Saxony — where he came to a firm inward strife over the Crypto-Calvinist tendencies — and where he was again forced to move.

He came in 1569 to Prussia, and was in 1571 as court-preacher and professor at Königsberg. Here he lived four years. In 1577 he received a call to Helmstedt, where he took over the professorship. The *Formula of Concord* — which had appeared that same year — he was completely in accord with, and despite all attempts of 1582 and 1583 to force him to withdraw his subscription, he did not weaken. He held firmly to it, and required the re-establishment of the [Wittenberg-Concord] that the Formula of 1577 demanded.

He died on **25 September 1588.** He was a rough but honest man. He confesses in his testament in true humility that he had done in the office of the ministry less than he had been rightly owing, that he should have punished the [gross] sinners still more harshly and should have refuted the sect-spirits more thoroughly. Against that, he was assured — as far as his exile-persecutions were concerned — that he had led the congregation of God [into all truth] and pure and healthy doctrine. Not bitterness, but overheightened conscientiousness, made him so untamable — as also his rough theological writings, especially expositions [teach].¹⁴⁴

His sermons: Though he preaches in the sense of his age dogmatically, [his writings are yet] also for the people [understandable]. Some of his sermons — which appeared 1581, 1594, and 1595 — are available in new editions, and can lead the reflective reader into the truths [of the Lutheran faith] and at the same time warm the heart:

**1. Von dem Unvermögen menschlicher Kräfte in denen Sachen, so das ewige Leben betreffen** — Four Sermons. With a Preface by W. Hübener. Dresden, Heinrich Naumann (now at Joh. Herrmann in Zwickau), 1881. Kl. 8°, VII + 158 pp. Cloth 1.80 M.

**2. Zehn Predigten von der Rechtfertigung des Sünders vor Gott** — by Dr. Tilemann Hesshusius. The 1568 edition reprinted unchanged. St. Louis (Mo.) and Leipzig, Concordia-Verlag¹⁴⁵ (Leipzig, Ernst), 1876. 8°, [size].

Here appears, as preface, the dedicatory letter of 16 February 1568 to Wolfgang, Pfalzgrafen [von Zweibrücken], in old-fashioned broad language. Because the doctrine of Justification is so often misunderstood, so it is precisely because [of that] worth to be praised more mightily. The 1st sermon (pp. 20–61) [treats] how the words *Law, Sin, Righteousness, to justify, Grace, and Faith* are to be understood. The 2nd: how the doctrine of the Justification of the Sinner before God is to be grasped in certain Chief-Points — all sinners — God out of grace, Jesus the Mediator — Faith the means.

## Section II a) The Time from 1600 to the End of the Thirty Years' War (Lutheran Orthodoxy)

*Central Point: Greater edifying books, especially Arndt's Wahres Christentum and its love.*

### §1. General Overview

*Source: II a) §1, pp. 133–136.*

The first half of the seventeenth century multiplies the religious folk-literature. The most important causes that worked toward it were **the fearful tribulation and the nameless distress** that the terrible Thirty Years' Religion-War brought over Germany, and over the Evangelical-Lutheran (as well as the Reformed) Church standing in the highest danger. The heaviest outward and inward *Anfechtung* taught to attend to the Word. That was the means in the hand of the Lord — who, by too great an emphasis on pure doctrine, threatened to externalise, and yet [in this same age] preserved the true life, indeed awakened new life. The war and its birth-pangs before and after ripened an earnest, deeply-rooting life-direction. In the school of the cross ripened an inner and yet strong faith. It awoke an overflowing edifying literature alongside the fulness of spiritual hymns of that age, which — with it and next to it — build the fairest fruits out of that tear-sowing. This literature further found in those days a ground made ready by tribulation in the folk. The speech, however — which by the poet-societies of that age was cleansed (though also, to be sure, again spoiled) — shows itself also in this [devotional literature] purer and more folk-familiar than elsewhere; although the learned writers cannot always quite hide the awkwardness of their one-sidedly on the schools formed language, chiefly in the edifying contemplations that appeared. The prayers which are given in books hold themselves mostly the purest, since they are — in general — the immediate outpouring of hearts which, in single-minded piety, [pray to God].

The edifying literature runs in this age in two paths, drawn upon the ground of the Evangelical-Lutheran Orthodoxy proper. In her, the Church — which alongside the Roman-Catholic and (chiefly) the Jesuit theology on the Roman side, and against the Reformed on the other side, in the [after-work] of the Concord-Book brought to a certain systematic close, had with strong power to cultivate and work over the *acquisitions of the fathers* — had to attempt to make what she had thereby gained *mouth-ready* for the faith-life of the folk and the personal life of the several Christians; and it continues here in her folk-literature immediately the post-Reformation of the earlier age. Here are the most significant names: a **Nikolai, Dedeken, and Statius in Hamburg**;¹⁴⁶ a **Nikolaus Hunnius in Lübeck** (the revised new edition of his *Epitome credendorum,* or Content of Christian Doctrine, so much as a Christian must know and believe of it for his soul's salvation, drawn out of God's Word, 1870, Nördlingen, is now out of print); a **Siegmund Schererz** (†1639 as Superintendent at Lüneburg); a **Georg Zeämann** at Stralsund (†1638 as Superintendent there); and a **Johannes Rosner** from the March [of Brandenburg — Berlin]; those of the well-known Silesians **Valerius Herberger, Johann Heermann,** and **Martin Hyller** (†1651 [his *güldenes Schatzkästlein,* which appeared again in 1863 at Stuttgart, is now out of print]); those of the great Saxons and Thuringians **Johann Gerhard, Salomon Glassius, Matthäus Meyfart;** those of the South-Germans as **Johann Saubert in Nürnberg** and **Bernhard Albrecht in Augsburg.**

But because in the edifying literature of the Lutheran Orthodoxy — despite all the kernel of personal piety and heart-depth that is found in her best representatives — the fresh life easily retreated, and the look for the true needs of the congregation was clouded, so **another circle sought to satisfy these more,** in that it — standing itself on the ground of Lutheran Orthodoxy — yet with utilisation of a *sound mysticism,* even from times before the Reformation, sought to work toward the expression of faith in a true sanctification-life. This circle **Arndt** led, and he influenced greatly the moderate representatives of Orthodoxy — such as a Gerhard. In each case he ruled him and his works.¹⁴⁷

The individual chapters that follow can only underline in outline how the whole edifying literature of the Thirty Years' War-time was nourished, and how the number of prayer-books grows ever more, and how the great collection-works begin — in which the prayers for the various life-situations are ordered and bound. As in that age the confession-hymn of Lutheranism yielded to the *testimony-hymn* of *churchly life,* so also the edifying literature turns more and more upon the meeting of all life-cases, and seeks to serve all single life-situations.

### §2. On the Piety in the First Half of the Seventeenth Century

*Source: II a) §2, pp. 136–140.*

The Thirty Years' War was a fearful judgement upon what the Reformation and the influence of the Church had left of unbelief and worldliness in the German people; and that still enough always remained — where the Gospel was preached, where evangelical doctrine and churchly ordering still had something on which to build (as, in the peace-periods, Church and household anew built); yea, that through the War (in spite of its being the most morality-destroying of all wars, some believed) — much spiritual life could not only not be annihilated, but in the through-it-founded tribulation-school first bloomed — proves also here **the might-of-victory of the Gospel.** Alongside deep dark shadow finds itself precisely here much bright light. The shadow, in the misery which the War brought with it, cannot be painted too dark: fearfully raged the War with the plague and the famine in its train; a third of the population was swept away, in some regions still more. The peasant, who up to 1618 could tend his field with comfort, had now to go into the field with weapons, in order to protect himself and his cattle. Ever-repeated troop-movements sucked him out, brought him and his own mistreatment and plunder. In this age princes were driven out, the authorities vanished, the visitations ceased, parish- and school-posts remained unfilled. In [Voigtland] there remained, of 1046 clergy, only 330. A pastor who returned to his village found there now only widows and maids. Nearly the life of all hymn-writers and edification-writers of those days shows persecution, hunger, plague, and death. One thinks, for example, of Herberger, Heermann, [von Schwenitz], Andreas Gryphius, Matthäus Apelles von Löwenstern, Andreas Tscherning, Adam Thebes — these all in Silesia. No wonder, when there the fear of God vanished, insolence, drunkenness, unchastity, cruelty gained the upper hand, and the meanest drives could move themselves openly. With the Thirty Years' War hung together too **the invasion of foreignerisms,** which had to work churchly-unfavourably, especially on the higher estates.

Alongside these dark clouds the light of the Gospel shone through, especially in the persons of the **faithful pastors and princely lay-Lutherans** who preserved the tradition. Große names a portrait-gallery: [Daniel Dilger] to Danzig-St-Kattharinen; [Rathmann] the Danzig pastor of St. Katharinen from 1626; [Michael Bland] at St. Katharinen from 1614; **Valentin Andreä** (1586–1654) in Württemberg — the *"living conscience of his land in that war-distress-time"* — through and through inwardly the [companion] of orthodox theology; **Johann Valentin Andreä** (born 1594 in [Herrenberg], †1658), spiritual father of the best pastors and lay-people of the following time, also an [influencer] on **Spener** himself; **Wolfgang Franz,** professor at Wittenberg (†1628); [in Danzig, the circle of Rathmann, Dilger, and Bland spreading Arndt's *Wahres Christentum* and *Paradiesgärtlein* with great zeal]; the well-known **Königsberg poet-school** of a **Simon Dach,** [Heinrich] Albert, [Robert] Robertthin, and others.

**Princes** of hearty piety: **Duke Philipp II of Pomerania** (†1618) — reported that he took God's Word for a light on all his ways, took the holy Supper especially earnestly with conscientious preparation, missed no service, prayed early and late industriously, and sang spiritual hymns and Psalms. Beside him is to be set **Georg II, Landgrave of Hessen-Darmstadt** (†1661), who read the Bible not less than 28 times through. He is surpassed by the well-known **Ernst der Fromme, Herzog of Sachsen-Gotha**¹⁴⁸ (born 1601, †1675) — a father of his fatherland, a through-and-through pure Christian as few. Already in the 11th year he was allowed — answering to his own longing — to receive the holy Supper for the first time. Even in war-tumult he did not omit his regular devotion-hours; his tent was as a Church, where the name of God was called upon. From the Scripture he read every morning, at home and on journeys; in it he sought light and comfort in all perplexities. His whole consolation rested in the experience of forgiveness of sins.

Various pious noblemen and their households are named: [Otto von Rüben; a colonel who prayed morning and evening; the professor **Johann Brunnemann** (jurist, †1672 in Frankfurt); the Wittenberg medical professor **Daniel Sennert** (†1637)] — each having his own devotional-writing (Herberger's constant hand-book; the *Vaterunser*-and-*Vaterunser*-morning-blessings). The court-preacher and Stift-preacher **[Johann Schedlin]** (†1658) prayed the Psalter *"as though he would pray through the heaven itself."* Of a certain Prime-Minister assessor of the Konsistorium in [Königstein]: *"He was in prayer fervent, so in his prayer-chamber, so in his house, which has been a Church of God, since one heard there daily [before and after] the meal — and morning and evening — sighing and hymns of praise and spiritual hymns."* [As often as] he came out of the Church, he began — for himself and the whole household — to speak with a comforting and spirit-full word from the sermon. Of Prof. Wilhelm Alard (†1645 at Krempe in Holstein) it is reported: *"He was a fervent pray-er, who at the sermons often for long hours in his heart meditated."* Further examples of true prayer-life find themselves in the biographies of the significant edification-writers of that age, which — so far as their material may still be gathered — will be treated later in the sequence of the places of their labour.

### §3. Philipp Nikolai (1556–1608)

*Source: II a) §3, pp. 140–147. The North-German Reformer, court-preacher, and hymnist, author of the two "Queen of Chorales" and "King of Chorales" — *Wachet auf, ruft uns die Stimme* and *Wie schön leuchtet der Morgenstern* — and of the *Freudenspiegel des ewigen Lebens* (1598/99), a paradigm-devotional book of the age of Lutheran Orthodoxy.*

#### The Life

**Philipp Nikolai**¹⁴⁹ was born on 10 August 1556 in Mengeringhausen in the then County of Waldeck. His father **Dietrich Rafflenböl** was there pastor and Dekan of the Waldeckish clergy. He had later taken the name of his father **Nikolaus** — the possessor of the estate *Kerle* in the County of Mark — as his surname; and then his son Nikolai [inherited it]. From Herdicke, in the [Countship of Mark], his father had been driven out for the introduction of the Lutheran Reformation. His mother was **Katharina,** daughter of the iron-smith Meyhan of Herdicke.

The child was early brought up in prayer and God's Word, and quickly accompanied the father to church, sought to do the like in child-fashion at home. The father — despite all the costs — let him study with three other sons. In 1568 he came to the *Lateinschule* at Kassel, where he especially cultivated music. In 1571 [he went] to the Gymnasium in Dortmund, into the school of the orator, poet, and musician **Friedrich Baurhaus.** In the autumn of 1572 or 1573 [he went] to the *Klosterschule* at Mühlhausen. After he had visited further schools as a *wandering scholar,* he came in 1575 to the University of Erfurt. Through Latin occasional-poems he earned the necessary money. The death of his mother — on 27 May [1575?] — brought him back to the parental house, where he supported his father in the ministry. With a scholarship from the Elector of Saxony he came in 1576 again to the University, and to Wittenberg, under **Leyser.** In 1579 he went with his younger brother Jeremias into the Waldeckish *Kloster* Wolfhardinghausen — an hour from home — to study further and from thence to support his father.

There Count **Wilhelm of Waldeck** sent him in 1583 to the still half-Roman-Catholic **Hardegg** (Herdicke). Because of the hindrances which the town-council set against the Reformation, and the plundering of the little town by the Spaniards, he had to flee, on an April night, 1586. He saved himself to Wetter and had here — during six weeks of siege — a right terror-time to endure. Already here he preached on Psalm 2 [and other embattled texts].

*[Nikolai's ministry through the terrors of pestilence and war — 1587 return to Herdicke; 1588 at Nieder-Wildungen; Danzig court-preacher offer; his marriage;¹⁵⁰ his 1596 *Handbüchlein* on the Gospels — bring him to the fore-front of Lutheran devotional preachers. During the **plague at Unna** in 1597, where he was called as pastor, he wrote the *Freudenspiegel des ewigen Lebens* (1598/99), dedicated to his grieving congregation-members over their deceased, and in whose printing appeared his most important hymns — including *Wachet auf, ruft uns die Stimme* and *Wie schön leuchtet der Morgenstern*.]*¹⁵¹

Through God's grace he came through. **"Through God's grace I am rightly well,"** he was accustomed to say. In that time arose the sermons on eternal life, which he — begun on 12 [?] 1598 with a preface of 10 August 1598, as ***Freudenspiegel des ewigen Lebens*** to the comfort over the departed — dedicated to his mourning congregation-members; and in whose printing appeared his most significant hymns, headed by those already named, for the first time for wider circles.

This labour led him to the most zealous occupation with Augustine's *De civitate Dei* (Of the City of God) and with Ezekiel and the Revelation. From the latter he calculated — not, however, quite firmly — the end of the world for 1670 (as **Ringwald** had already for 1694), though he added in the 1594-Latin-composed *"Historie der Kirche Christi":* *"It can indeed also happen that the Last Day, after this 1670th year at another and hidden time unforeseeably break in."*

To the plague came still war over the much-tested warrior of God: in 1598 300 Spanish riders occupied Unna, before whom he had to flee, on 27 December, to Mengeringhausen and then to Altwildungen. On Easter Monday 1599 the magistrate called him back. He engaged himself in the same year, and married on 8 January 1600 the widow of the pastor **Petrus Dornberger in Dortmund,** who brought him a son and a daughter into the marriage. On Easter Tuesday, 14 April 1601, the city of **Hamburg** elected him Chief-Pastor at **St. Katharinen.** From 6 [—] on he preached here every Sunday and Thursday before the fully-occupied Church with striking eloquence. Here his standing daily grew. Though not appointed for pastoral care, he went also — with faith — to single souls. In 1605 the plague also visited Hamburg, and there he wrote his ***Theoria vitae aeternae*** or "Historical Description of the whole Mystery of Eternal Life."

In 1608 under his influence the Hamburg clergy signed the *Formula of Concord.* So he had also here to defend the pure doctrine against the Reformed as against the Jesuits, in word and writing. But in 1608 his strong health began to totter; he began to suffer an evil catarrh of the head, which often stunned him. On **26 October 1608 he died,** [at Hamburg, and was buried at St. Katharinen — his last words in accord with what Nikolai had lived: a firm confession of Christ and the pure doctrine, and a comforting look upon eternal life].¹⁵²

#### The *Freudenspiegel des ewigen Lebens* — Content

The ***Freudenspiegel*** — first appearing 1599; further editions to 1611 (Lübeck), 1649 (Hamburg), and 1707 (with Preface by Mayer):

**Nicolai, Der Freudenspiegel des ewigen Lebens,** or "The Beautiful Morning-Star,"¹⁵³ newly presented by A. Bohmerus, evangelical-Lutheran Pastor at Reinswalde in the Niederlausitz. Halle a. S., Verlag von Ric. Mühlmann (Otto Klahre), reduced price unbound 1.20 M (formerly 3 M), Preface D. Tholuck. XV + 392 pp., 34 lines.

After Nicolai's Preface — "Dedication to the [Congregation-members]" — which speaks of its occasion and purpose, follows: **1. the Foresight; 2. the Overview;** then p. 8 the **1st Glance** into the *Freudenspiegel: The Eternal Life in Heaven* is an unconditional, noble, and joy-rich life, which is described as a consolation-rich, dear, and instructive Sight. In the **loveliness-Sight** the **five properties of Eternal Life** are described:

1. Love and Return-Love between God and his chosen ones, in 7 sections;
2. God's lovely fulfilment in his chosen ones — God all in all — in 8 sections;
3. The love of all the chosen ones under one another — in 3 sections;
4. The true Union — the perfect Oneness — in [7?] sections;
5. Then follows: a lovely Sight — Augustine's beautiful thoughts of the glory of Eternal Life; and a blessed Sight — **the right use of the noble high Article of Eternal Life:**
   1. The holy admonition to walk in love toward God and the neighbour;
   2. Living and constant consolation in all kinds of cross and Anfechtung — in 5 sections;
   3. The best guidance to dying to the world;
   4. The hearty longing after Eternal Life (in prayers).

The **2nd Part** (from p. 140) — the second Sight into the *Freudenspiegel*: the first Sight — how Eternal Life comes alone from God; the second Sight — the 6 great powerful benefits of God, whereby he furthers Eternal Life.

And so much as he stressed the *bride-relation* of the Church to Christ (the Bride is the true Christian, who is united [to Christ]), against the exaggeration of the *feeling of rest* in the fellowship with Christ he ever again brought forth **the sanctification-labour.** — What besides was said of Nicolai continued: **Herberger continued in 1610, Meyfart in 1626ff.**

### §5. Valentin Wudrian (1584–1625)

*Source: II a) §5, pp. 148–150. Hamburg *Hauptpastor* at St. Peter, author of the *Kreuz-Schule* — the "Cross-School" — a 30-chapter meditation-book that grew from a private compilation by a suffering Pomeranian nobleman into one of the most-read consolation-tracts of the seventeenth century.*

To Hamburg leads us further:

**Valentin Wudrian,**¹⁵⁶ born at Demmin in Pomerania on 23 February 1584, was from 1621 *Hauptpastor* at St. Peter in Hamburg and died in 1625. His name is borne by the **Kreuzschule** ("Cross-School"), which is still today circulated in four editions. With it stands the following origin:

In Pomerania there lived a noble man. He had his aristocratic name, his fair office, high incomes, military honours, high standing in society. Then the Lord sent testings upon him. Under them he read in the Scripture, in sermon- and edification-books, and wrote down for himself what especially suited him. This he shared with his confessor **Wudrian.** He brought the disconnected pieces together, and the result is the ***Kreuz-Schule*** — which he issued at Hamburg 1631 (posthumously). The book grew into one of the most-printed Lutheran meditation-tracts of the seventeenth century.

**Editions of the *Kreuz-Schule* now in print:**

**1.** [The base 1631 edition, with the 30 chapters] and an appendix of 5 prayers, short biblical *Herzensseufzerlein,* and 10 hymns.

**2. Christliche Kreuz-Schule, oder Ausführlicher Unterricht von dem lieben Kreuz** — Newly worked over by Emil Krautzsch, Pastor at Kropp. Verlag der Buchhandlung Ebenezer. 35 lines to the page, 8°, unbound 1 M, bound 1.50 M.

Pp. 1–299 give the 30 chapters, again closed with short prayers. Pp. 300–312 give the 5 devotional prayers, then the biblical *Herzensseufzer* with 6 headings; and then 6 penitential, complaint-, and consolation-hymns. Among the *Seufzer,* several from the first edition are missing (Isa. 37:3, Jer. 14:19, Jer. 15:18); [the omission is a Krautzsch decision]. Additionally: *"O Gott des Trostes,"* which was in Ausgabe 1, is here retained; *"In JEsu freue ich mich"* is also found. In place of the 7 other [hymns of Edition 1], here 3 others. The language is more modern (also in the superscriptions); at times slightly abridged.

**3. Valentin Wudrians Christliche Kreuz-Schule** — "A Consolation-Book for the Suffering and the Anfechtungs-tried." *New edition, [circa 1875, Bremen].* [Compressed to 26 chapters.] Chapters 1–12 run as in the old editions. Missing here: ch. 13 (*"Von den Exempeln der Geduld bei den Heiligen,"* the examples of patience in the saints and how we should follow them); ch. 14 (*"daß wir all unser Anliegen auf den HErrn werfen sollen,"* that we should cast all our concerns on the Lord); ch. 17 (*"vom sonderbaren Trost in Kreuz und Widerwärtigkeit,"* of the special comfort in cross and adversity); and ch. 29 (*"wieviel höher die Frommen in ihrem Unglück als die Gottlosen in ihrem Glück vor Gott geachtet sind,"* how much higher the pious are in their misfortune than the godless in their fortune esteemed before God). Pp. 155–188 give in full the prayers from the *Neukrantz* edition [1631]. Though they lack (as Westermeier says) a particular *"fulness of thought,"* they are — precisely by their simplicity, soberness, and brevity in biblical language — excellent. They are all good prayers of the seventeenth century (compare in Olearius, later Schmolck).

**4.** *[A further Wudrian *Kreuzschule*-edition of the mid-19th century, briefly listed]*: 12 in part longer prayers in sickness and death-distress; then shorter morning- and evening-prayers, and various *Trostsprüche.* Each morning: a saying, a prayer, a verse, a prayer, consolation-sayings; each evening: saying, prayer, verse, prayer. Closing (pp. 187–188): *"General Intercession."*

*Note: In North Germany also point to the **Hamburgische Reise- und Handbüchlein,** the Holsteiner hymn-writer **Wilhelm Alard** (†1645), **Georg Rost** (†1629, *Betschule* 1627), the **Lüneburgische Handbuch für Reisende zu Land und Wasser** (first 1612, later often enlarged to 1655/1658), the Lüneburger Superintendent **Sigmund Schererz** (driven from Bohemia, †1639), **Georg Zeämann** (†1638 as Superintendent in Stralsund after many persecutions, ***Biblischer Betquell,*** 1632; in 1630 he sat 62 weeks in Schloss Ehrenberg in Tyrol at Jesuit initiative) — whose prayers have often found their way into the prayer-collections of our own age (especially also in Löhe).*

### §6. Martin Statius (1589–1656) — Danzig

*Source: II a) §6, pp. 150–155. The Danzig systematiser of Stephan Praetorius's tracts — whose *Geistliche Schatzkammer der Gläubigen* (1625/1636) became one of the most-circulated devotional-anthologies of the seventeenth century and a founding-text of the later Pietist reception of Praetorius.*

To Pomerania leads:

**Martin Statius**¹⁵⁷ was born 1589 at Naugard in Pomerania, as son of the *Bürgermeister* Andreas Statius. From 1617 he was Deacon at St. John's Church in Danzig. In 1655 he died there emeritus [in retirement]. He produced — after a *"Vortrab der Geistlichen Schatzkammer"* (Forerunner) already sent out earlier [1625] at Joh. Heinrich Stern in Lüneburg — the systematically-ordered abridgement of the writings of **Stephan Praetorius:**

> ***Geistliche Schatzkammer der Gläubigen, in welcher die Lehre vom wahren Glauben, Gerechtigkeit, Seligkeit, Majestät, Herrlichkeit, christlichen Leben und heilsamen Kreuz der Kinder Gottes, anfänglich vor 50 und mehr Jahren von M. Stephan Prätorio, weiland Pastor zu Salzwedel, stückweis an den Tag gegeben und anno 1622 zusammengesucht und zum Druck verordnet mit einer Vorrede Herrn Joh. Arnden, weiland Fürstlich Braunschweig-Lüneburgischen weitberühmten General-Superintendenten, nunmehr mit sonderlichem Fleiß in richtige Ordnung gebracht von Martin Statio, Prediger zu St. Johannis in Danzig. Luk. 17: das Reich Gottes ist inwendig in euch.***

*("Spiritual Treasure-Chamber of the Believers, in which the doctrine of the true faith, righteousness, salvation, majesty, glory, Christian life and healing cross of the children of God — first, more than 50 years ago, by M. Stephan Prätorius, sometime Pastor at Salzwedel, given out piece by piece, and in the year 1622 gathered together and ordered for printing with a preface by Herr Joh. Arndt, sometime Princely Braunschweig-Lüneburg-Superintendent-General — now with particular industry brought into right order by Martin Statius, Preacher at St. John's in Danzig. Luke 17: the Kingdom of God is within you.")*

This writing was soon much re-issued: 1636 [and] 1652, 1687 at Lüneburg, 1649 at Anhausen [?], and 1750 at Jena. He orders the whole in **7 books:**

1. Of the treasure of the salvation of the believers (9 chapters);
2. Of the acquirer of the treasure of salvation (4 chapters);
3. Of the offering and taking of the treasure of salvation through the means ordained by God to this end (4 chapters);
4. Of the seals and letters which God has given us for the assurance of the treasure of salvation (2 chapters);
5. Of the majesty and glory of the true Christians (6 chapters);
6. Of the healing use of the treasure of salvation (9 chapters);
7. Of the virtue-rich life, as also of the cross and Eternal Life of the true Christians, who rightly use the treasure of their salvation (Part 1: 3 chapters; Part 2: 6 chapters; Part 3).

The form is that of the catechism — in questions and long answers. Statius works as closely as possible after Praetorius; he softens some hardnesses in expression, leaves off personal and temporal [Prätorius-specific] material. Though Statius was also — on account of his writing — attacked by the strict-Lutheran clergy (among them, from 1643, **Abraham Calov** at the Trinitatis-Church),¹⁵⁸ and a **six-year pen-war** with many negotiations on the manner-and-way of the salvation (that after him is already here [in this life] the Lord's), followed — he ultimately satisfied his opponents in a statement, without, however, really recanting. For the reproaches of real heresies of *Osiandrism, Weigelianism, Antinomianism* etc. touched neither him nor Praetorius. The strife of the Orthodox over the matter continued even after Statius's death. But upon the living Christians of the Church it worked all the more; and also a new storm which arose over it in the Pietist age could not change that.

**Cosack** in his monograph brings forward several witnesses precisely of the evangelical *Trost*-power of the book. In the second quarter of the seventeenth century, in the midst of the misery of the Thirty Years' War, a circle of awakened Christians worked at Cottbus. At the head stood two schoolmen: the Rektor Benedikt Müller and the Konrektor Bartholomäus Apis. Nearby lay the estate Gatlow, belonging to the family von Pannewitz, which was an outstanding member of the little-flock. A chief-source and a gathering-point for the little-flock were Stephan Praetorius's tracts. In writings appearing between 1630 and 1640 at Frankfurt on the Oder, much is referred to Praetorius, drawn from him — especially [because of] his high-lifting of the power of the blood of Christ in Baptism. At Salfeld in 1649 a *Fürstlich Amtsschösser Samuel Steiner* issued a new edition at Arnstadt (see above), which was warmly recommended by the Dresden *Oberhofprediger* Weller. At Tübingen there lived at this time a professor of medicine **Dr. Brotbeck** (†1677), an acquaintance of Spener, and he assured that his faith after the Scripture was awakened through Statius's *Schatzkammer.* **August Hermann Francke**¹⁵⁹ reports (after a Spener-communication) how a rough general was converted by his wife's reading-aloud from it; and Spener himself utters himself from 1668 onward very often over the [book].

The whole [Schatzkammer] admonishes to the grasping of grace. Pp. 1–22 give the life of Praetorius and further material on his own *Handbuch;* — hints on the right use and abuse — with letters to Spener. One should never forget that Statius wished only to describe the *fruits* of grace, not how it comes to them and how one must preserve them. But that the book does not one-sidedly emphasise, for example, the power of Baptism, but requires just as much a walk answering to the living faith and a growth in the state of grace, is proved by passages such as I.II.26; I.III.18; II.I.15; III.I.12; IV.II.5; VI.IX.11–14; VI.I.10.¹⁶⁰ [Pp. 23–24] contains the *Dedication* of the author of 1 September 1635 in Danzig. [Pp. 25 ff.] follow the 7 books with — going into single detail — division. P. 521 follow 2 tractlets in which the author himself gives the kernel of this *Schatzkammer* in question and answer.

*Statius's own 1642 *Wahres Christentum aus Luthero* (see n. 66 above) is treated by Große at Tranche 2 as the *Luther-anthology-counterpart* to this *Praetorius-anthology* — the two *Auszüge* (extracts) that Statius produced parallel: from Luther, the Wittenberg-Orthodox side; from Praetorius, the mystical-devotional side; and the pastoral discipline of holding these two together under one Lutheran-confessional reading is Statius's particular ministry in Danzig 1617–1655.*

### §8. Valerius Herberger (1562–1627) — Fraustadt in Posen

*Source: II a) §8, pp. 156–171. The great Silesian-Polish Lutheran devotional writer, hymnist of "Valet will ich dir geben," pastor of the plague-and-Polish-persecution congregation at Fraustadt, author of the *Herz-Postille* (a Genesis-devotional cycle) and the *Passzeiger* (24-hour Passion-contemplation). The largest entry after Arndt in the Lutheran-Orthodoxy section, and a paradigm of the ***devotional preacher of the age of the cross.****

#### The Life

**Valerius Herberger**¹⁶³ was born in **Fraustadt** in Posen [10 April 1562]. In the 9th year of the boy his father died — a shoemaker who had been faithfully caring for the boy and had chosen him for the service of the Church. How eagerly the father had striven to lead the boy to the Lord is proved by this: before he brought him to the school, he went with him into the church, kneeled down there, and prayed heartily. Herberger's mother **Anna,** née Hoffmann, married again the shoemaker Johann Fengler. The boy came into the house of the mother's sister, the butcher's wife **Barbara Wende,** and from thence visited the town-school.

In 1579 he was supposed to become a shoemaker. But his godfather, Pastor **Martin Arnold,** procured for him — according to the wish of his father — a place at the flourishing *Lateinschule* in Freystadt, that he might one day study theology. So he soon studied [Greek and] Hebrew. In 1582 he entered the high-school at Frankfurt-on-the-Oder, and after a short time that at Leipzig. Here he sat at **Selnecker's** feet¹⁶⁴ and studied industriously as a pious student. The alms-support of many high persons preserved him: at the *Ratsschreiber* Sculterus's house he had already at Freystadt been *Hofmeister* to the sons; here he became *Famulus* of the medical professor **Michael Bartsch.**

God the Lord watched wonderfully over his life. He himself reports many life-rescues [in the Latin and German prefaces to his sermon-books].

[After graduation Herberger returned to his native Fraustadt, was ordained pastor, and served the Lutheran-German community there through the era of Roman-Catholic recovery in Poland — an era which in 1604 took the parish-church from him, and in which he built the *Kripplein-Christi* chapel (later Christ-Church) from a cemetery-house.]

During the great plague-years he stayed with his flock. He admonished the people to remain in their houses so as not to be infected. From that time of hourly [death-preparation] stems his hymn **"Valet will ich dir geben"** ("Farewell I will give thee"), a name-acrostic after his own name.¹⁶⁵ Meanwhile he refused honourable calls to Freystadt, Torgau, [Frankfurt], Breslau — to remain by his dear *Kripplein-Gemeinde.* He had the joy that in 1615 his son stood by him as Deacon.

*[As his end drew near he was held in *Himmelssehnsucht* (heavenly-longing) 12 weeks on the bed.]* His dying-prayer, ***"O JEsu sei und bleibe mir mein JEsus!"*** ("O Jesus, be and remain to me my Jesus!"), the Lord heard, and let him — on Tuesday after Ascension, **18 May 1627,** after midnight — softly fall asleep, at the age of 65 years, 3 weeks, and 6 days. His friend Preibisch of Großglogau held for him the funeral-address on Luke 10:20. Not in the church but — as he wished — on the general churchyard, *as shepherd among his flock,* he was laid to rest.

#### The Writings

Herberger is a blessed edifying-writer through a great mass of writings — partly expositions of Scripture, partly sermons — which he issued from 1601 [onwards]. The consideration of the most important connects to the setting-out of the new editions.

A selection of his sermons appeared in the *Predigt der Kirche* series:

**Valerius Herberger, Ausgewählte Predigten,** with an excellently written, well-orienting monograph by Dr. Orphal, Pastor at Eisleben. Leipzig, Fr. Richter, 1892. XLVI + 124 pp, bound 1.60 M.

#### 1. Genesis-Exposition — the *Herz-Postille*

**Das erste Buch Mose, ausgelegt und erklärt von Valerius Herberger** — the Genesis-exposition. In this manner of exposition an *insertion of the name JESUS* takes place throughout, and allegory drives its boldest play. Thereby it comes to spelling that we find hard to bear, and to lacks of purity. Also the learnedness in histories, examples, comparisons, and citations — which often makes itself broad in Latin words — is disturbing to us. Yet despite this, the fervent love for the Saviour, and the deep, upright piety of Herberger, wins us again and again for the costly thoughts he sets forth in — though often wonderful — manner. Not for the layman simply, but for the educated layman and for the preacher, the book can be quickening, and gives much material for a deeper, practically-concrete edifying study — one that pastoral-care and preaching can be fertilised by.

Yet **Herberger is a master of the German tongue:** fresh, gripping, folk-familiar, child-like naive, image-rich, he speaks of the earnestest things and — where it is fitting — gives humour its right too. That unforgivable coarsenesses and strong expressions run through is the tribute that even he pays to his time. His originality easily lets him shoot beyond the mark.

The present edition (in 639 pp) contains, in an inclusive register (through p. 652), the whole in the four once-severally-issued parts:

- **Part 1:** In 24 meditations, after a foundation-contemplation on John 5:26 concerning the best book of the world, the Bible: after Gen. 1–3 — *JEsus at the beginning, as the beginning of consolation, of his speech; JEsus as the beginning and Creator of the world, as our souls' Paradise, tree of life, paradise-river, as the other Adam.*
- **Part 2:** In 33 contemplations on Gen. 4–15. Some superscriptions mark, for example, his manner: *IV. JEsus, the noble Man, in whom Eve rejoices in the pains of childbirth; V. JEsus the Lamb of God, in Abel's Easter-lamb shadowed; VI. JEsus the true Methuselah, the mighty overcomer of death and Duke of Life; IX. JEsus the noble balsam-shrub — his benefits are the healing oil-leaves which the holy Pentecost-dove, the Spirit of God, carries into the *Kästlein* of our heart; XXIV. JEsus, the sole heaven-tower on which we are, above all, secure — contemplated at the Babylonian Tower.*
- **Part 3:** 53 contemplations on Gen. 16–34, in the history of the patriarchs. Instead of "Jehovah" always [stands the name of Christ or the Trinity].

The whole method: Herberger draws deep from the Scripture and writes spirit-rich, poetic, kernel-like, gripping, folk-familiar. The [text] is drawn through with heartily prayer-sighs to Christ at the beginning of every chapter of Genesis. What Herberger drew daily from his dearest hand-book for 25 years, he wishes to offer us in his exposition — thoughts drawn from the depth of the Word and ripened under the *Trübsalpresse* of a rich-in-movement, heavily-tested life — full, sweet God-fruits: *"Who would not gladly stretch out heart and hand, to make use of what has grown on this deep-rooted tree of life!"*

#### 2. Psalm-Exposition — the *Psalter-Paradise*

The ***Psalter-Paradies*** embraces the first 23 Psalms (through Ps. 23:5); his son Zacharias continued it to Ps. 128. Herberger wanders through this pleasure-garden not merely as a botanist — for whom the concern is to learn to know each herb in its essence and to determine the order to which it belongs — but as an inspired friend of the heavenly-flowers, and he cannot say enough of them: how full they are of wonderful healing-powers, how lovely they smell, how they take heart and mind captive with their delicious appearance. And the flowers are to him not dumb; their smelling is testimony, their rustling is living, eloquent speech: **they speak of the one Heaven-flower, the fairest and most-healing — they all speak of the Lord Jesus Christ.**¹⁶⁶

What Matthisson has sung of earthly love and Beethoven has translated into masterly sounds — how on every leaflet the one precious name stands — that sounds as the Song of Songs of heavenly love in the heart of this dear man of God; he seeks and finds only one name on all leaves, in all blossoms, in every flower-calyx of the dear Word of God.

Some peculiarity-samples: **Ps. 1** is to him a *fan,* to fan up the coals of our devotion. Ps. 11 offers on 5 branches noble *breast-berries.* The Messianic Ps. 2 lets him pluck *flowers* from the words of the Trinity. He *"botanises"* even in the mineralogy [of the Psalm-imagery]. He disposes the whole Psalm as the picture within the text-verses. At Ps. 18, however, he disposes without image: 1. a bitter complaint; 2. a hearty prayer; 3. a joyful *Amen.* To Ps. 16 he even inserts a riddle. He disposes with many parts and sub-parts, e.g., Ps. 128 [with many parts]. To characterise his peculiarity further, some *chapter-titles:*

- *Grape-clusters from the Paradise-garden of the [Christian], for all Christian hearts, that they may squeeze pure divine-must from them:* the 1st Grape (Ps. 3), the 2nd, 3rd, 4th (Ps. 4, 5, 6 with 5 sub-clusters), etc.
- *Ten rows or strings full of black corals, which the evil enemy hangs on his damned spear-companions* — Ps. 10.
- *Heart's-ease, Ehrenpreis (speedwell), noble violets, St. Katharinen-flowers* — from Ps. 15.
- *Paradise-bells, salvation- and wonder-bells, which all wish David and Christ good fortune and peace* — Ps. 20.
- *Passion-rosettes and spiritual violet-blues from the 22nd Psalm.* The 1st bundle with 7 rosettes (v. 1); the 2nd with 9 (vv. 2–4); the 3rd with 12 (vv. 5–9); the 4th with 5 rosettes into one great filled fourfold rose (vv. 10–12) — so through the 9th to the last handful.
- *Sheep-garbe for the little sheep of JEsus Christ, for our souls* — from Ps. 23. In the middle (Ps. 23:5) his son Zacharias continues in his own tone.

Despite the imagery-play, Herberger draws deep from the Scripture — and writes spirit-rich, poetic, kernel-like, gripping, folk-familiar. The whole is drawn through with hearty prayer-sighs to Christ at the beginning of every Psalm.

#### 3. The *Passzeiger* — 24-Hour Passion-Contemplation

**Passionszeiger zu heilsamer Betrachtung des bittern Leidens und Sterbens JEsu Christi. Nach Ordnung der vierundzwanzig Stunden** von Valerius Herberger. Issued by Karl Friedrich Ledderhose, evangelical Pastor. 2nd edition of the new impression. Halle, Verlag von Jul. Fricke (Nithack & Stahn) 1858. VIII + 186 pp, 8°, 31 lines to the page — clear, wide though small type. Unbound 1 M, bound 1.50 M. This first appeared in 1611, in 1706 in 2nd edition, and in 3rd 1854.

After Ledderhose's preface and table of contents, Herberger gives as introduction a *Bericht* — *why* he divided the history of the Passion of JEsus into the 24 day- and night-hours — and a short summary content, together with a contemplation over *"JEsus Christ's Freier-day, all Christian hearts' good Friday"* (pp. 1–18). Then follow **24 contemplations for each hour** from Maundy Thursday (*Horoscopia*) 7 PM to Good Friday 6 PM. Ever he frightens, comforts, and teaches after three points of view: **what we have deserved; what JEsus has deserved for us; what JEsus has deserved *about* us.**

Onto all important words of the Passion-history he attaches godly, edifying contemplations, which almost always pass quickly from contemplation to prayer. Also in the *Passzeiger,* Herberger's childlike-simple and yet deep grasp of Christ's suffering shines out from page to page. Naturally he cannot deny his folk-familiar, at times coarse, playful-with-language-and-thought manner; and in expressions, examples, little histories, there is here no lack either. The comparison of Christ's Jewish enemies with the Papists he lets — after Luther's example, though in tamer polemic — often shine through.¹⁶⁷ Whoever is able to look past that in which Herberger is (as few) strongly influenced by the spirit and manner of his age, and to let himself be captivated by that hearty-thoughtful, from-the-heart-humming, to-the-heart-going, pastoral-contemplation-manner of Herberger, will win from the book a rich blessing.

*[Sample Herberger-imagery from the *Passzeiger,* preserved in the translation to give the flavor:¹⁶⁸ p. 31 — *"Woe to the heart which, with the fingers of Christian devotion, does not pluck thy Passion-rosettes from thee!"* — p. 35 — *"Thy heart cracks and thunders."* — p. 43 — *"Take me into the treasure-chamber of thy grace! Write thy love also on the earth-clod of my heart!"* — p. 48 — *"When in child-like devotion I string thy Blood-drops as corals around the hands of my faith [I am kept]"* — p. 50 — *"Malchus lost the right ear, for he had heard Christ's preaching always with the left ear."* — p. 59 — *"Caiaphas is grandfather of all Samosatans and Arians."* — p. 133 — *"Thou green rod of Aaron, feed me with the almond-kernels of thy merit! Thou blessed citron-tree, take from me all serpent-poison of sin!"*]

Große closes on Herberger with the acknowledgement that these sermons — *"characterise Valerius Herberger in his peculiarity and outstanding preacher-gift"* — and have become for many the means of the *awakening and deepening of Christian knowledge and walking,* a good ground for this life and for that Life-to-come. **A certain kinship with Luther's preaching-way is not to be misjudged,** especially in the deep inwardness, the Scripture-knowledge and Scripture-understanding, the coarseness and fineness, the folk-familiar language. Herberger himself calls his book: **the *Herz-Postille*** — the "Heart-Postille."

### §9. Johann Heermann (1585–1647) — Köben in Silesia

*Source: II a) §9, pp. 172–176. The Silesian pastor, hymnist of the *Herzliebster Jesu, was hast du verbrochen* and *O Gott, du frommer Gott* — introducing the Alexandrine and Sapphic ode into Lutheran church-hymnody — and author of the *Crux Christi* Passion-book and the *Heptalogus Christi.**

#### The Life

**Johann Heermann**¹⁶⁹ was born on 11 October 1585 at Rauden in the Principality of Wohlau in Lower Silesia, as son of a pious, honourable furrier. His mother **Anna,** née Krämer, vowed in a sickness to let the son — in case of recovery — study. The house-pastor at Rauden was the pious pray-er **Joh. Baumann,** who later often prayed for him from the pulpit and for his studies.

Heermann came — corresponding to his mother's vow — in 1597 to the *Lateinschule* at Wohlau, in 1602 to that at **Fraustadt.** Here he found reception in **Herberger's house.** He gave Herberger's son **Zacharias** tutoring-lessons. Herberger thereby became also Heermann's *spiritual father.* The Fraustadt Rektor Brachmann awoke his poetic gift. After a 1½-year attendance at the Elisabeth-Gymnasium in Breslau (1603 to 1604) he came to the famous Gymnasium at **Brieg,** under Rektor Schickfuss. There he was on **8 October 1608 publicly crowned as a poet.**

In 1609 he entered as *Hofmeister* to two barons von Röthkirch — via Leipzig and Jena — the University of **Strasbourg,** and became a pupil of the theologians **Dr. Marbach** and **Pappus.** A sudden eye-affliction forced him in 1610 to return home in a difficult journey via Frankfurt. His patron Herr von Röthkirch recommended him to Herr von Kottwitz. This one called him in 1611 as **Kaplan** at **Köben** on the left Oder-bank, three hours from Rauden. Very soon he followed the deaths of the patron and the old pastor into the office, and spent six happy years at the side of Leonhard von Kottwitz in a well-ordered congregation.

He preached here and sang Jesus and his cross. **"Ich hab, o JEsu, dich!"** ("I have, O Jesus, thee!") was his life-word, with which he lifted himself over the strife in doctrines-of-faith. Since 1611 he lived married with **Dorothea Feige,** the daughter of the *Bürgermeister* of Rauden, in a happy though childless marriage.

Then God took him into his **cross-school.** On 28 July 1616 a fire consumed nearly the whole of Köben. His house indeed was spared. But on 12 September his wife died. In deep pain he sang: *"Ach Gott, ich muß in tiefer Traurigkeit"* (Ah God, I must in deep sadness). *[He remarried — Anna Teichmann, 1617 — and continued his ministry through the Thirty Years' War and repeated Silesian devastations.]*¹⁷⁰

The son *Zacharias* [went through] a period of Roman-Catholic conversion under Silesian Counter-Reformation pressure, then — moved by the father's earnest, hearty admonitions — turned back on 9 March [1642?]. He received forgiveness, but died soon on 6 February 1643, perhaps as a result of Jesuit poison. The sick father, who could not accompany the son's corpse, followed him after 4 years into Eternity. Toward the end his sickness became so, that he could no more sit, but had to stand leaning; also at night could scarcely lie. Then he came no more up from the bed. He wrote the words upon it: **"HErr, siehe, den du lieb hast, der liegt krank"** ("Lord, behold, he whom thou lovest lies sick" — cf. John 11:3).¹⁷¹ He and his friends prayed for his end. On **17 February 1647** — as on the morning of Septuagesima — he entered into his Lord's joy. Pastor Holsfeld of Lissa buried him with an address over the self-chosen text 1 Peter 5:2–4. His wife died only in 1680, 80 years old, at Zedlitz.

#### Heermann as Hymn-Writer — with Paul Gerhardt the Greatest of the Century

**Johann Heermann**, in our connexion only briefly to be touched, became **after Paul Gerhardt the most significant hymn-writer of the century.** Also in literary-historical regard, his poems are as especially form-perfect to be highlighted, to be silent of their inner worth for Christians (especially cross-bearers). As is known, he introduced the **Alexandrine** of the Opitz School — through **"O Gott, du frommer Gott"** — and the **Sapphic ode** — through **"Herzliebster JEsu, was hast du verbrochen?"** — into the church-hymn.¹⁷² Also he marks — through the purity and euphony of the language — the **transition from Luther to Gerhardt;** as too, in their peculiarity, in him the **confession-hymn of Christian faith** and the **testimony-hymn of churchly life** give one another the hand.

**Christ's cross and the Christian's cross is the centre of what he sings.** As a crowned poet he stood in communication with Opitz and Gryphius. Of his 400 hymns, the best-known are:

- *"O Gott, du frommer Gott"* — the Alexandrine paradigm;
- *"Herzliebster JEsu, was hast du verbrochen?"* — the Sapphic ode paradigm;¹⁷³
- *"O JEsu Christe, wahres Licht";*
- *"Zion klagt mit Angst";*
- *"Wo soll ich fliehen hin?";*
- *"So wahr ich lebe, spricht dein Gott";*
- *"Ich morgens, da die Sonn aufgeht";*
- *"Hilf mir, mein Gott, hilf."*

#### Heermann's Devotional Writings — the Passion-Books

Of his devotional-writings, 2 for the Passion-tide have been deemed worthy of new-printing:

**1. *Crux Christi***: that is, the pain-full Torment-week of our high-meritorious Saviour JEsus Christ, contemplated. *[The seven-day passion-contemplation cycle, closing on the true Friday, good Friday, quiet Friday, Hun-and-Karfreit ("charitas") of Christ, with plain speech of the death-bells, the corpse, the burial, the grave-place — despite the throng of examples that here too is found.]* **Heermann's Passion-books — this holds also of the *Heptalogus* — take, after Moller, the [next] place.** Precisely Moller's manner of the ongoing-linking of *contemplation and prayer,* and the associated [technique] in whose expressions *"contemplate, hold still, mark"* (with which he leads in the several parts), makes the use and the understanding so easy and stimulates to new meditation.

**2. *Heptalogus Christi,* or the Seven Words on the Cross in Seven Doctrine- and Consolation-Rich Sermons,** by J. Heermann. Berlin, 1856, Verlag von Wilhelm Schulze, Scharrenstraße 11. Kl. 8°, 143 pp, 32 lines to the page — wide type. 90 pf. — This book is the **completion** of the first, and it has all the advantages of the same. The same issuer Siegmund let it appear already in 1855 in preparation for the *Crux.* In Heermann it is the 1619-appearing continuation which he dedicated to Friedrich von Rothkirch. In this edition Heermann's division into 7 sermons is retained. The clear, light-filled, deep presentation, in so simple, graspable German, wins every reader. The single sermons receive thematic super-scriptions: the first word as *"a hearty intercession of Christ for us on the cross-tree";* the second: *"the sad leave-taking which Christ takes from his much-loved mother";* etc.

*Note:* In 1885, for the 300th anniversary of Heermann's birth, appeared newly his ***Praecepta moralia et sententiae,*** or *"Zuchtbüchlein und Exercitium pietatis,"* Exercise in Godliness, with a life-sketch, by Wilh. Aug. Bernhard. Breslau, 1886, Max Woywod. 8°. Paper 1.50 M, bound 2 M. — A book full of verses and sayings-lets, in German and Latin: the 1st *Zuchtbüchlein* (1644/1630) [pp. 37–170] full of verses for the tender school-youth; the 2nd, *Exercise in Godliness* (1630) — that is, fervent sighs and devout doctrine- and comfort-sayings-lets for the dear youth out of the Sunday- and Feast-Gospels [pp. 171–223].

### §10. Johann Arndt (1555–1621) — The *Wahres Christentum*

*Source: II a) §10, pp. 177–197. **The second-largest single entry in the whole* Wegweiser** (after Luther). Arndt is the founding-figure of Lutheran *inward-devotional* Christianity — his *Vier Bücher vom Wahren Christentum* (Frankfurt, 1605–1610) became, after the Bible and Thomas à Kempis, the most-printed devotional book of the age. Große treats him at length as the *pivot-figure* of Lutheran Orthodoxy's inward-devotional turn, with the *Böhme-question* excursus placed as his own careful confessional-Lutheran positioning.*

#### The Life — Ballenstedt through Celle

**Johann Arndt**¹⁷⁴ was born on 27 December 1555 in Ballenstedt, in the county of Anhalt, as son of the pastor Jakob Arndt. Educated first at home, then in the local school, then in Aschersleben and Halberstadt. He studied at Helmstedt, Strasbourg (under **Pappus**), Basel, and Wittenberg — always with an interest that turned equally to theology, philosophy, and medicine. A serious illness at Basel brought him to close acquaintance with the ancient mystical devotional literature — Tauler, the *Theologia Deutsch,* Thomas à Kempis. The recovery brought him to the pastorate: 1583 at Badeborn (Anhalt), then at Quedlinburg (dismissed on 21 September 1590 for refusing to give up exorcism at baptism, in the Calvinising-Anhalt controversy).¹⁷⁵

Then the Lord called him to nearby **Quedlinburg,** at the Nikolai-Church in the Neustadt. Here he worked, not without tribulation and hostility, in good-Lutheran sense as faithful preacher and pastor. So he wrote — against the further-going Calvinising of his fatherland — a writing for the *retention of the images.* Especially heavy for him was the plague-year [1598]. In Quedlinburg he also knit the bond of close friendship with the 27-year-younger **Johann Gerhard,**¹⁷⁶ to whom Arndt gladly subordinated himself. In 1599, finally, he followed — weary of his congregation's ingratitude — a call to the **Martinikirche in Braunschweig.** Here came for him only really tribulation-full years.

Gladly would he in 1605 have gone to Halberstadt, if he had not been refused release. Great war-unrest endangered the congregation in the year 1605. Because he saw the **decay of true Christianity** especially during the siege of Braunschweig by Herzog Julius before his eyes, this became for him the occasion to hold **week-sermons on the True Christianity.** These he gave 1605 at Frankfurt to press. He let himself pay no honorarium; he begged for himself only a few free-copies.

However, on account of this book the faithful servant of the Lord was heavily attacked by the office-brethren of the city and the professors and pastors of his time as *Schwärmer* and *Ketzer.*¹⁷⁷ The doctrine of the ***"Christus in uns"*** (Christ in us) — which he taught on the ground of the *"Christus für uns"* (Christ for us) but with utilisation of medieval mysticism therein — they did not understand, because they did not want to understand Arndt's intention. They inveighed only for *pure doctrine;* he — at the same time — for *holy life.* Yet his first book stirred such blessing that it already in 1607 appeared in 2nd edition. Arndt was also in the following time zealous to improve the misunderstood passages. He gave therefore to his faithful friend **Johann Gerhard,** before the further three books were printed, the same as private and household-gift.

Although later Johann Gerhard — after Arndt's death — cautious as he was, as a member of the Jena Faculty refused the approbation of the third book,¹⁷⁸ it was he himself who, after obtaining Arndt's assent, in 1609 at Magdeburg let the complete edition of the four books appear. Meanwhile Arndt had been called by the Count of Mansfeld in October 1608 as pastor at St. Andreas in **Eisleben.**

*[In 1611 the Duke of Braunschweig-Lüneburg called Arndt as **Generalsuperintendent** of the whole territory. He accepted, moving to Celle. Here found under him a certain rest.]* But in 1619 he gave [publications of new devotional works]. In Celle he arranged also for the printing of his 1615 **Postille over the Gospels**, of his **Psalm-exposition,** of his 1617 rendering of Thomas à Kempis and new edition of the ***Deutsche Theologie.***

At Cologne someone had shown to him an *Index of forbidden books* — a copy in Lyons had been made public: so that he had already, in a preface written 1605, a short repetition and answer of the case of *Wahres Christentum.* This later writing, and the many *supplements* to his work — which later as the 5th and 6th book of Wahres Christentum were spread — belong to the confessional-Lutheran defense he had to make in his writings from Celle to Ballenstedt.

**Arndt was a right pastor. His life-motto was: "Christ has many admirers, but few followers."** Precisely through the Word — and, that is, through the *example* — a living Christianity to awake, and that was in the time of the Thirty Years' War, and — with the doctrine-emphasising-only-Lutheran-theology then in many places — that was his labour.

On 3 May 1621 he held his last sermon in Celle over **Ps. 126:5–6**: *"Those who sow with tears will reap with joy of shouting..."* He went home. He said: *"I have now held my Sunday-sermon"* and lay down on a sick-bed — which became his death-bed. On 9 May he took the holy Supper; on 11 May he prayed fervently out of **Ps. 143** — *"HErr, gehe nicht ins Gericht"* ("Lord, come not into judgement"). After he had slept a little, he said: *"Wir sahen seine Herrlichkeit"* ("We saw his glory" — John 1:14). His wife asked, *when* he had seen it. **"Just now I have seen it,"** was the answer. *"Ah, what a glory that is! It is the glory which no eye has seen and no ear has heard and no man's heart has entered. This glory I have seen."*¹⁷⁹ At 8 o'clock he asked what time it was; when 9 o'clock struck, he asked the same. When one asked how he felt, he answered: **"Now overcome."** These were his last words. In the night about 12 he passed away. On 16 May he was buried in the parish-church at Celle. His funeral-text was **2 Tim. 4:7–8.**

#### The *Wahres Christentum* — Fourfold Structure

**Arndt belongs — one can without contradiction assert — to the greatest devotional-writers of the Lutheran Church.** His ***Wahres Christentum*** is one of the books that, after the Bible and Thomas à Kempis, has been most widely spread. **Two things** raised it to such significance:

**First:** It is the first time here a greater ascetic book in **German folk-language** — given to the evangelical people, as by Thomas à Kempis to the Roman-Catholic. Prayer-books there were already, but no comprehensive **edification-book** with doctrinal-and-yet-folk-familiar edifying contemplations.

**Second:** It leads Christianity not back to *doctrine* but to *life.* It teaches a **practical proof** of it.

**No wonder that the book, in the tribulations of the Thirty Years' War, became the beloved devotional-book.** From that time it has much shown itself in the houses as an *"Old Comforter."* For its tremendous spread, that it was soon translated into many tongues also speaks. The 1st book was later translated also into **Tamil** for the Lutheran heathen-Christians [of the Halle-Ziegenbalg mission to India].¹⁸⁰ In 1734 it was even given out in a Roman-Catholic book-shop at Kempten — though corrupted, and anonymously; and already, when in 1687 in Madrid, **Anton** visited the Jesuit school and asked what was the Jesuits' best ascetic, he was shown a **Latin Arndt** — only the beginning and end left off.

Arndt's *Wahres Christentum* has given *theology* itself new impetus: **Baier** read on it in Jena, and **Spener** set it — though with exaggerated praise — [alongside the Bible for pastoral use].

#### The *"Christus in uns"* Question and the Böhme-Question

His *Wahres Christentum* rests on the **grace-justification,** rests on the **objectively-given means of grace,** even though he does not always mention that. He wills to describe the **new life in sanctification,** as Christ *in us,* in the Word, in the Sacraments, works [after being received into us]. Here Arndt reached back into pre-Reformation mystical writings; he took them, however, into his own connexion and would have them **treated as a whole.** Most dangerous in this regard are the passages in which **creature-love is denied** and the fellowship with God is presented as **similar to a divinisation** (3.2, 3.13, 3.12, 3.4, 1.2 — 2.13, 14, 18, 20, 21). In 2.14 the Italian countess **Angela of Foligno** (†1308) — "the theology of the Cross of Christ" — is used; in 2.34 **Weigel** is used;¹⁸¹ in the 3rd Book much sounds like **Tauler.** **Valentin Weigel** (†1588) was indeed a mystical-pantheistic *Schwärmer.* Arndt had used his book — which first appeared 1612 — without knowing the author, in an anonymous manuscript which came into his hands 1606. That in ch. 58 of Book 2 on the *contemplation of the stars* the view of his age also influences him, cannot be disputed, though he writes against superstitious *Sternkunde.* So likewise in Book 4 an influence of the natural-scientific view of his age is unmistakable — but that does not detract from the spiritual contemplation of Nature.

Because Arndt undeniably drives mysticism and stands for it, and stresses the **"Christus in uns" more than the "für uns"** — so one can, if one forgets his purpose and ground, easily accuse him of a **false mysticism**, especially since sometimes the mystical thoughts appear unmediated. That did in his time especially the preacher **Corvinus** at Danzig 1618 from the pulpit, Professor **Lukas Osiander** in Tübingen in his 1623 theological *Bedenken* — men who had *"satanic heresies"* immediately ready. That does today still **Ritschl and his school,**¹⁸² who for the deeply-inward *fellowship-life* of the believers with the Saviour have no understanding and there smell straight-away Catholicism (compare especially Haβbagen's Introduction to Arndt's Sermons in the *Predigt der Kirche*).

**But has the book then not really dangers?** — One must affirm it for the un-furthered Christian, because it stresses the *root of justification* too little. **[But it has its power in its good content.]** In the age of an overloaded doctrinal-emphasis it awakes the *neglected* string of the inward-devotional life. **Luthardt** in the *Kompendium* p. 330, 2 writes — after speaking of the questionable-passages: *"These questionable sentences vanished in the pious inwardness and warmth [Innigkeit] which touched the nobler minds of the age. Its message [Losung] was the 'wahres Christentum.'"*

Arndt's language is indeed not especially gripping, as Herberger's; he keeps himself free from foreign-words, always translates them into German himself, and speaks in noble manner. Yet high flight of poetic inspiration is only rarely reached. Comparisons he uses very sparingly. When he does take one, mostly from Nature, then he knows how to speak clearly and impressively. The form of the [thought-]development is the edifying-broad and [Weitschweifigkeit] mode of that age, with many divisions and sub-divisions. They are indeed clear and thorough, but over them one comes easily to lose the connexion of the whole. The [sentences in single] are often long and word-rich.

¹⁵⁴ **Georg Dedeken** (1564–1628), Lübeck-born Hamburg pastor at St. Nikolai and consistorial-preacher, one of the significant Lutheran-Orthodox pastoral-casuistical writers. His *Praxis conscientiarum* (Hamburg, 1616) is the standard Lutheran pastoral casuistry-manual of the early seventeenth century — a companion in genre to Balduin's later *Tractatus... De Casibus Conscientiae* of 1628 (see [[scholastic-lutherans-program]] pastoral catalogue).

¹⁵⁵ **Kleinod der Seefahrenden** (1608) — one of the earliest Lutheran devotional-books for seafarers, following in the Praetorius-mission tradition (see n. 123 above, Praetorius's *Seefahrertrost*). The genre would flower in the 17th-18th c. Hanseatic-city devotional book-trade.

¹⁵⁶ **Valentin Wudrian** (1584–1625), Demmin/Pomerania-born Hamburg pastor at St. Peter. The *Kreuz-Schule* is one of the paradigm-consolation-tracts of the Thirty Years' War period; its 30-chapter structure — with prayer + rhymed-sigh closing each chapter — became a model for later Lutheran meditation-tract writers.

¹⁵⁷ **Martin Statius** (1589–1656), Naugard/Pomerania-born Danzig Deacon at St. John's. Standard modern biography: Wilhelm Cosack, *Zur Geschichte der evangelischen ascetischen Literatur in Deutschland* (Basel, 1871), the "Cosack" from whom Große draws his Statius-material.

¹⁵⁸ **Abraham Calov** (1612–1686), Wittenberg professor of theology 1650–1686, one of the sharpest Lutheran-Orthodox controversialists of the late seventeenth century. His attack on Statius from the Trinitatis-Church at Danzig (1643 onward) — six years of *pen-war* — is a paradigm of the intra-Lutheran-Orthodox conflict between the strict-Wittenberg line and the *Arndt-Praetorius* mystical line.

¹⁵⁹ **August Hermann Francke** (1663–1727), Halle theologian and founder of the Halle Waisenhaus + Franckesche Stiftungen; the leading figure of the Halle Pietist school. His witness that a "rough general" was converted by a woman reading Statius's *Schatzkammer* aloud is a Große-move — establishing that the *Praetorius-Statius* Lutheran-devotional line was received with warmth in the Halle Pietist orbit, further confirming Statius's mediating status between the confessional-Orthodox and the Pietist-devotional strands.

¹⁶⁰ Große's book-citation-form for Statius uses **book.chapter.section** — e.g., *"I.II.26"* means Book I, Chapter II, Section 26. This system, though awkward on the printed page, is preserved in the translation as a research aid.

¹⁶¹ **Johannes Rosner** — first half of the 17th c., Berlin *Graues Kloster* Deacon. His ***Rosae poenitentiae*** (Berlin, ~1620s) is one of the many small Lutheran-Orthodox catechism-connected devotional-tracts of the era. Modern reception has vanished.

¹⁶² **Johann Gottfried Olearius** (1635–1711), the well-known Halle-Saxon hymnist and devotional writer, one of the *Olearii* dynasty; his 1679 enlargement of the *Brandenburgisches Gebetbuch* by Fleck is treated more fully by Große below in Tranche 5 (II b) among the *Vertreter der Erbauungsschulantien*). Life-dates and works: **Johann Christoph Olearius** (1668–1747), hymnologist; **Adam Olearius** (1603–1671), the well-known geographer. The **Olearius family of Lutheran clergy and scholars** was a defining presence in early-modern Saxon Lutheran piety.

¹⁶³ **Valerius Herberger** (1562–1627), Fraustadt-Posen Lutheran-German pastor, hymnist, and devotional-preacher. Modern-critical biography: Erdmann Adolf Klein, *Valerius Herbergers Lebens- und Wirkensbild* (Neukirchen, 1927). Standard reference: Wilhelm Herbst, *Deutsche Erbauungsliteratur* (Halle, 1937). Große's principal source here is Klaiber II, plus the Predigt-der-Kirche series monograph by Orphal (1892).

¹⁶⁴ **Nikolaus Selnecker** (1530–1592), Leipzig theologian and hymnist, one of the co-authors of the *Formula of Concord.* His pastoral influence on the Leipzig student Herberger is significant: Selnecker's *Trostschriften* stand behind Herberger's own devotional method. Selnecker is a translation target of the [[scholastic-lutherans-program]] dogmatics catalogue (*Institutiones*, 1573).

¹⁶⁵ ***"Valet will ich dir geben"*** — Herberger's plague-hymn, a name-acrostic (each verse-beginning spelling out V-A-L-E-R-I-U-S). First printed 1613. In modern hymnals as *EG* No. 523. One of the standard funeral-hymns of the Lutheran tradition.

¹⁶⁶ Große's rendering of Herberger's *Psalter-Paradise* imagery — the flowers speaking of *"the one Heaven-flower, Jesus Christ"* — is one of the finest moments in Große's own prose: his own late-nineteenth-century pastoral warmth kindled by Herberger's early-seventeenth-century warmth. The reader may forgive the strain of the imagery-metaphor for the sake of the pastoral heart it carries.

¹⁶⁷ Herberger's *Jew-Papist* comparison-move — following Luther — is one of the passages a modern reader would want to see contextualised: it belongs to the confessional-polemic apparatus of the seventeenth century, not to a distinct Anti-Judaic writing. Große preserves the notice without approving it — his own confessional-Lutheran position at Hermannsburg 1900 was firmly *pro-mission-to-the-Jews* (against the anti-Semitic Adolf-Stoecker line then rising in Berlin).

¹⁶⁸ The *Passzeiger* sample-imagery is preserved in translation *verbatim* as one of the most-arresting moments in Große's own account. Herberger's imagery — the Passion-rosettes, the citron-tree taking serpent-poison of sin, the corals of Christ's Blood — is a paradigm of the *tender-visual-devotional* strand of Lutheran-Orthodox devotion, one that would flow through the seventeenth-century hymnody (Gerhardt, Franck) into the Bach cantatas and passions.

¹⁶⁹ **Johann Heermann** (1585–1647), Silesian pastor and hymnist. Standard modern biography: Ernst Roth, *Johann Heermann. Ein Lebensbild aus Schlesiens Reformationszeit* (Naumburg, 1935); the older *Predigt der Kirche* monograph (Nr. 17, 1892) by Ahlfeld is Große's principal source. Complete hymn-edition: **C. Ahlfeld,** *Sämtliche Lieder des Johann Heermann* (Leipzig, 1882).

¹⁷⁰ The OCR breaks at Heermann's remarriage and the account of his ministry through the wars; the reconstruction preserves the biographical content that Große gives, with brackets marking uncertain elements.

¹⁷¹ **John 11:3** — Martha and Mary's word to Jesus about the sick Lazarus: *"Lord, behold, he whom thou lovest is sick."* Heermann's writing the words on his own bed makes his last sickness a lived-out Lazarus-typology.

¹⁷² **The Alexandrine** (12-syllable iambic line, cesura at 6) was introduced into German poetry by **Martin Opitz** (1597–1639); Heermann's *"O Gott, du frommer Gott"* is one of the first sustained applications of the Alexandrine to the sacred hymn. **The Sapphic ode** (11-syllable line, from Sappho of Lesbos via Horace) had been used by Melanchthon in Latin school-verse; Heermann's *"Herzliebster JEsu, was hast du verbrochen?"* is the first great Sapphic in the German sacred hymn — the paradigmatic vehicle of the Sapphic in German Passion-tide devotion, famously set by Bach in the *St. Matthew Passion* (BWV 244) as chorale No. 3.

¹⁷³ ***"Herzliebster JEsu, was hast du verbrochen?"*** — Heermann's Passion-hymn, first printed 1630. Text at EG No. 81. Bach's setting in the *St. Matthew Passion* (BWV 244) has fixed the hymn as one of the paradigm Passion-chorales of the Lutheran tradition. Compare Nikolai's "Wachet auf" (BWV 140) and "Wie schön leuchtet" (BWV 1) — Nikolai and Heermann are the two principal 17th-c. Silesian-Lutheran hymnist forerunners of Bach's cantata-passion cycles.

¹⁷⁴ **Johann Arndt** (1555–1621) — Ballenstedt-born Lutheran pastor and mystical-devotional writer. Standard modern biography: Hans Schneider, *Johann Arndt. Studien zu einer Biographie* (Marburg, 2011); an older biography that Große is here drawing on is Wilhelm Koepp, *Johann Arndt. Eine Untersuchung über die Mystik im Luthertum* (Berlin, 1912). The ***Vier Bücher vom Wahren Christentum*** (Books 1–4: 1605–1610; Books 5–6 added posthumously) is the founding-text of Lutheran devotional theology.

¹⁷⁵ **Arndt's Quedlinburg-Neustadt ministry and the exorcism-controversy** — the Duke of Anhalt was moving in a Calvinist direction, requiring pastors to give up the exorcism-formula at infant baptism (which Reformed theology considered a Roman-Catholic remnant, but which Lutheran Orthodoxy defended as adiaphoron). Arndt's refusal cost him the position and set the pattern of his lifelong Lutheran-confessional resistance to Calvinist pressure.

¹⁷⁶ **Johann Gerhard** (1582–1637), Jena theologian, the greatest of the Lutheran Orthodox dogmaticians. His friendship with Arndt — begun at Quedlinburg — is the fixed reference-point of Arndt's confessional-Lutheran standing: Gerhard's support of *Wahres Christentum* protected the book against the strict-Wittenberg attacks. Große treats Gerhard in his own portrait at §11 below.

¹⁷⁷ The Wittenberg and Tübingen strict-Lutheran attacks on Arndt — accusations of *Schwärmerei* and *heresy* — are one of the fundamental confessional-Lutheran controversies of the seventeenth century. The literature is enormous; key modern studies are Winfried Zeller, *Der Protestantismus des 17. Jahrhunderts* (Bremen, 1962), and Wolfgang Sommer, *Politik, Theologie und Frömmigkeit im Luthertum der frühen Neuzeit* (Göttingen, 1999).

¹⁷⁸ **Johann Gerhard's later withholding approbation from the 3rd book** — despite his personal defense of Arndt — signals the confessional-Lutheran caution about the more mystical passages of Book 3. Gerhard's own *Meditationes sacrae* (1606) — a devotional book that shows the Arndtian influence tempered with Wittenberg-Orthodox discipline — is the practical answer to the Arndt-difficulty from within the Lutheran Orthodoxy circle.

¹⁷⁹ **Arndt's dying-vision of Christ's glory** — a paradigm of Lutheran-Orthodox visionary devotion, preserved in the funeral-address and the eye-witness accounts by his wife and by Johann Gerhard. The line *"Now I have seen it"* — followed by 1 Cor. 2:9 — sets a high-water mark of *seventeenth-century Lutheran deathbed devotion,* one of the founding-texts of the *evangelischer Sterbegebet* tradition (compare Luther's own at Eisleben, n. 51 above).

¹⁸⁰ **Bartholomäus Ziegenbalg** (1682–1719) and **Heinrich Plütschau** (1677–1747), the first Lutheran missionaries to Tranquebar in South India (from 1706), translated the *Wahres Christentum* Book 1 into **Tamil** for the newly-converted Lutheran Tamil Christians. Große's note here is a Halle-Missionshaus cross-reference: the Halle Waisenhaus tradition of Francke sent the Tranquebar mission, and the Tranquebar mission carried Arndt into Tamil — a Lutheran-devotional trajectory from Braunschweig 1605 to Tranquebar 1706.

¹⁸¹ **Valentin Weigel** (1533–1588), Saxon pastor and mystical writer whose posthumous 1612ff. writings — read only after Arndt had used them — became one of the sources of the 17th-c. **Weigelianismus** movement. Weigel's mystical-pantheism was decisively rejected by the strict-Wittenberg Lutheran Orthodoxy. Große is here careful to note that Arndt used only an anonymous 1606 manuscript (not knowing the author) — a defense against the strict-Wittenberg accusation of *Weigelianism.*

¹⁸² **Albrecht Ritschl** (1822–1889), Göttingen theologian and founder of the *Ritschl-school* — the liberal-Protestant theological school that dominated late-19th-c. German Protestantism. Ritschl's rejection of both Roman-Catholicism and of the *fellowship-mysticism* of Lutheran Orthodoxy (including Arndt) is one of the fundamental conflicts of Große's own age. Große's polemical alignment here — *"Ritschl and his school... smell Catholicism there straight away"* — is a characteristic late-19th-c. confessional-Lutheran anti-Ritschlian move. The reference to **Haβbagen's Introduction** — probably F. Th. J. von Hahn, though the OCR is unclear — is to a *Predigt der Kirche*-series volume in which the anti-Arndt-Ritschlian position is set out.
