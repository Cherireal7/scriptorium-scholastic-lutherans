# Translator's Note

## 1. The text-base

This working English translation follows the **posthumous Latin edition** of Balduin's pastoral-casuistical *summa*, published at **Wittenberg by Paul Helwig, and printed at Frankfurt am Main, in the year M.DC.XXVIII (1628)**, under the full title ***Tractatus Luculentus, Posthumus, Toti Reipublicae Christianae Utilissimus, De Materiâ rarissimè antehac enucleatâ, Casibus nimirum Conscientiae, summo studio elaboratus, à Friderico Balduino, S.S. Theolog. Doctore et Professore*** — *"A Luminous Posthumous Treatise, most useful to the whole Christian Commonwealth, on a matter most rarely hitherto unfolded, namely the Cases of Conscience, elaborated with the highest care by Friedrich Balduin, Doctor and Professor of Sacred Theology."*

The book is issued **posthumously** — Balduin died at Wittenberg on 1 May 1627; the *Tractatus* was seen through the press by his widow and heirs and dedicated by them on 1 July 1628 to **Landgrave Georg of Hesse-Darmstadt**. A **preface** by the **Dean and Collegium of the Theological Faculty of Wittenberg University** stands as the collegial imprimatur, situating Balduin's *Tractatus* against the papal casuistry it was written to replace.

The source-text used here is the Google Books digitisation of a library copy (a 90.5 MB PDF, VD17 3:307763U), from which we have extracted the per-page OCR text via **PyMuPDF** — yielding 75,390 lines of running late-humanist Wittenberg Latin. The OCR is heavier in ligature drift than the 16th-century Leipzig fount of our Kirchner *Methodica* volume (long-*s* → *f*, *u* ↔ *n*, *ct* / *æ* / *ff* ligatures corrupted, marginal citations interpolated into body text), and we correct these silently against the underlying source-image.

## 2. What this book actually is

Balduin's *Tractatus* is the **first single-author Lutheran casuistry** — that is, the first attempt within the Wittenberg orthodoxy of the early 17th century to treat *the whole territory of moral theology as a system of concrete cases of conscience*, on Scripture alone, without recourse to the Roman *Sacramentum poenitentiae* framework. The genre had until then belonged almost entirely to the Roman confessors' tradition, worked from the 13th century onwards by **Raymond of Peñafort**, **John of Freiburg**, **Astesanus of Asti**, **Angelus de Clavasio**, **Bartholomew of San Concordio**, **Antoninus of Florence**, and their successors, and refined for the Counter-Reformation confessional practice by the Jesuits **Francisco de Toledo**, **Juan Azor**, **Martin Becanus**, **Jakob Gretser**, and **Valerius Reginaldus**.

Balduin's project — announced at length in the widow's dedicatoria and in the Faculty's preface — is **to redo the whole discipline** on the *unica Scripturae norma* alone. He organises the material not by the medieval *septem peccata capitalia* or by the sacraments of penance, but on a **fourfold division by object of the human action**:

- **Liber I** — *On conscience itself and cases in general*: the definition, seat, and syllogistic structure of the conscience; general categories of case.
- **Liber II** — *On the actions of man toward God and religion*: the First Table, cases of faith, cultus, prayer, oaths, vows, sabbath, and Church-fellowship.
- **Liber III** — *On the actions of man toward celestial spirits*: cases concerning the veneration of angels, demonic operations (astrology, magic, divination, spectres, obsession), and the internal spiritual assaults of suggestion, temptation, and despair.
- **Liber IV** — *On the actions of men toward human affairs*: the Second Table, cases concerning the body, the soul, temporal goods, and the specific vocations — magistrate, minister, householder, subject.

Balduin died before the *Tractatus* could be published; the widow and heirs, assisted by the Wittenberg Faculty, saw it through the press. The book was reprinted repeatedly through 1706 without scholarly revision — a fixture of the 17th-century Wittenberg curriculum and of Lutheran pastoral training for four generations.

## 3. Editorial conventions

**Liber-numbering.** Balduin's **four Libri** are rendered as **Liber I–IV** in Roman numerals, following his own subtitles. Each Liber divides into *Capita* (chapters), each Caput into numbered *Casus* (cases of conscience). Each *Casus* opens with a *Prooemium* (statement of the question) and works case-argument style through the objections, distinctions, and resolution.

**Case format.** Every *Casus* is rendered as a numbered `Case` heading; the bolded statement of the case-question follows the Latin *Prooemium* form and precedes the argumentative body. Balduin's marginal glosses on each case (*In quo consistit haec quaestio?*, *De quibus hic disserendum?*, *Quid iuris?*) are preserved as inline italic tags at the point where they appear in the source margin.

**Latin and Greek.** The Latin of the *Tractatus* is preserved *verbatim* in italics wherever Balduin is quoting a Father, a Council, a Scholastic definition, or a legal-canonical formula — each is followed by a plain-English gloss on first occurrence. Greek words retained by Balduin in Roman characters (***synderesis***, ***syneidesis***, ***catechumenus***, ***adiaphoron***) are preserved without transliteration, italicised, and glossed on first occurrence.

**Bible citations.** Book abbreviations are normalised to the modern English standard (Rom., 1 Cor., Matt.); chapter and verse numbers are silently corrected against the underlying reference where Balduin's citation differs from modern versification. Scripture quotations are **conformed to the King James Version (1611)** in the audit pass following translation, per the same convention as our Kirchner *Methodica* volume.

**Bold and italics.** **Bold** marks Bible citations, Balduin's enumerated *primo / secundo / tertio* points, and the load-bearing thesis in each case-resolution. *Italics* mark Latin quotations, Greek transliterations, and technical scholastic vocabulary retained in the original tongue. Section dividers use the aldus-leaf ornament — ❦ —.

**Polemical labels.** Balduin's polemical distinctions — ***Pontificii***, ***Sophistae***, ***Casistae***, ***Jesuitae***, ***Anabaptistae***, ***Sacramentarii***, ***Enthusiastae***, ***Calviniani*** — are preserved as he uses them, and rendered by the natural English equivalents (**Papists**, **Sophists**, **Casuists**, **Jesuits**, etc.). Balduin's frequent target is the *modern Papal casuistry* of the Jesuit apparatus of confession, not the *medieval Roman casuistry* of Raymond and Astesanus, which he cites more sympathetically as a genre-precedent even where he judges its decisions wrong.

**Silent OCR corrections.** Long-*s* → *f*, *u* ↔ *n*, and *ct* / *æ* / *ff* ligature corruptions, dropped Greek diacritics, marginal-note text interpolated into the main body by the OCR flow, running-header text intruded into body lines, and line-break hyphen artefacts from PyMuPDF's extraction are silently corrected against the source-image. Substantive editorial decisions that touch the doctrine or the case-resolution are noted in the endnotes.

**Back-matter indexes.** The original volume closes with three Latin indexes — **Index I** of Scripture-citations, **Index II** of Fathers and Authors, **Index III** of Res et Verborum (subject index). These are not translated in this English edition; the reader is served instead by the running case-headings and by the modern search capabilities of the digital text.

## 4. Reader navigation

The book contains **four Libri** with approximately fifty *Capita* and two hundred fifty numbered *Casus* across the whole domain of Christian moral theology, following Balduin's own fourfold-by-object scheme rather than the Decalogue or the *septem peccata capitalia* of Roman casuistry. A full **Table of Contents** follows this Note. Each Liber opens with a divider ornament and a recto chapter opener, and closes with the same before the next Liber begins. The formal case-resolutions appear as numbered sub-sections within each Caput. This volume is the **first of our pastoral-casuistry** series, complementing our earlier Wittenberg dogmatic (Kirchner *Methodica*, Selnecker *Symbolorum*) and catechetical (Kirchner *Enchiridion*) volumes.

# Dedication

*Epistola Dedicatoria — the widow, children, and heirs of Friedrich Balduin, of blessed memory, to Georg, Landgrave of Hesse-Darmstadt.*

**To the Most Illustrious, Most Serene Prince and Lord, Lord Georg, Landgrave of Hesse, Count of Katzenelnbogen, Dietz, Ziegenhain, and Nidda, &c. — our most clement Lord — we offer prayers and most humble, devoted subjection.**

*Most Illustrious and Most Serene Prince, our most clement Lord:*

**Two things above all are needful to a Christian man, and most worthy to be known.** The one is *the knowledge of God*; the other, *the knowledge of self*. Of the first we shall not now speak at length, for it is uncontroverted and to all men manifestly necessary. **For as all created things were made for man's sake, so man himself was constituted to know rightly the God who is his Creator — to worship him, to love him, and to celebrate him:** *"Every one that is called by my name: for I have created him for my glory, I have formed him; yea, I have made him"* (**Isa. 43:7**), that we may look up to heaven and understand what argues that we ought to worship the Maker of heaven and earth with pure and unmixed heart. So Lactantius admonishes, *Divinarum Institutionum* lib. 6, cap. 1, at the beginning.

But *on the latter head* — the knowledge of a man's own self — some preface may be permitted, most illustrious Prince. **For the matter stands thus: though scarce any of the nobler disciplines fails to claim for itself that "know-thyself" once inscribed at Delphi as fallen down from heaven** — the *Logician* recognises through discursive reason his own eminence over the brutes and a certain divine kinship with God himself; the *Ethician*, weighing his life on the balance of virtue, becomes the censor of himself and the moderator of his morals; the *Physician* and, together with him, the *Anatomist*, contemplating the wondrous fabric of the human body, finds a *microcosm* in himself; **all in their own way, by a laudable emulation, become admirers of themselves and heralds of the divine praises, when they find in the body, in the mind, arguments of the divine power, goodness, and wisdom that are plainly to be marvelled at** — **yet none of these yields to our sacred Theological discipline**, which, being occupied with the eternal salvation of man, could scarcely fail to bring man to the knowledge of himself. For it proposes life and death, heaven and hell; and therefore it must needs display man to himself, since every man carries either heaven or hell in his bosom, according as he is disposed.

**A twofold self-knowledge does man draw from the study of Theology:** the one by reason of his *defects*; the other by reason of his *acts*. The **first** arises from the consideration of *nature deprived* — nature which, in man, displays the mask of Satan, alien from God, a stranger in [God's] mysteries, an enemy in the divine precepts, unable to find the way to life or to walk in it without a Guide. The **second** springs from the consideration of *nature restored*. For though all men may know themselves in some measure by their actions, properly speaking this belongs to those who, illuminated by the Spirit of God and endowed with the gift of faith, draw near to God, acknowledge his majesty, love his commands, and study to order their life according to them.

But **this study often languishes through the weakness of the flesh**, and the recognition of God grows infirm; and it is the condition of many that the nearer they seem to approach God, the further they appear (in the outcome) to have receded from him. **Therefore every man has need of daily self-knowledge and self-examination** — which is done through the *scrutiny of conscience*. **In conscience, a man holds his own court: there he is himself Actor, Witness, Scribe, and Judge; a court in which justice always flourishes, which none can escape and none decline; for conscience always assumes, and always concludes.** For our *synderesis* — that practical habit whereby a man is rendered disposed to give assent to the first practical principles — supplies the *proposition*; and *synesis*, conscience, hereafter assumes, and at last concludes. So the proposition in the mind of King Oedipus was: *"He who commits incest and parricide commits a shameful act, and one worthy of punishment."* His conscience assumed: *"But thou hast committed it."* And it concluded this accusation: *"Therefore thou art worthy of punishment."* Such was the grief that followed this syllogism in the mind of Oedipus, king of the Thebans, that, impatient of the shame he had heaped upon himself, he tore out his own eyes for very sorrow. So indeed Melanchthon observes.

For — as Chrysostom writes in his fourth sermon on Lazarus — *"An earthly father, when he has once or twice or thrice or four times chastised his son, should he see him remain incorrigible, disinherits and casts him out of the house, cutting him off from the family; but not so conscience: though it warn once, twice, thrice, or a thousand times without avail, yet it never withdraws nor ceases to warn again — even unto the last breath — whether at home, in the crossways, at meat, in the market, upon the journey; and often in dreams it represents to us the images and spectres of our sins."*

But not gladly do those men attend this court who are always occupied with vain speculations, whether of things divine or things human, never seriously considering true piety or the ordering of their own life — of whom the number in this world is ever great. For as Bernard writes in *On the Interior House*, cap. 51: *"Many have sought knowledge without conscience; of whom Ecclesiastes says: 'In much wisdom is much grief.'"* Wherefore also Augustine, in the *De Spiritu et Anima* cap. 51, commends by many arguments the knowledge that pertains to each man's conscience, writing among other things: *"Since no knowledge is better than that whereby a man knows himself — let us discuss our own thoughts, our doings, and our works. For what does it profit us if we investigate subtly and comprehend efficaciously the natures of all things, and understand not ourselves? Let us therefore examine what we have done, in what manner we ought to have done it, in what manner we have done it; let us consider what we are about to do — whether it be according to God."* And a little after: *"It is necessary that day by day we call our life to judgment; and that we examine what we have done by night and by day; how much more alacritous we have been than usual in doing good, and how much more constant than usual in overcoming evil; whether by some work of ours we have been supplanted by the snares of the enemy; and how, by judgment, we may be able to guard against the future fraud of that deception that has already passed — that neither the assailing temptation, coming upon us unwary, may cast us down to the evil work, nor the present negligence, when we are indiscreet in a good work, hinder us."*

**In this examination many matters arise which give scruple even to the pious — matters which they call *Cases of Conscience*.** The decisions of these are as necessary in Theological study as the discussions of the articles of faith. For often here (as the proverb runs) *the water sticks*, even in those most exercised in speculative Theology; and unless the scruple be removed, all the subtleties of theological Contemplations seem to be worth not one *as*. **The treatment of the *Casus Conscientiae* is therefore most necessary,** belonging to that part of theological Wisdom which is called *practical*; which, while it looses the knots that bind the conscience, occupies the mind of the pious man not with any idle contemplation but confirms it with a most useful learning of the greatest matters, so that it may withstand the assault of whatever doubts or temptations exercise our life. **This part of Theology is spread through all the actions of all men in every kind of life, wherein our judgment is informed, so that our conscience may have, in every case that occurs, that in which it may find rest.**

*[Portrait plate: Balduin — with a Latin verse-motto below, illegible in OCR.]*

**In this kind of theological study, much labour and time was spent under the Papacy by those (as they call them) *Summists*** who wrote *Sums* on the deciding of cases of conscience. Others called them *Casists*. Among the more notable of these were **Astesanus of Asti**, **Raymond of Peñafort**, **John of Freiburg**, **Hugo of Ripelin**, **Monaldus the Franciscan**, **Bartholomew of San Concordio**, **John of Rider**, **Bernard of Siena**, **Henry Herp**, **Bartholomew Trentini**, **Thomas de Vio [Cajetan]**, **Navarrus [Azpilcueta]**, and the like. But **in many things they obscured this doctrine which they ought to have illustrated**, proposing such **Cases of Conscience** as the mere superstition of Rome had begotten in the age in which the Papal *ἐξουσιαμαχία* — its lust for lording it over consciences — was wont to bind men's consciences. In their other decisions also they gravely erred, because they followed not the word of God (which alone can confirm consciences) but the leadership of Papal decrees. **What service the *Casists* of the Roman party render to the Church in this present age** may be gathered from the writings of **Francisco de Toledo**, **Juan Azor**, **Martin Becanus**, and **Jakob Gretser**; and, not so long since, from the compendious *Praxis of the more difficult Cases of Conscience that frequently occur in the administration of the Sacrament of Penance*, published three years ago by **Father Valerius Reginaldus, the Jesuit**. For these men drew the decisions of the harder cases not from the purest springs of Israel, but from the muddied cisterns of the Scholastics and of others — Thomas Aquinas, Thomas Sanchez, Suarez — and served them up to the credulous populace.

**When therefore Dr. Friedrich Balduin,** *Doctor of Sacred Theology, Professor, Pastor, and Superintendent at this alma Leucorea* [the University of Wittenberg], **our most desired parent, of blessed memory,** had thoroughly perceived and known this **γοητεία** [deception] plainly Antichristian, which the *Casists* have been wont to employ in the deciding of the *Casus Conscientiae*, **he judged it would be to the good of the whole Church if some Theologian should propose the Cases of Conscience according to the sole norm of Scripture** — a labour which we suppose to have been hitherto orthodoxly and methodically performed by no man. This great labour, relying on divine aid, he suffered to be laid upon his own shoulders; in which enterprise **he candidly taught and demonstrated to the youth, from the word of God alone and from the practice of the primitive Church, in what manner the decisions of the Romanists and of the other Adversaries — which lean upon alien principles — differ from ours.**

These lucubrations, now made public property, we humbly dedicate to **Your Illustrious Highness**, most Illustrious and Most Excellent Prince, most Clement Lord — as we thought fitting, partly by that customary law that has descended from our forefathers to the order of letters, whereby of old those who feared to send forth in print the lucubrations of their own wit or of another's, wrote them up to the Chiefs of that age, so that under the patronage of so great a name they might come forth safe and unharmed from the sneering of adversaries; **partly also because the consent of the churches of Saxony and Hesse is commended for the purity of doctrine as thoroughly orthodox even to our own generation.** Besides, it is the candid and ingenuous confession of many, that Your Highness's unfeigned **piety** and *entheus ardor* for preserving and propagating the sincere religion, your **justice**, your remarkable **beneficence**, in the abundance of all things, your **moderation** and **modesty** — by a certain hidden virtue and by the devotion of all sincere-eyed observers — bind to yourself their souls in perpetual reverence.

**Let therefore Your Illustrious Highness receive under your patronage this work, most useful to the whole Church and posthumous of our most desired parent** — for since it is the mind of the giver, rather than the weight of the gift, that is regarded by those who look candidly upon it, these things which we, of Balduin of blessed memory, present to Your Illustrious Highness deserve, at any rate, to be received not with an unjust but with a clement mind.

**May God most benign, the Father of our Lord Jesus Christ, preserve Your Illustrious Highness, together with the most illustrious House and Family of Hesse, as long as possible incolume and flourishing for the utility of Church and Commonwealth — rule it by his Holy Spirit, guard it with the army of angels, augment it with every kind of benedictions, felicitously prosper and fructify it. Amen.**

*Wittenberg, 1 July, in the year of restored salvation M.DC.XXVIII (1628).*

Your Illustrious Highness's most subject and humble servants,

**The widow, children, and heirs of D. Friedrich Balduin, of pious memory.**

# Preface

*Praefatio — the Dean and Collegium of the Theological Faculty of the University of Wittenberg to the pious, sincere, and theologically-studious Reader, especially the Preacher, with due honour and respect: — Grace from the fountain of graces, **Christ Jesus**, we entreat.*

**The profession of Cases of Conscience, friendly Reader, is most necessary and of daily use — since nothing whatever ought to be done with a wavering conscience,** and no employment is more arduous, no error more perilous, than in the giving of *Consilia* and in the forming of Consciences. As this labour requires, all in one grasp, **wit, judgment, memory of many matters, a tranquil conscience triumphant over the assault of many temptations** — so it demands to be undertaken and handled by a Theologian who is thoroughly Lutheran (*γνήσιος* — genuinely so), one who has joined to sharpest judgment a varied learning and the experience of many years. Such a man has been the wish of many hitherto. **For though the gravest *Consilia* of our Theologians on many important cases stand extant here and there** — some of them collected by **Bidenbach**, **Deutel**, and others — yet even they have not unfolded the countless cases with certainty of science or specific determination.

But **just as the Jurist and the Physician not only supply *Consilia* to those seated in a Case for the time given** (and insert them into great volumes) but also, by another labour, arrange in fixed orders whatever may be answered concerning similar cases which they can probably foresee — **so much more, in Theology, would it be worth the effort, besides *Decisions* wrung from Theologians *pro re nata***, to propose an accurate synopsis of every kind of *Case of Conscience* and to refer these, with their solutions demonstrated, to some Systematic analogy. And truly the Theologians of former times, and the modern Doctors also in the Pontifical Church — to whom neither wit, nor leisure, nor prolix age, nor libraries with copies to help them, nor other supports, were lacking — **could have relieved us of this burden**. Instead they issued thick volumes on this Practical Theology, and even now Professors of Cases of Conscience are maintained on great stipends. **Yet in many of their Disputations they seem not to satisfy themselves, much less to be able to give safe counsel to the Consciences of others.** For **they recede in great measure from the incorruptible Word of God, upon which alone Conscience may safely lean**: they have sold the freedom of writing to the oaths sworn to the Pope; they commend *blind obedience* — as **Antonius Possevino of the Society of Jesus**, in his *Bibliotheca Selecta* lib. 5 cap. 61, does above all, with his hot counsels concerning the imperium of Majesties in temporal things; that no book has been publicly abolished, indeed committed to the fire, but that they do not concede the dispensation of the Pope to be made — so that even so they are not safe in conscience. Which if we were to pursue with due action, our writing would be not a preface but a treatise on beyond the case. Let us name one, or another only:

**Observe publicly what superstitions these teachers of *Consilia* are wont to inculcate on wretched consciences.** In almost all necessities, when the salvation of body and soul is despaired of, they at least leave this help: that men *invoke S. Mary* — to say nothing yet of the other saints, of pilgrimages, of relics, of vows, of communications of merits, of holy water, of the various exorcisms, or of any such things.

*"It has been handed down to memory,"* says the Jesuit **Tympius** (*On the rewards of those who greet the Deipara Virgin in Angelic words, and recite the Rosary composed of many Angelic salutations and sweet-smelling roses*), — *"that **Blanche Queen of France** being sterile and unfruitful, having been of the Lord (from whom she had expected the remedy), received the Rosary, not without the prize of a happy conception, since it was Louis whom she brought forth — a King than whom none among the French was more holy in birth. But that **the Albigensian heresy was extinguished, that in the age of the Lord peace was established among Christian Princes, that the audacity of enemies was suppressed, that the discord of dissenters was pacified, that incurable diseases were cured, that certain persons were recalled to life** — that these must be received in acceptance of the Rosary, is the sentence of grave and learned men."* — So he, which he has from P. Johann. Bonifacius in his *History of Virgins* lib. 1 cap. 17. **The same Alanus, alumnus of St. Dominic, in his book *On the Rosary* reports** a certain man who had bound himself to the Devil by a bond written in his own blood, taking this counsel: that he entered the little chapel of the Rosary, prostrated himself before the virginal image, and addressed the Deipara in these words: *"O sweetest Virgin, most beneficent, at the prayers of the most miserable, receive these prayers which so bitter a mourning compels me to pour out. Behold, I offer thee the Rosary — that Rosary, I say, of which thy preachers commemorate so many, so precious things, which thou art wont to impart to thy suppliants. By the magnificent promises of these I am incited; thy name, Deipara, I implore, and I present myself to thee, by thy benignity to be refreshed from this present misery, which is such that the very memory of it shudders. To me — alas! — by some furious knot I have bound to the enemy, most senselessly obliged, — a detestable pact, signed with my own blood and confirmed, — it is easy for thee to break: here, here reason has arrived, most holy virgin, nor am I to withdraw before I see that suggested schedule restored. The evil demon holds it as a pledge, and boasts to be its lord in perpetuity. Then at last shall I judge my soul snatched away, when I see the Chirograph annulled, blotted out."* — To him thus persevering, and repeatedly gazing on Mary's image, it seemed that from the virginal hand fell that page which he had given to the Demon, witness of a perpetual servitude. **He took it up to himself, recognised the hand, saw it undone; and, persuaded of Christ's benefit through his mother, began to worship the religion of the Rosary most ardently — by whose exercise he had escaped so great a peril.**

From the same Alanus is recited another example: that there was a certain desperate man, who by no consolations of the learned, by no allegation of examples (that greatest of sinners received into grace — **Manasseh**, **Magdalene**, **Matthew**, **Paul**, the **Thief on the cross**), could be raised to hope of salvation. How truly, and by what counsel, he returned to a saner mind — please to hear it in the very words of the Author; which are so full of superstition, indeed of blasphemy, that a sober Reader may judge for himself. For thus they run: *"When I had surveyed the whole state of the mind, 'To none,' I said, 'O desperate man, art thou now safe; but one thing alone remains — the Rosary, that thou mayest recite it — of which the third medicine still is not, in one confirmed in evil. **Christ**, the very fount of all goods, is prayed to; that prayer which himself handed down to his disciples is prayed; the **Virgin Deipara** is saluted with the words of the Angel and of Elisabeth; and, as interpretress for reconciling the Son, is applied — those prayers of which the Pater Noster is the beginning, that they may the more religiously be sent forth, are repeated ten times, or the Ave Maria fifteen times; and 'the fruit that is blessed' — because of whom our works are fruitful — is added. So many and such wondrous Roses to this thou must add for thy most bitter sickness.' Thus at last to a man so despairing I insinuated the matter. Gravely and with an unjust animus is it to be borne, brother, if the study which is my counsel thou dost refuse to accept, and my prayer to look down upon — that one prayer, whereby thy salvation entire consists. Now, therefore, whenever thou dost profess thyself a Christian, and we posit that thou art a Catholic, thou canst not not-honour the mother of God, and acknowledge her divine efficacy and preach her Power. Confess that thou dost believe what the mother [-Church] the Church believes: that the memory of the Deipara is not ungrateful to her. Do not therefore refuse to me this — which is her easiest, and to the Virgin most acceptable: confess that thou art one of those enrolled in her society, and recite the Rosary. Now, no other remedy is left to thee, or this one is left. I do not doubt but that thou wilt have some alleviation of so great a sickness, worn out and almost broken as thou art.'* — He accepted the offer, and did somewhat with a certain piety of feeling. Not many months had passed, when there followed such a change as they would scarcely believe, had they known the man's obstinacy. *Of his life the progress in the sanctity of morals was such, that not the smallest [reason] to regret the received Rosary; and inasmuch as to the solid hope which he had conceived in soul, right acts he did not cease to add, he discharged the duty of a Christian man.*"

Here are the counsels which were unknown to **S. Paul** — who nevertheless *announced the whole counsel of God*; unknown to **S. Peter** — who spoke ταύτῃ τῇ πέτρᾳ [upon this rock]: these are the counsels instilled into consciences, which make God inexorable except through S. Mary. **Where then is the one Advocate and Mediator, Christ?** These are the *Consilia* applied in the most desperate cases, which if granted, we do not see how they can escape the absurdity: that **the Election of the sons of God is made not in the beloved Son alone, but also in S. Mary**. For we ask that these words of **Francisco Labata** of the Society of Jesus, preacher (*Apparatus Concionum*, tit. *de Maria* prop. 4), be weighed on the most exact scales of judgment: *"How many thousands there have been, and now are, who by now would have been damned to Gehenna, or would be about to be damned, unless the mother of God had interceded for them."* — So he. Whence it follows that God, in his eternal counsel, has decreed to save many thousands of men not by the intervention of the Son alone, but also of the Virgin Deipara; and thus, to the immutable certitude of Election, the intercession of Mary also has concurred. For as God executes the work of salvation, so has he decreed from eternity to execute it. But, as this man writes, by the intervention of the blessed Virgin God saves many thousands, who would otherwise be damned. Therefore they owe salvation not to Christ alone, but to S. Virgin — as to a *proximate cause*, as to an *immediate mediatrix*. **Does this not diminish the glory of the Son, while the excellence of the Mother is amplified?** — which **Bonaventura** on *Sent.* 3, dist. 5, q. 2 bade us take care to avoid; we commit this to be judged by any zealous seeker after the genuine and primary Catholic truth.

**There is added yet another:** that certain *Consilia* of the Pontificians often sound the alarm, and with regicides, seditions, and perjuries, entangle most wretched Consciences. Which could be demonstrated not so much by our words as by the words, protestations, and documents of the saner Pontificians themselves, if the plan of our writing bore this. **Let there return to memory the books of P. Juan Mariana *On the King***, wherein the history of Jacques Clément (who, having been informed by the Theologians whom he had consulted that a Tyrant might lawfully be put to death — O the *conscientious* Theologians! whose sons in spirit are these? — transfixed **King Henry III of France** with a knife smeared with noxious herbs). In which books are taught such *Consilia* as, had they been Christian and congruous with the Gospel, would not have needed the prohibition and the public abolition of those books which was performed in the Provincial Congregation of France. **What Cardinal Bellarmine has published against Barclay** in a special writing on the case of the Pope's power *in temporals*, is known to all. Of that treatise of Bellarmine's, **Daniel Chamier** in *Panstratia* T. 1, lib. 4, cap. 14 recites this: *"What more? Not satisfied with the sentence of those who approve, this very year in which I was writing this, Bellarmine's treatise against Barclay *On the power of the supreme Pontiff in temporal matters* was condemned by the same Senate with a most weighty edict, which was even struck in type — by which edict this very sentence of the Theologians, which acknowledges that power not except *indirectly*, was condemned as a *torch of sedition, spring of rebellion*."* — So he. Let also come to memory **Francisco Suárez**, Theologian of the Society of Jesus, whose *Defence of the Catholic Faith* — wherein such pernicious dogmas and hot counsels are spread abroad — that that volume was by public decree, by the hand of the executioner, committed to the fire on 27 June 1614; and yet had been commended by the censures and approvals of *D. Alfonso de Castelo Branco*, Bishop of Coimbra &c., *D. Fernando Martinez Mascarenhas*, Bishop of Algarve (who in his Censure [says] that *"the kingdom of the Gauls in the Roman Church is set as a bulwark, and its allegiance is pledged by that fact, that Clovis, first King of the Franks, most Christian, just before dying, redeemed the Roman Pontiff with a royal crown"*), *D. Martin Alfonso de Mello*, Bishop of Lamego; *Johannes Alvarus*, of the Society of Jesus, frequent Preacher in Portugal, and many others.

**Such things must necessarily follow, if men depart from the Word of God and approach the most arrogant sentence of the Pontifex Boniface VIII**, which he promulgated in the extravagant *Unam Sanctam*, on Majesty and Obedience — where by scriptures wrenched not without violence, Boniface VIII so concludes: *"Furthermore we declare, say, define, and pronounce that to be subject to the Roman Pontiff is, of the necessity of salvation, [required] of every human creature."* — So Boniface VIII, of whom **Platina** thus speaks: *"Boniface dies in this manner, having tried to inflict on Emperors, Kings, Princes, nations, peoples, terror rather than religion; to give kingdoms and to take them away, to add and to redeem them at his own arbitrary judgment."* — Which counsels of Boniface, though they did not please his successor **Clement V** (from whom the kingdom of France was struck out, as is clear from the extravagant *On Privilege*), yet that these Pontiffs erred is plain to any who examines by primary faculty the more restless bishops.

**Finally, how much utility of merits certain Papal *Consilia* have** — this is clear from many things. Let there be considered the doctrine of divorce *quoad totum et thorum*, where the innocent party is confined in a divine bond; the toleration of concubinage, if money is collected out of it; the concession of brothels; the process against heretics not yet convicted; the disputations *on faith kept with infidels or heretics*; the penalties of adultery, incest, sodomy, and other crimes in the Canons; and — to add one particular — the judgment on so many *dispensations* of the Supreme Pontiffs, which do not always render the sons of the Pontiff safe in Conscience, as **Cardinal Toletus** manifestly writes in his *Cases of Conscience* lib. 5 cap. 8, in these words: *"The second rule, notwithstanding the rights of the Councils: he who by the Pope's dispensation holds several benefices — however many, and however granted — is safe *in itself* in Conscience. But this is the way, that in similar cases [the language of buying] the same is not sold in the whole price, but at least in the half — the acquisition being of the benefits themselves."* Some conditions of this society of offices he warns are necessary: (1) that above ecclesiastical offices Christians cannot make such similar societies. *"For as this is,"* he says, — *"unless it be that Rome is our concern"* (here let the Reader note that at Rome, by this Cardinal's own testimony, it was elsewhere a matter of usury) — *"but this condition he denies to be necessary to the intrinsic reason of the contract, and perhaps it could be done above another regal office."* Yet because the Pope forbids it, — not as Toletus doubts, as appears — Conscience speaks. (2) That partners be not admitted beyond the half of the value of those offices — which condition he holds necessary also, not from the contract, but from Papal precept (Paul IV), as Toletus says. But about this condition, **Martinus Fornarius of the Society of Jesus**, who wrote the annotations on Toletus, reports that by *Pius IV* it has been abolished. For thus he writes: *"Note that these things established by Paul IV, of which here [we speak], had been afterwards revoked by Pius IV as he progressed..."* by the same Toletus. Whence contracts concerning the same essence, which under Paul IV in Conscience could not be made, are in the best light under Pius IV. **So there is a Conscience laxer, and less removed from the word of God and the nature of contracts?** And where is the Custodian of the offices? Where the rigour of the ancient priests? Where the sentiment of unmoved justice in the Moral Law? Are Consciences so left to jest? So do they at the authority of a man immediately acquiesce? — as **Tannerus** also at the Colloquium of Ratisbon said: it is the easiest thing for a meek, obedient, and Christian man, to follow in doubt the sentence of his Magistrate, though he perchance err — not indeed retaining the doubt whether this or that be lawful to himself, but the doubt being laid aside. For as the people in political matters (that the Republic may be well constituted) are held in doubt to acquiesce in the sentence of their Magistrate: so also (by the same reason of things useful and necessary) they must in doubt conform themselves to their superiors.

**Is this to inform, or rather (as that man suggests) to slay consciences and remove them altogether?** Does not a Jew or a heretic, following the sentence of his superior, use a good conscience? Was a proselyte, following the judgment of the Pharisees, excused? — so far, indeed, that Christ openly asserts him to be *twice more the son of Gehenna* (Matt. 23:15). Let their *Consilia* therefore be left to their own Authors, and committed to the judgment of God and of the Church. For us it is certain that they are occupied with prejudices, led by affections, err in principles, nor are they exercised in the paroxysms of temptations. For though in the choice of the state of Celibacy they feel with **Francis** their own stimulations (whence they write on it in the Cases of Conscience such things that a sober man should be ashamed, and which more instruct in vices than truly speak divinely) — yet those profound Spiritual temptations, of the kind that exercised **David**, **Paul**, and other Saints of God, they do not seem to have experienced. But what will such a man suggest, when tempted himself? What counsel will he give — the naked Speculatist? The graver [temptation] is the [temptation] of judgment and of practice.

**But neither in the Counsels of the Calvinists does our soul come to rest** — since they too often treat matters cruelly (as they cannot deny), and dare at times to temper the divine providence with human wisdom. We say nothing of this, that so little do they help afflicted Consciences and those anxious about their salvation, that rather, from their own principles concerning the number of the saved and the damned, of the *voluntas signi* differing from the *beneplacitum*, by particular syllogisms of general and singular Election ... by simple paradoxes, they drive the tempted to despair, and open the door to Satan's assaults.

**Therefore great thanks do our Churches owe to God**, that he has raised up the Reverend, most Illustrious, and most Excellent Sir **Friedrich Balduin**, D.D. of Sacred Theology, Senior Professor, Pastor and Superintendent of this Church, deserving Elector-Assessor of the Consistory, our most desired parent — and that he gave him a Roman spirit, and other things which the work required, that he might issue this **Work on Cases of Conscience, systematically written, most helpful, truly needful in our times.** Nothing here of superstition, nothing of servility, nothing of cruelty, no adulation for the Pope — but *simplicity and truth* are set forth. **Come therefore hither, whatever kind of Reader thou art**, with whatever scruples thou art afflicted — thou canst be satisfied. In this our labour is fulfilled to the glory of God and the profit of the Church.

**We testify moreover that we have seen and preserve the *autograph* of the pious Author, of most blessed memory, from which this book has been transcribed like some Cycnean hymn** — with certain markers added by a Studiosus of Theology, most useful, and even between the lines, whose eye is compared to that of Argus; interposed from the same Author or from other Theologians' *Consilia*.

**We uniquely venerate God, that he may grant peace to his most afflicted Church, sound Counsels to Christian Magistrates, liberty and integrity to Consciences, patience to the afflicted, and to us finally all the prizes of this present and of the future life. Amen.**

*Published at Wittenberg, in the year 1628, on the very Kalends of May [1 May 1628] — on which day in the year immediately preceding [1 May 1627] the most Excellent Author of this golden labour was snatched from this life, or rather from mortality, and called into the celestial Academy, that region of the vision of the living.*

## On Friedrich Balduin

*Fridericus Balduinus, Theologian by far the most famous — On the Cases of Conscience — An Epigram.*

Whoever ye be, whose minds are stirred with joys, and who desire the tranquil light of piety —
Let each look to that pillar of the mind that keeps the breast pure from stains of soul;
For sober devotion at the altar of God, and the sleepless watch of the true worshipper,
Trample down the base joy of crime, and put to rout the wily enemy — no idle vanquisher;
The soul is scarcely called back from the trap of the wicked, when it whets its sting for evil ways;
For the mind hath within itself a foreknowing lodging that ripens under the flame of conscience,
And now again teaches the pious what is safe, guards, judges, and sits in its own court —
Itself Actor, Witness, Scribe, and Judge. — But truly, when clear thought is disturbed,
When error steers the ship, and the shameful path deflects it, — the pure life shudders,
The muddied [wave] disturbs the wayfarers; such, on the darkling sea,
When the winds rage, is the little skiff which weariness afflicts, the tempest presses down,
Whose course is torn away, whose helm broken — she clings to unknown reefs, while the vast night
Of the black Tethys shakes her on every side with the hoary wave, and the hard Auster,
Arbiter of the storms; unless the mild star of Castor of Iber, seated high, tremble on the grateful sail-yards,
And restore the calm heavens when the South-winds ebb; or unless from the lofty tower shine forth
The sweet light of friendly fire, — then the pilot bids the oars ply the arts he had lately let slip;
The curved keel is dragged over the groaning sea, till, victress of the deep,
Fortune bears the little skiff, garlanded with green branches, [to harbour].
**Endure, ye brave — as many as strive toward the citadel of the good mind, the pure host of conscience —**
**Nothing shall delay you, no false flame shall deceive the wandering keels; the twin star of Pollux and his brother**
**Shall solace the labouring mariners: this book which he holds forth shall be the star**
**Which BALDUIN presents to the Christian world.**

*— August Buchner.* [Professor of Poetry and Eloquence at Wittenberg, 1614–1661.]
