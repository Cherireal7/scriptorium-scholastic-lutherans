# Translator's Note

## 1. The text-base

This working English translation follows the **posthumous Latin edition** of Balduin's pastoral-casuistical *summa*, published at **Wittenberg by Paul Helwig, and printed at Frankfurt am Main, in the year M.DC.XXVIII (1628)**, under the full title ***Tractatus Luculentus, Posthumus, Toti Reipublicae Christianae Utilissimus, De Materiâ rarissimè antehac enucleatâ, Casibus nimirum Conscientiae, summo studio elaboratus, à Friderico Balduino, S.S. Theolog. Doctore et Professore*** — *"A Luminous Posthumous Treatise, most useful to the whole Christian Commonwealth, on a matter most rarely hitherto unfolded, namely the Cases of Conscience, elaborated with the highest care by Friedrich Balduin, Doctor and Professor of Sacred Theology."*

The book is issued **posthumously** — Balduin died at Wittenberg on 1 May 1627; the *Tractatus* was seen through the press by his widow and heirs and dedicated by them on 1 July 1628 to **Landgrave Georg of Hesse-Darmstadt**. A **preface** by the **Dean and Collegium of the Theological Faculty of Wittenberg University** stands as the collegial imprimatur, situating Balduin's *Tractatus* against the papal casuistry it was written to replace.

The source-text used here is the Google Books digitisation of a library copy (a 90.5 MB PDF, VD17 3:307763U), from which we have extracted the per-page OCR text via **PyMuPDF** — yielding 75,390 lines of running late-humanist Wittenberg Latin. The OCR is heavier in ligature drift than the 16th-century Leipzig fount of our Kirchner *Methodica* volume (long-*s* → *f*, *u* ↔ *n*, *ct* / *æ* / *ff* ligatures corrupted, marginal citations interpolated into body text), and we correct these silently against the underlying source-image.

## 2. What this book actually is

Balduin's *Tractatus* is the **first single-author Lutheran casuistry** — that is, the first attempt within the Wittenberg orthodoxy of the early 17th century to treat *the whole territory of moral theology as a system of concrete cases of conscience*, on Scripture alone, without recourse to the Roman *Sacramentum poenitentiae* framework. The genre had until then belonged almost entirely to the Roman confessors' tradition, worked from the 13th century onwards by **Raymond of Peñafort**, **John of Freiburg**, **Astesanus of Asti**, **Angelus de Clavasio**, **Bartholomew of San Concordio**, **Antoninus of Florence**, and their successors, and refined for the Counter-Reformation confessional practice by the Jesuits **Francisco de Toledo**, **Juan Azor**, **Martin Becanus**, **Jakob Gretser**, and **Valerius Reginaldus**.

Balduin's project — announced at length in the widow's dedicatoria and in the Faculty's preface — is **to redo the whole discipline** on the *unica Scripturae norma* alone. He organises the material not by the medieval *septem peccata capitalia* or by the sacraments of penance, but on a **fourfold division by object of the human action**:

- **Liber I** — *On conscience itself and cases in general*: the definition, seat, and syllogistic structure of the conscience; general categories of case.
- **Liber II** — *On the actions of man toward God and religion*: the First Table, cases of faith, cultus, prayer, oaths, vows, sabbath, and Church-fellowship.
- **Liber III** — *On the actions of man toward celestial spirits*: cases concerning the veneration of angels, demonic operations (astrology, magic, divination, spectres, obsession), and the internal spiritual assaults of suggestion, temptation, and despair.
- **Liber IV** — *On the actions of men toward human affairs*: the Second Table, cases concerning the body, the soul, temporal goods, and the specific vocations — magistrate, minister, householder, subject.

Balduin died before the *Tractatus* could be published; the widow and heirs, assisted by the Wittenberg Faculty, saw it through the press. The book was reprinted repeatedly through 1706 without scholarly revision — a fixture of the 17th-century Wittenberg curriculum and of Lutheran pastoral training for four generations.

## 3. Editorial conventions

**Liber-numbering.** Balduin's **four Libri** are rendered as **Liber I–IV** in Roman numerals, following his own subtitles. Each Liber divides into *Capita* (chapters), each Caput into numbered *Casus* (cases of conscience). Each *Casus* opens with a *Prooemium* (statement of the question) and works case-argument style through the objections, distinctions, and resolution.

**Case format.** Every *Casus* is rendered as a numbered `Case` heading; the bolded statement of the case-question follows the Latin *Prooemium* form and precedes the argumentative body. Balduin's marginal glosses on each case (*In quo consistit haec quaestio?*, *De quibus hic disserendum?*, *Quid iuris?*) are preserved as inline italic tags at the point where they appear in the source margin.

**Latin and Greek.** The Latin of the *Tractatus* is preserved *verbatim* in italics wherever Balduin is quoting a Father, a Council, a Scholastic definition, or a legal-canonical formula — each is followed by a plain-English gloss on first occurrence. Greek words retained by Balduin in Roman characters (***synderesis***, ***syneidesis***, ***catechumenus***, ***adiaphoron***) are preserved without transliteration, italicised, and glossed on first occurrence.

**Bible citations.** Book abbreviations are normalised to the modern English standard (Rom., 1 Cor., Matt.); chapter and verse numbers are silently corrected against the underlying reference where Balduin's citation differs from modern versification. Scripture quotations are **conformed to the King James Version (1611)** in the audit pass following translation, per the same convention as our Kirchner *Methodica* volume.

**Bold and italics.** **Bold** marks Bible citations, Balduin's enumerated *primo / secundo / tertio* points, and the load-bearing thesis in each case-resolution. *Italics* mark Latin quotations, Greek transliterations, and technical scholastic vocabulary retained in the original tongue. Section dividers use the aldus-leaf ornament — ❦ —.

**Polemical labels.** Balduin's polemical distinctions — ***Pontificii***, ***Sophistae***, ***Casistae***, ***Jesuitae***, ***Anabaptistae***, ***Sacramentarii***, ***Enthusiastae***, ***Calviniani*** — are preserved as he uses them, and rendered by the natural English equivalents (**Papists**, **Sophists**, **Casuists**, **Jesuits**, etc.). Balduin's frequent target is the *modern Papal casuistry* of the Jesuit apparatus of confession, not the *medieval Roman casuistry* of Raymond and Astesanus, which he cites more sympathetically as a genre-precedent even where he judges its decisions wrong.

**Silent OCR corrections.** Long-*s* → *f*, *u* ↔ *n*, and *ct* / *æ* / *ff* ligature corruptions, dropped Greek diacritics, marginal-note text interpolated into the main body by the OCR flow, running-header text intruded into body lines, and line-break hyphen artefacts from PyMuPDF's extraction are silently corrected against the source-image. Substantive editorial decisions that touch the doctrine or the case-resolution are noted in the endnotes.

**Back-matter indexes.** The original volume closes with three Latin indexes — **Index I** of Scripture-citations, **Index II** of Fathers and Authors, **Index III** of Res et Verborum (subject index). These are not translated in this English edition; the reader is served instead by the running case-headings and by the modern search capabilities of the digital text.

## 4. Reader navigation

The book contains **four Libri** with approximately fifty *Capita* and two hundred fifty numbered *Casus* across the whole domain of Christian moral theology, following Balduin's own fourfold-by-object scheme rather than the Decalogue or the *septem peccata capitalia* of Roman casuistry. A full **Table of Contents** follows this Note. Each Liber opens with a divider ornament and a recto chapter opener, and closes with the same before the next Liber begins. The formal case-resolutions appear as numbered sub-sections within each Caput. This volume is the **first of our pastoral-casuistry** series, complementing our earlier Wittenberg dogmatic (Kirchner *Methodica*, Selnecker *Symbolorum*) and catechetical (Kirchner *Enchiridion*) volumes.

# Dedication

*Epistola Dedicatoria — the widow, children, and heirs of Friedrich Balduin, of blessed memory, to Georg, Landgrave of Hesse-Darmstadt.*

**To the Most Illustrious, Most Serene Prince and Lord, Lord Georg, Landgrave of Hesse, Count of Katzenelnbogen, Dietz, Ziegenhain, and Nidda, &c. — our most clement Lord — we offer prayers and most humble, devoted subjection.**

*Most Illustrious and Most Serene Prince, our most clement Lord:*

**Two things above all are needful to a Christian man, and most worthy to be known.** The one is *the knowledge of God*; the other, *the knowledge of self*. Of the first we shall not now speak at length, for it is uncontroverted and to all men manifestly necessary. **For as all created things were made for man's sake, so man himself was constituted to know rightly the God who is his Creator — to worship him, to love him, and to celebrate him:** *"Every one that is called by my name: for I have created him for my glory, I have formed him; yea, I have made him"* (**Isa. 43:7**), that we may look up to heaven and understand what argues that we ought to worship the Maker of heaven and earth with pure and unmixed heart. So Lactantius admonishes, *Divinarum Institutionum* lib. 6, cap. 1, at the beginning.

But *on the latter head* — the knowledge of a man's own self — some preface may be permitted, most illustrious Prince. **For the matter stands thus: though scarce any of the nobler disciplines fails to claim for itself that "know-thyself" once inscribed at Delphi as fallen down from heaven** — the *Logician* recognises through discursive reason his own eminence over the brutes and a certain divine kinship with God himself; the *Ethician*, weighing his life on the balance of virtue, becomes the censor of himself and the moderator of his morals; the *Physician* and, together with him, the *Anatomist*, contemplating the wondrous fabric of the human body, finds a *microcosm* in himself; **all in their own way, by a laudable emulation, become admirers of themselves and heralds of the divine praises, when they find in the body, in the mind, arguments of the divine power, goodness, and wisdom that are plainly to be marvelled at** — **yet none of these yields to our sacred Theological discipline**, which, being occupied with the eternal salvation of man, could scarcely fail to bring man to the knowledge of himself. For it proposes life and death, heaven and hell; and therefore it must needs display man to himself, since every man carries either heaven or hell in his bosom, according as he is disposed.

**A twofold self-knowledge does man draw from the study of Theology:** the one by reason of his *defects*; the other by reason of his *acts*. The **first** arises from the consideration of *nature deprived* — nature which, in man, displays the mask of Satan, alien from God, a stranger in [God's] mysteries, an enemy in the divine precepts, unable to find the way to life or to walk in it without a Guide. The **second** springs from the consideration of *nature restored*. For though all men may know themselves in some measure by their actions, properly speaking this belongs to those who, illuminated by the Spirit of God and endowed with the gift of faith, draw near to God, acknowledge his majesty, love his commands, and study to order their life according to them.

But **this study often languishes through the weakness of the flesh**, and the recognition of God grows infirm; and it is the condition of many that the nearer they seem to approach God, the further they appear (in the outcome) to have receded from him. **Therefore every man has need of daily self-knowledge and self-examination** — which is done through the *scrutiny of conscience*. **In conscience, a man holds his own court: there he is himself Actor, Witness, Scribe, and Judge; a court in which justice always flourishes, which none can escape and none decline; for conscience always assumes, and always concludes.** For our *synderesis* — that practical habit whereby a man is rendered disposed to give assent to the first practical principles — supplies the *proposition*; and *synesis*, conscience, hereafter assumes, and at last concludes. So the proposition in the mind of King Oedipus was: *"He who commits incest and parricide commits a shameful act, and one worthy of punishment."* His conscience assumed: *"But thou hast committed it."* And it concluded this accusation: *"Therefore thou art worthy of punishment."* Such was the grief that followed this syllogism in the mind of Oedipus, king of the Thebans, that, impatient of the shame he had heaped upon himself, he tore out his own eyes for very sorrow. So indeed Melanchthon observes.

For — as Chrysostom writes in his fourth sermon on Lazarus — *"An earthly father, when he has once or twice or thrice or four times chastised his son, should he see him remain incorrigible, disinherits and casts him out of the house, cutting him off from the family; but not so conscience: though it warn once, twice, thrice, or a thousand times without avail, yet it never withdraws nor ceases to warn again — even unto the last breath — whether at home, in the crossways, at meat, in the market, upon the journey; and often in dreams it represents to us the images and spectres of our sins."*

But not gladly do those men attend this court who are always occupied with vain speculations, whether of things divine or things human, never seriously considering true piety or the ordering of their own life — of whom the number in this world is ever great. For as Bernard writes in *On the Interior House*, cap. 51: *"Many have sought knowledge without conscience; of whom Ecclesiastes says: 'In much wisdom is much grief.'"* Wherefore also Augustine, in the *De Spiritu et Anima* cap. 51, commends by many arguments the knowledge that pertains to each man's conscience, writing among other things: *"Since no knowledge is better than that whereby a man knows himself — let us discuss our own thoughts, our doings, and our works. For what does it profit us if we investigate subtly and comprehend efficaciously the natures of all things, and understand not ourselves? Let us therefore examine what we have done, in what manner we ought to have done it, in what manner we have done it; let us consider what we are about to do — whether it be according to God."* And a little after: *"It is necessary that day by day we call our life to judgment; and that we examine what we have done by night and by day; how much more alacritous we have been than usual in doing good, and how much more constant than usual in overcoming evil; whether by some work of ours we have been supplanted by the snares of the enemy; and how, by judgment, we may be able to guard against the future fraud of that deception that has already passed — that neither the assailing temptation, coming upon us unwary, may cast us down to the evil work, nor the present negligence, when we are indiscreet in a good work, hinder us."*

**In this examination many matters arise which give scruple even to the pious — matters which they call *Cases of Conscience*.** The decisions of these are as necessary in Theological study as the discussions of the articles of faith. For often here (as the proverb runs) *the water sticks*, even in those most exercised in speculative Theology; and unless the scruple be removed, all the subtleties of theological Contemplations seem to be worth not one *as*. **The treatment of the *Casus Conscientiae* is therefore most necessary,** belonging to that part of theological Wisdom which is called *practical*; which, while it looses the knots that bind the conscience, occupies the mind of the pious man not with any idle contemplation but confirms it with a most useful learning of the greatest matters, so that it may withstand the assault of whatever doubts or temptations exercise our life. **This part of Theology is spread through all the actions of all men in every kind of life, wherein our judgment is informed, so that our conscience may have, in every case that occurs, that in which it may find rest.**

*[Portrait plate: Balduin — with a Latin verse-motto below, illegible in OCR.]*

**In this kind of theological study, much labour and time was spent under the Papacy by those (as they call them) *Summists*** who wrote *Sums* on the deciding of cases of conscience. Others called them *Casists*. Among the more notable of these were **Astesanus of Asti**, **Raymond of Peñafort**, **John of Freiburg**, **Hugo of Ripelin**, **Monaldus the Franciscan**, **Bartholomew of San Concordio**, **John of Rider**, **Bernard of Siena**, **Henry Herp**, **Bartholomew Trentini**, **Thomas de Vio [Cajetan]**, **Navarrus [Azpilcueta]**, and the like. But **in many things they obscured this doctrine which they ought to have illustrated**, proposing such **Cases of Conscience** as the mere superstition of Rome had begotten in the age in which the Papal *ἐξουσιαμαχία* — its lust for lording it over consciences — was wont to bind men's consciences. In their other decisions also they gravely erred, because they followed not the word of God (which alone can confirm consciences) but the leadership of Papal decrees. **What service the *Casists* of the Roman party render to the Church in this present age** may be gathered from the writings of **Francisco de Toledo**, **Juan Azor**, **Martin Becanus**, and **Jakob Gretser**; and, not so long since, from the compendious *Praxis of the more difficult Cases of Conscience that frequently occur in the administration of the Sacrament of Penance*, published three years ago by **Father Valerius Reginaldus, the Jesuit**. For these men drew the decisions of the harder cases not from the purest springs of Israel, but from the muddied cisterns of the Scholastics and of others — Thomas Aquinas, Thomas Sanchez, Suarez — and served them up to the credulous populace.

**When therefore Dr. Friedrich Balduin,** *Doctor of Sacred Theology, Professor, Pastor, and Superintendent at this alma Leucorea* [the University of Wittenberg], **our most desired parent, of blessed memory,** had thoroughly perceived and known this **γοητεία** [deception] plainly Antichristian, which the *Casists* have been wont to employ in the deciding of the *Casus Conscientiae*, **he judged it would be to the good of the whole Church if some Theologian should propose the Cases of Conscience according to the sole norm of Scripture** — a labour which we suppose to have been hitherto orthodoxly and methodically performed by no man. This great labour, relying on divine aid, he suffered to be laid upon his own shoulders; in which enterprise **he candidly taught and demonstrated to the youth, from the word of God alone and from the practice of the primitive Church, in what manner the decisions of the Romanists and of the other Adversaries — which lean upon alien principles — differ from ours.**

These lucubrations, now made public property, we humbly dedicate to **Your Illustrious Highness**, most Illustrious and Most Excellent Prince, most Clement Lord — as we thought fitting, partly by that customary law that has descended from our forefathers to the order of letters, whereby of old those who feared to send forth in print the lucubrations of their own wit or of another's, wrote them up to the Chiefs of that age, so that under the patronage of so great a name they might come forth safe and unharmed from the sneering of adversaries; **partly also because the consent of the churches of Saxony and Hesse is commended for the purity of doctrine as thoroughly orthodox even to our own generation.** Besides, it is the candid and ingenuous confession of many, that Your Highness's unfeigned **piety** and *entheus ardor* for preserving and propagating the sincere religion, your **justice**, your remarkable **beneficence**, in the abundance of all things, your **moderation** and **modesty** — by a certain hidden virtue and by the devotion of all sincere-eyed observers — bind to yourself their souls in perpetual reverence.

**Let therefore Your Illustrious Highness receive under your patronage this work, most useful to the whole Church and posthumous of our most desired parent** — for since it is the mind of the giver, rather than the weight of the gift, that is regarded by those who look candidly upon it, these things which we, of Balduin of blessed memory, present to Your Illustrious Highness deserve, at any rate, to be received not with an unjust but with a clement mind.

**May God most benign, the Father of our Lord Jesus Christ, preserve Your Illustrious Highness, together with the most illustrious House and Family of Hesse, as long as possible incolume and flourishing for the utility of Church and Commonwealth — rule it by his Holy Spirit, guard it with the army of angels, augment it with every kind of benedictions, felicitously prosper and fructify it. Amen.**

*Wittenberg, 1 July, in the year of restored salvation M.DC.XXVIII (1628).*

Your Illustrious Highness's most subject and humble servants,

**The widow, children, and heirs of D. Friedrich Balduin, of pious memory.**
