# Translator's Note

*This working translation is at its kick-off state. The paragraphs below record the intended text base, the editorial conventions binding on the eventual body prose, and — with full disclosure — what remains to be verified once the 1685 Wittenberg imprint has been sighted paragraph by paragraph.*

## 1. The text base

The English rendered here follows Pars Prima of Johann Andreas Quenstedt's *Theologia Didactico-Polemica, sive Systema Theologicum* — the four chapters running from *De Theologia in genere* through *De Sacra Scriptura* — as printed in the **1685 Wittenberg *editio princeps*** at the expense of the Heirs of Simon Fincelius in the printing house of Matthaeus Henckel. Pars Prima is the theological-encyclopedic prolegomena of the *Systema*: it treats theology as a discipline, its object, its principle, and — at length, in Chapter IV — the Sacred Scripture which is its principle. In the older bibliographic tradition this prolegomenal quartet was also cited (from Quenstedt's earlier Wittenberg academic exercitationes on the same subjects) as *Exercitationes de theologia in genere ejusque principio sancta scriptura*, and it is under that title that this working translation presents it.

The 1685 *editio princeps* is our theologically-strict text-base. Because the 1685 is not, at the time of this note, immediately at hand in a digitised full-text scan (candidate holdings at the **Bayerische Staatsbibliothek Munich**, the **Sächsische Landesbibliothek Dresden**, and the **Herzog August Bibliothek Wolfenbüttel** are being pursued), the *operational* text-base for the OCR and paragraph-by-paragraph translation is the fully-digitised **Leipzig 1715 reprint** held at the Internet Archive under the identifier `TheologiaDidactico`, in which Pars Prima runs from page 1 to page 347. Where the 1685 and the 1715 diverge on any theologically substantive reading — verified against the 1685 as soon as the sight of it is secured — the 1685 governs and the divergence is disclosed in an endnote. The 1691 and 1696 intermediate editions and the 1702 Leipzig reissue are noted for completeness but not used as text-bases; the tradition of the text is stable in its substance and the divergences among the 17th-c. editions are almost entirely orthographic.

A modern English partial translation of Chapter I *De Theologia in genere* by Luther Poellot — *The Nature and Character of Theology: An Introduction to the Thought of J. A. Quenstedt from Theologia Didactico-Polemica* (Concordia Publishing House, 1986) — has been consulted as comparanda for the first twenty-seven pages of Pars Prima. Our reading of the Latin is our own; where our English rendering of a technical term or a sentence-shape diverges materially from Poellot's, the divergence is noted in an endnote with the reasoning stated briefly, so that the reader may weigh the two renderings side by side rather than being asked to trust either silently.

## 2. What the author himself supplies

Quenstedt supplies all of the argument. This is a *systema theologicum* in the strict late-orthodox sense: every chapter opens with a *definitio*, proceeds by *distinctio*, states *theses*, weighs the *sententiae* of adversaries in short lettered paragraphs (*a.*, *b.*, *c.*), and closes with *corollaria*. The apparatus is Quenstedt's own — the divisions of the discipline (theologia archetypa / ectypa; theologia viatorum / comprehensorum), the causal analyses (*causa efficiens*, *materialis*, *formalis*, *finalis*), and the specifically Wittenberg-scholastic technical vocabulary (*modus subjecti*, *modus adhaesionis*, *habitus theologicus practicus vs. theoreticus*, *articulus fundamentalis / non-fundamentalis*). None of this scholastic architecture is smoothed into narrative English; all of it is preserved, because the architecture is itself the argument.

The marginal apparatus of the 1685 imprint — biblical loci, cross-references to Chemnitz, Gerhard, Calov, Hutter, Hunnius, Meisner, Balduin, and (in the polemical registers) to Bellarmine, Becanus, Suárez, Calvin, Beza, Junius, and Amesius — is Quenstedt's own citation-work, and it is preserved in the body. Where a marginal is a bare biblical locus, it is absorbed into the running English text with parenthetical citation. Where a marginal names a source (*"Gerh. loc. I §174"*, *"Chemn. Ex. Conc. Trid. IV"*, *"Bellarm. de verb. Dei III, c. 3"*), the citation is preserved verbatim in the body and augmented in the endnotes with modern edition-standards where those standards exist (WA, MSA, CR, PL, PG, CCSL, and — for the 17th-c. Lutheran and Reformed dogmaticians — the standard Wittenberg or Leipzig imprint plus the corresponding PRDL identifier).

## 3. What the Scholastic Lutherans have added — and where

*Nothing in the body text is our own.* All editorial additions are relegated to the numbered endnotes at the end of the volume. These endnotes supply, and only supply:

- Standard modern citations for the patristic sources Quenstedt quotes (PL / PG volume:column; SC, CCSL, GCS where a critical text has since superseded Migne; NPNF or an equivalent English translation where one exists)
- Standard modern citations for scripture (ESV numbering, with the Vulgate reference in parentheses on first occurrence in each chapter)
- Standard modern citations for Luther (WA and, where available, LW volume:page) and for Melanchthon (CR, MSA), and for the Book of Concord (BSLK / BSELK, Kolb-Wengert English)
- Standard imprint-identifications for the 17th-c. Lutheran, Reformed, and Roman-Catholic authors Quenstedt cites — the Chemnitz *Examen* Frankfurt 1578; the Gerhard *Loci Theologici* Jena 1610–22; the Calov *Systema* Wittenberg 1655–77; the Bellarmine *Disputationes* Ingolstadt 1586–93; the Amesius *Medulla* Amsterdam 1623 — with PRDL identifiers appended where those exist
- Biographical dates on first named mention of each cited author
- Modern equivalents for early-modern toponyms and personal names
- Brief technical notes where the Latin carries a distinction the English cannot preserve
- Explicit disclosure, at each occurrence, of divergence between our English rendering and Poellot 1986 for the material Poellot translated

Body prose contains no modern scholarship, no editorial voice, no bracketed insertions. Everything is Quenstedt.

## 4. Editorial conventions

- **Register:** scholarly / academic-critical English. Reverent but not archaising. The scholastic architecture is preserved visibly — numbered sections (§ 1., § 2.), lettered sub-paragraphs (*a.*, *b.*, *c.*), definition / division / thesis / objection / refutation blocks — because the shape of Quenstedt's argument is his argument.
- **Greek** is preserved in polytonic script, with parenthetical transliteration and English gloss on first substantive occurrence in each section — e.g. **θεολογία** (*theologia*, "theology," lit. "word concerning God"). Do not footnote every Greek term.
- **Hebrew** appears often in Chapter IV *De Sacra Scriptura*; it is preserved in pointed Hebrew script, with parenthetical transliteration and gloss on first occurrence. Where Quenstedt is arguing from the vocalisation itself (as in the Buxtorf–Cappellus controversy on the age of the vowel points), the vocalised and unvocalised forms are both preserved.
- **Latin technical terms** — *theologia archetypa*, *theologia ectypa*, *theologia viatorum*, *theologia comprehensorum*, *principium essendi*, *principium cognoscendi externum / internum*, *articulus fundamentalis / non-fundamentalis*, *analogia fidei*, *auctoritas / perfectio / perspicuitas / efficacia Scripturae* — are preserved on first occurrence in each chapter in italic, glossed briefly in English, and rendered thereafter in the English rendering used in this volume's glossary. Where the term is itself the subject under discussion in the passage, the Latin form is retained throughout the discussion.
- **Scripture:** ESV for the English rendering. Vulgate numeration and Quenstedt's own citation form are noted parenthetically on first citation per chapter — e.g. "2 Timothy 3:16 (Vulgate: 2 Tim. iii. 16)". For the Old Testament, Vulgate book-names appear in parentheses on first citation per chapter for Psalms (where the Vulgate numeration diverges by one for most of the middle Psalter), for 1–2 Samuel / 1–2 Kings (Vulgate: 1–4 Regum), and for Sirach (Vulgate: Ecclesiasticus).
- **Long-*s*** (ſ) is silently modernised to round *s*. Sixteenth- and seventeenth-century *v/u* and *i/j* variation is normalised to standard modern Latin orthography in citation. Printer's abbreviations (macron for *n*/*m*, *q̃* for *quam*, *p̄* for *per*, *&c.* for *et caetera*, the standard Antiqua ligatures) are silently expanded.
- **Named authors, first occurrence:** modern English name in the body (*Chemnitz*, *Gerhard*, *Bellarmine*, *Cappellus*), with the Latinised form in parenthesis on first occurrence in each chapter (*Chemnitius*, *Gerhardus*, *Bellarminus*, *Cappellus / Louis Cappel*). Life dates appear in the endnote, not in the body.
- **Confessional restraint.** Quenstedt writes with the confidence of a mature confessional Lutheran polemic against Rome and against the Reformed. His tone — measured but firm, scholastic in its architecture rather than rhetorical in its polemic — is preserved. It is neither softened toward modern ecumenical sensibilities nor amplified in either polemical direction.
- **Rubrication, typeface, layout** — locked via `shared/reference-custom.docx` and identical to the Praetorius trilogy, the completed Chytraeus 1594 translation, and the in-flight Flacius *Catalogus*. Palatino Linotype body, Perpetua Titling MT display in `#B71C1C` for chapter openings, justified body, centered titles, aldus-leaf ornamental dividers (`— ❦ —`), raw-OOXML page breaks between the paratexts, the Translator's Note, each chapter, the Notes section, and the Glossary.

## 5. Scope disclosure — the tranche structure

The Pars Prima comprises **approximately 347 folio pages** in the Leipzig 1715 collation, distributed markedly asymmetrically across four chapters: the theological-encyclopedic essays proper (Chapters I, II, III) occupy the first seventy-six pages together (respectively 27, 18, and 31 pages), and the fourth chapter — *De Sacra Scriptura* — occupies the remaining 271 pages. Chapter IV is where the volume becomes an encyclopedia of the Lutheran doctrine of Scripture: name, authors, canon, autographs, translations, points and vowels, and the four *affections* (authority, perfection, perspicuity, efficacy) that structure the whole discussion.

Because of that asymmetry, this working translation is planned in **five tranches**, documented in the working notes: (1) the front matter of the 1685 imprint together with Chapter I *Of Theology in General*, cross-checked line by line against Poellot 1986; (2) Chapter II *Of the General Object of Theology, Religion*; (3) Chapter III *Of the Principle of Theology*; (4) the first half of Chapter IV *Of the Sacred Scripture* (through the discussion of the affections of Scripture); and (5) the second half of Chapter IV (Scripture and tradition, Scripture and the Church, the analogy of faith, the reading of Scripture by the laity, and the closing objections and refutations). Chapter-internal page-boundaries within Chapter IV are provisional and will be re-cut against the source at each tranche's kick-off.

At this working-translation state, the scaffolding is complete: the text base is fixed against the 1685 Wittenberg *editio princeps* (with the 1715 Leipzig as operational OCR-source pending sighting of the 1685), the editorial conventions are set to match the Praetorius / Chytraeus reference implementations, and the release plan is drawn against the sighted Pars Prima structure. Body prose accrues tranche by tranche.

# Front Matter

## The Preface to the Reader

### *Praefatio ad Benignum et Candidum Lectorem*

Since so many excellent Systems of the whole of Sacred Theology have hitherto been sent forth into the light by the most celebrated Theologians, some might wonder why I, again, have set my hand to this arduous and difficult undertaking. For after that most merciful God, through his singular instrument, the blessed **Martin Luther**, placed the doctrine of the Gospel — cleansed most happily from the filth of the Roman Antichrist and the Monks — in clear light, many men flourishing in the deepest knowledge of theological matters have proved their industry, in composing Theological Commonplaces, to the Christian world, with the eternal glory of their names and with great fruit for the Church.

And would that the same blessed Luther had signed off his meditations on all the articles of the faith in that order in which they are wont to be arranged in a Theological System, and had collected them into a single body — we should certainly have had the most complete treasury of all Theology. But the unequal pressure of his time's adversaries did not permit this, as the great **Hülsemann** observes in his *Method of Theological Studies*, page 299, edition of 1648.¹ **Philip Melanchthon**, once (and would that also constantly!) the faithful shield-bearer (*βαστάζων*) of *Megalander* Luther,² published *Theological Commonplaces* which — with the corruptions and depravations of his later revisions set aside — the Church has held in high esteem, and has received into the Academies for the training of students of theology.

After Philip, the blessed **Dr. Martin Chemnitz**, the highest and chief theologian of the Augsburg Confession, wrote *Theological Commonplaces* in which he both perspicuously explains the *Commonplaces* of Dr. Philip Melanchthon and sets forth an entire body, as it were, of Christian doctrine sincerely to the Church of God. Of this remarkable work, **Dr. Polycarp Lyser** does not hesitate to affirm, in his Dedicatory Epistle, that "there does not exist, in this genre of writings, any labour of any man which could equal — much less surpass — this blessed Chemnitz in usefulness, in weight of matter, and in perspicuity of exposition." **Dr. Leonhart Hutter** likewise, once the most celebrated theologian of this Academy [of Wittenberg], wrote upon the *Commonplaces* of Philip Melanchthon a perspicuous and copious commentary — and yet in such a manner that whatever things had been said either inconveniently or less orthodoxly, he weighed against the analogy of faith and the scales of celestial truth, as Hutter himself puts it in the closing of chapter IV of his *Prolegomena*.

Among those, however, who have chosen to devote their labour to this manner of writing, there shines as it were a bright star, the incomparable theologian, the blessed **Dr. Johann Gerhard**, my most-loved uncle,³ who with unbelievable industry wrote out *Nine Volumes of Theological Commonplaces*, which are held in the highest esteem by all who are piously and eruditely learned, and which serve many in place of a most choice library. As **Dilherr** rightly says in his *Funeral Oration* upon Dr. Johann Gerhard: "Nothing, after the departure of blessed Luther, that Phoenix of Germany, was ever wrought by the venerable band of theologians (and many things, indeed, both excellent and destined to outlive every age, were wrought) that could be compared with the Nine Volumes of Theological Commonplaces."

To Gerhard I deservedly join the senior of the orthodox learned theologians, that inestimable ornament of the Christian Church, my **Dr. Abraham Calov** — my son-in-law, colleague, and most-conjoined brother in Christ Jesus, and to be reverenced with that observance which is due — who besides his other immense and most useful labours upon the whole of Theology, whether Didactic, or Exegetic, or Polemic, or Casual [i.e. Casuistical], will shortly hand over to the public good his *System of Theological Commonplaces* comprised in twelve volumes, published some years ago and now revised, augmented by nearly infinite accessions, and — chief of all — enriched with Casual Theology.⁴ May **JEHOVAH** preserve him, and pour manifold grace around his venerable grey hairs, adorned by innumerable merits toward the Church universal; and may he not, save very late indeed, bear him into heaven, and adorn him with the unfading crown of glory!

There also came to fame **Dr. Erasmus Brochmann**, once the splendid light of the Danish orb, by his edition of an excellent *Theological System* composed by accurate method, in which all the chapters of the celestial and salvific doctrine are dexterously and nervously explained, and vindicated invincibly against the tricks of every gainsayer; and cases of conscience are solidly decided out of the Word of GOD.⁵

All these, and many other most deserving theologians of the Church of Christ, have accomplished so much in this manner of writing that they seem to have left almost nothing remaining, whether as to the explanation and defence of the Catholic faith, or as to the refutation of the sects both old and new.

And it is besides an arduous and difficult undertaking, to draw back the whole Christian doctrine — diffused in the many and almost innumerable books of others — to certain chapters; to assert all the dogmas of faith from the unmoved foundations of Sacred Letters; to fight them through against adversaries of every rank; to refute the contrary opinions of all heretics and heterodox, both ancient and recent; to illustrate the more obscure passages of Scripture; to explain the *hard-to-understand things* (*δυσνόητα*, 2 Peter 3:16); and to vindicate them from the exceptions and depravations of the *gainsayers* (*τῶν ἀντιλεγόντων*). Immortal God! how many monsters, from the very first age of the nascent Church, and especially in this and in the preceding century — most fertile in strife and controversies — have come forth from Orcus warring against celestial truth! But how arduous and difficult it is to overcome all these, only those know at last, who through their whole life have sweated (as they say) *ex professo* against the heretics, and have grown old in waging the wars of the Lord.

Nevertheless, this attempt of mine will not on that account be rightly disapproved, since the reason of the office committed to me demanded it. For from that time onward at which the Profession of Theology was entrusted to me in this Academy (which fell in the year 1649), I judged it my part to assert the truth and purity of Christian doctrine from the most sacred Word of GOD, and, on the contrary, to destroy the falsity and impurity of the heterodox dogmas. This duty of mine, by divine grace and aid, I have endeavoured to discharge to the extent of my powers. Theological Schools — Didactic, Polemic, Exegetic — I have opened up to the studious youth, either severally or conjointly, and I have often repeated them by frequent lecturing and disputing. There were always flocking in in great throng the Auditors, who eagerly received with their ears and their pens what I set forth — as very many pious and learned men will testify who through the last thirty-six years have given their diligence to Theology in this Academy, and who are now, with credit and edification, serving the promotion of the glory of God, partly in Academies and Schools, partly in Churches; and who yet keep, and hold among their delights, those things which they took down from my mouth as teacher.

This alacrity and ardour to learn among my Auditors caused me to unroll, with indefatigable study, the writings, whether of the ancients or of the more recent, whether of foreigners or of domestic, whether of orthodox or of heterodox theologians; to note down with a diligent pen the best of what could serve for my use and my Students'; and to arrange all things in their proper camps. I laboured chiefly at this: that what has been widely and scatteredly disputed and handed down by others I should compress into summaries, and dispose them by a fitting method, and in the most convenient order. Wherefore this work grew every year by the accession of arguments confirming the truth, and of very many controversies. When I became aware that it was passing about not without very many errors, some six years past and a little more, at the instance of many young men consecrated to the more sacred study, I resumed the work, revised it, augmented it, amplified it, and at last, that it might serve the uses of very many, I have made it public property.

— ❦ —

Now, so far as concerns the order and method itself, I have recalled the whole matter to two Sections, the *Didactic* and the *Polemic*. The **Didactic** exhibits the causes, effects, definitions, attributes, adjuncts, etc. of all and single articles of faith through *theses* or *positions*, following the plan of the *Positive Theology* of the blessed Dr. Johann Friedrich König,⁶ once my most conjoined friend. And since nothing is to be asserted concerning divine matters except what has been expressed in the *God-breathed* (*θεοπνεύστοις*) Scriptures — for so **Basil**, in his Homily against the Calumniators of the Holy Trinity: *"To what has been written, give credit; what has not been written, do not investigate"* (*τοῖς γεγραμμένοις πίστευε, τὰ μὴ γεγραμμένα μὴ ζήτει*)⁷ — and **Cyril of Jerusalem** (*Catech. IV*, p. 85): *"It behoves that not even the least thing be handed down of divine and holy mysteries of the faith without the Sacred Letters"* — I have drawn the proofs of the *theses* from the Sacred Scripture of both Testaments, as the foundation and unique principle of our religion. I have adduced, however, the passages of Scripture not indiscriminately (as is the usual way), but the *fundamental* passages and those most cogent, mindful of that saying of **Thomas Aquinas** (I, q. 32, art. 1): *"When someone brings forward, to prove the faith, reasons which are not cogent, it lapses into the derision of unbelievers; for they believe that we rest on reasons of this sort, and that on their account we believe."*⁸

And since in the bare rehearsal of Scripture-passages there is no subtlety (as Scaliger somewhere says), I have investigated the genuine sense, expounded it at greater length, and shown the use, whether in Polemics — that is, in the controversies of the faith — or in Homiletics, that is, in sacred sermons.

The **Second Section**, namely the *Polemic*, treats the controversies which are stirred up around the articles of the faith, whether these are moved from of old, or agitated today. Controversies, I say, chief and such as seem of any moment or weight, setting aside the curious, thorny, and vain — the deciding of which is neither useful nor necessary. When the *question in dispute* (*ζητούμενον*) has been proposed:

1. The **true state of the controversy** is rightly formed, with false states removed. For the Adversaries of celestial truth most often pervert maliciously the state of the question, or do not faithfully propose it, and often forge for themselves and impute to us monstrous opinions which they might overthrow.
2. The **certain thesis** (*θέσις*) is set up, in which the orthodox opinion is briefly and perspicuously proposed.
3. The **exposition** (*ἔκθεσις*) is subjoined, in which the difficulties that occur around the state of the controversy are cleared up through distinctions and observations that deserve to be known and impressed upon the memory; and the thesis itself, and its terms, are more fully explained.
4. The exposition is followed by the **antithesis** (*ἀντίθεσις*), which is occupied in reciting the opinions opposed to the Truth — of all heretics and heterodox, both ancient and recent, namely of Jews, Papists, Calvinians, Socinians, Arminians, Syncretists, Weigelians, Anabaptists, and other Fanatics — whose either whole words, or the sum of them, are faithfully (and so far as could be done) adduced from their own books. For "the victory over heretics is the manifestation of their opinion," as **Irenaeus** says (*Against Heresies* I. 35); and, as **Cyril of Jerusalem** put it (*Catech. VI*, which is *Of the Illuminated*): *"It is far better that you know and hate their dirt, than that you fall into them unaware."*
5. There follows the **confirmation of the thesis** (*θέσεως βεβαίωσις*), in which the passages of Scripture proving and confirming the thesis are repeated from the earlier section. For the most certain decision of the controversies of faith cannot be drawn from anywhere else than from the Word of GOD. "Let us stand," says **Basil to Eustathius**, "by the arbitrament of the God-inspired Scripture; and among whomsoever there are found dogmas consonant with the divine oracles, to them let the sentence of Truth be altogether adjudged." I did not wish, however, in confirming the truth, to heap up testimonies from Antiquity — partly because they may be found everywhere among Our own, partly lest this book should swell into a greater bulk. Now and then, however, I have adduced those sentences of the Fathers which propose the matter acutely, briefly, and most significantly, and sometimes I have confirmed the thesis by reasonings drawn from Scripture which infirm and enervate the tricks and sophisms of *the adversaries* (*τῶν ἀντιδίκων*) — by reasonings, I say, drawn from the fountains of Scripture, not from the muddy rivulet of corrupted reason. For, as **Cyril of Alexandria** (*Dialogue on the Trinity* V. i, p. 466) declares: *"They act impiously who attempt rashly and foolishly to examine those things which are above the capacity of the mind, by the inventions of miserable little reasonings."*
6. There is subjoined the **vindication** (*ἐκδίκησις*), in which the sacred texts are vindicated from the frivolous interpretations, distortions, and depravations of the heterodox.
7. This is accompanied by the **dissolving of objections** (*διάλυσις*), in which the defences that the adversaries subtly seek out from Sacred Scripture and from the Fathers for their desperate cause are wrenched from them; and *fair-shining Truth* (*ἡ εὐπρεπής ἀλήθεια*), which they wish to have been made captive to their errors, is restored to herself and to her liberty.
8. Finally, to each of the Controversies the **Authors of either side are indicated** — those of the adversary usually in the *antithesis*, so that each may recognise that the opinions opposed to the truth are candidly adduced; and the Writers of Our side are alleged at the end of each Controversy — and this not only in order that those consecrated to the more sacred study may know by which theologian this or that controversy has been more copiously, more fully, and *ex professo* treated (for to know where you can find something is a great part of erudition), but also that I may sincerely profess through whom I have made progress, and whence I have drawn, for the most part, my own material. **Gerhard Vossius** (*Of Idolatry* III. 84, p. 1185) deservedly reproves those who, with an ungrateful and no less proud spirit, refuse to point the finger toward the fountain from which they have themselves profited, whence others also might more fully and more certainly satisfy their thirst.

This labour of mine, therefore, does not tend toward this: that the *Systems* of the greatest Theologians (which the Studious of Theosophy are wont to have familiar in hand) may be knocked from their hands; but that those may be commended more and more, and that they may be led back to the fountains through these little streams derived thence, and that with those most-praised works of others these poor meditations of mine, of what quality soever they may be, may be all the more fruitfully joined. To this pertains that word of **Augustine** (*On the Trinity* I. 3): *"It is useful that many books be made by many with diverse style — not with diverse faith — even concerning the same questions, that the matter itself may reach the greater number: to some thus, to others thus."* Those who cannot spread themselves through every kind of Author, and cannot acquire for themselves an apparatus of Theological erudition, have here in a compendium or synopsis what in every article of Faith our theologians hold, and what the Adversaries hold; on what foundations our own rest, and on what theirs; how the truth is to be demonstrated and how the falsity is to be destroyed and refuted.

May the most-holy Godhead of the Triad grant that this work — which has been undertaken under his conduct, and consummated by his aid — be always directed to the glory of the divine Name, to the edification of the Church, and to our own salvation. Let it be, JESUS, let it be!

# Chapter I. Of Theology in General

## *De Theologia in genere*

### Section One — Didactic

#### *Sectio Prima Didactica*

##### *Θέσις Ι — Thesis I*

**THEOLOGY**, if you regard the force and use of the word, is nothing other than **ὁ λόγος περὶ τοῦ Θεοῦ καὶ περὶ τῶν θείων** — *speech concerning God and concerning divine things* — just as *pneumatology* (*πνευματολογία*) is speech or teaching concerning spirits, and *astrology* (*ἀστρολογία*) is speech concerning the stars.

*Note I. As to the origin*, the noun *Theology* is not simple but compound, made up from the words *θεός* (*God*) and *λόγος* (*word*). Here, however, a distinction is to be made between *λόγος θεοῦ* (the *word of God*) and *λόγος περὶ θεοῦ* (the *word concerning God*). *λόγος θεοῦ* — or, as it is found in Romans 3:2 and Hebrews 5:12, *τὰ λόγια* or *τὰ λόγια θεοῦ* — is Sacred Scripture itself, the *principle of Theology*; but *λόγος περὶ τοῦ Θεοῦ* is Theology itself, its *principiate*.⁹

*Note II. As to signification*: The noun *θεός* in this composition denotes not only God himself — one in essence, three in persons — but at the same time embraces *τὰ τοῦ θεοῦ*, the things which are of God, or divine things. For Theology deals with God both in the *nominative case*, so to speak, and (as the Scholastics put it) in the *oblique case* — that is, both with God himself, and with the things that are of God.

*Note III.* Certain Calvinians — as Antonius Wallaeus (*Loci Communes*, p. 3), Johannes Scharpius, Scotus (in his *Cursus Theologicus*, p. 1), and Ludovicus Crocius (*Syntagma Theol.*, p. 62) — assert that the word *θεολογία* properly signifies *λόγιον θεοῦ*, "the word of God" or "the utterance of God." But we prove our own opinion (1) from the *nature of a concrete and its abstract*: the concrete *Theologus* and the abstract *Theologia* descend from one and the same theme. Now a *Theologus* is one who *speaks concerning God*; therefore Theology also is not the *speech of God*, but the *speech concerning God*. (2) From the derivation of the word: the derivation of *Theologia* is to be sought from the verb *θεολογεῖν*, which means *to discourse concerning God, to confess God*. (3) From the analogy of similar words: thus *πνευματολογία* is not the speech or utterance of the Spirit, but the doctrine concerning spirits; *astrology* is not the speech of the stars, but the doctrine concerning the stars — so likewise Theology is not the speech of God himself, but the speech concerning God. (4) From the distinction of Sacred Scripture and Theology: Scripture is the *principle* of Theology, being namely *λόγιον θεοῦ*; Theology, however, is the *principiate*, being *λόγος περὶ τοῦ θεοῦ*.

*Note IV.* Theology is rightly denominated from GOD, because (1) originally, it is from God; (2) objectively, it treats of God; (3) terminatively, it is directed unto God; and (4) effectively, it leads man to God. Hence **Thomas** (Part I of the *Summa*, question 1, article 7): "Theology is taught by God, teaches God, and leads to God." And so Theology has deservedly taken its denomination from God: for whether you look to the principal efficient cause, GOD is its author, by the mediation of revelation made through the word; or to the object and matter, this is God; or, finally, to the very end, God is the end of it — and that saving and glorious vision of God himself, unto which, with this knowledge of God (*θεογνωσία*) as our guide, we tend. The very reason of the name, however, is derived not so much from the principal efficient or from the end, as from the object of consideration, so that it is *ὁ λόγος περὶ τοῦ θεοῦ*, the *speech concerning God*.

*Note V.* Concerning the *use* of the noun *Theology*, it is to be observed that the word *Theology* was first employed by the Gentiles — for, as **Lactantius** says (*Of the Wrath of God*, chapter XI), the most ancient writers of Greece, "since they treated of the gods and of their worship, were called *Theologi*, and their science was called *Theology*"; and thereafter this word was also employed by Christians.

*Note VI.* A distinction is to be drawn between the adequate and inadequate *object* of Theology. The inadequate and primary object comprises all divine things, that is, God himself and whatsoever is of God and whatsoever is from God and is ordered unto God. And thus the word *God* (*θεός*) is either to be extended, so as to embrace also those things which are of God, or a further clause is to be added, so that Theology is *λόγος περὶ τοῦ θεοῦ καὶ περὶ τῶν θείων*.

*Note VII.* Concerning the *signification* of the noun *Theology*, a distinction is to be made between (1) *the most general signification*, by which it denotes any speech or doctrine at all concerning God, whether true or false — chiefly among profane authors; (2) *the general*, by which it designates any knowledge of God — true, however, and even natural; (3) *the special*, by which it denotes the knowledge of God and of divine mysteries revealed and handed down in the Scriptures; and (4) *the most special*, by which it signifies that part of the celestial doctrine which treats of the divinity of Christ — so that it becomes speech *concerning God the Word* (*περὶ τοῦ θεοῦ λόγου*), that is, concerning the *hypostatic Word of God*. In this sense, among the Fathers, *to speak the theological word* (*θεολογεῖν λόγον*) is to assert the divinity of Christ; and **John the Apostle and Evangelist**, that outstanding herald of this Mystery, is in the Apocalypse honoured with the epithet *ὁ θεολόγος*: by **John of Damascus** he is called *ὁ θεολόγος τῆς εὐαγγελικῆς*, precisely because he expressed openly and outstandingly the divinity (*τὴν θεότητα*) of the Word (*τοῦ λόγου*), while the other Evangelists were occupied *in the economy* (*ἐν οἰκονομίᾳ*) — that is, in the teaching of the incarnation of the Son of God, and of the reparation of the human race which was wrought through it.

*Note VIII. As to the use of the noun*: The word was first taken up among Gentiles (as we said in Note V); afterwards it was received by Christians.

*Note IX.* A distinction must be drawn between the word *Theology* taken in the *abstract*, in which sense it is *ἄγραφον* — for as to the very letter it is not found in Sacred Scripture — and the word *Theology* taken in the *concrete*, in which sense it is *ἔγγραφον*. For it is had in the Apocalypse (as we said just above), where the Evangelist John is *κατ' ἐξοχήν* called *ὁ θεολόγος*, "the Theologian." And although some object that this title is not found in all Greek exemplars, nor in the Syriac version, yet a very many of the more correct Codices have it; so that most think it was added by John himself, not for the sake of boasting but of truth.

*Note X.* Although the noun *Theology* is not found in the abstract in Scripture as to its sound, yet as to its sense it is found there, inasmuch as Scripture employs various synonyms of equal force (*ἰσοδύναμα*) to this name. For it is called absolutely *σοφία*, *Wisdom* (1 Cor. 2:6); and with an added word, *σοφία θεοῦ*, *Wisdom of God* (v. 7); *λόγος σοφίας*, *word of Wisdom* (1 Cor. 12:8); *σοφία πολυποίκιλος*, *the manifold Wisdom* (Eph. 3:10); *γνῶσις τῆς σωτηρίας*, *knowledge of salvation* (Luke 1:77); *ἐπίγνωσις ἀληθείας τῆς κατ' εὐσέβειαν*, *the knowledge of truth which is according to godliness* (Titus 1:1); *ἔκθεσις τῆς τοῦ θεοῦ ὁδοῦ*, *the exposition of the way of God* (Acts 18:26); *ὑποτύπωσις ὑγιαινόντων λόγων*, *the pattern or form of sound words* (2 Tim. 1:13); *διδασκαλία κατ' εὐσέβειαν*, *doctrine which is according to godliness* (1 Tim. 6:3); *θεοδιδασκαλία* (1 Thess. 4:9); and *φρόνημα τοῦ πνεύματος*, *the mind of the Spirit* (Rom. 8:6).

##### *Θέσις ΙΙ — Thesis II*

To the noun *Theology* there attaches a **double homonymy** or equivocation: one *pure*, the other *analogical*. The *pure* homonymy is when the name *Theology* is employed also of a false or merely opinable theology¹⁰ — for example: of the *pagan mythology* (*μυθολογία*) of the Ethnics; of the *pseudology* (*ψευδολογία*) of the Jews and Turks; of the *emptylogy* (*ματαιολογία*) of the Heretics; of the *portent-logy* (*τερατολογία*) or prodigious Theology of the Scholastics and the *Quodlibetarians*; and of the theology either of fictitious gods, or of gods falsely so-called (thus the Theology of the Ethnics or Gentiles), or of things which are handed down falsely concerning the true God (thus the Theology of the Jews, Samaritans, Turks, Heretics, and Pseudo-Christians).

*Note IV.* The form of the Theology of the Gentiles or the Ethnics consists (1) in *πολυθεΐα* (*polytheism*), or the fiction of many gods, and in the worship and adoration of creatures and of demons under statues and idols; (2) in the turpitude and foulness of morals and life. It is not so much a Theology or knowledge of God, as an *ignorance of the true God* (Gal. 4:8) and *ἀθεολογία* (Eph. 2:12).

*Note V.* The principle of the Ethnic Theology, as such, consists in the oracles and responses of demons, the auguries of soothsayers, the presages of dreams, and the divinations of magicians. See **Vossius**, *Of the Idolatry of the Gentiles*.

*Note VI.* The *ψευδολογία* of the Jews, the *Talmudic*, is that which, having spurned the books of the New Testament, receives only the Old Testament — but depraves it with Talmudic traditions and Rabbinic fictions, perverts the sense of many sayings of Scripture, repudiates the true Messiah and Redeemer of the world (who has long since been manifested in the flesh), curses him with horrid blasphemies, and awaits another to come. In one word: the Theology of the Talmudists and the Rabbis does not heal, but is insane — for (1) it adulterates and perverts the biblical sayings; (2) it contains blasphemous utterances; and (3) it hawks about innumerable Aesopic emptynesses.

*Note VII.* The principle and norm of the Theology and Religion of the Jews is the **Talmud** (Heb. *תלמוד*, "the body of doctrine," from the root *לָמַד* "he learned," in *Piel* "he taught"), the book of instruction of the Jews, embracing all their divine and human science, gathered from the sentences of many Rabbis, and filled with many and various fables and corruptions of the Scriptures. Of it there are two parts: the **Mishnah**, that is, secondary and oral, or the text; and the **Gemara**, that is, the explication. And there is a double Talmud: the **Jerusalem**, composed at Tiberias in the third century after the birth of Christ; and the **Babylonian**, which began in the fourth century, but has depressed the other by its dignity. Concerning the difference between the Jerusalem and the Babylonian *Mishnah*, "in words the least, in things the greatest, they are," says **John Lightfoot** (*Hebraic Horae*, Chorographic Century upon Matthew, chap. 81, p. 143, Leipzig edition).

*Note VIII.* The Samaritan Theology was neither wholly Israelite, nor wholly Ethnic, but a certain sort of monster concreted from a various admixture of religions. For the Samaritans worshipped alike the true God and the false Deities of the Gentiles, and mingled Jewish ceremonies with Gentile superstitions (2 Kings 17:32 and following); they received only the Pentateuch, the five books of Moses, having rejected all the rest, both Historical and Prophetic — which some understand only of a rejection of reading in the Synagogues.

*Note IX.* The *ματαιολογία* of Muhammed acknowledges as its author the pseudoprophet and impostor Muhammed, who — having scattered his detestable dogma — began to collect followers in the seventh century after the birth of Christ. Its norm and principle is the **Alkoran** (whose handling they concede to no one save Muhammedans, and these washed and clean; whence they inscribe upon its cover the words *Let no man touch it save the clean*). It is a book most full of absurdities and inepties, patched together from the Talmudic books of the Jews, from Muhammedan lies, and from the dreams of Heretics — Arians, Nestorians, Manicheans, and likewise Epicureans, etc. See **Hottinger**, *Oriental History*, book I, in which he treats of Muhammedanism and of its procreating and preserving causes.

*Note X.* Pseudo-Christian Theology is properly that of those who impugn especially the article of the Most Holy Trinity of Persons in one divine essence — the *foundation* of Christianity — and Baptism, the *door* of the same; who deny the merit and satisfaction of Christ, and (so far as in them lies) overthrow it, and yet lurk under the shield of the Christian title. Such are the Socinians, the Anabaptists, and the Tremulants or Quakers in England.

*Note XI.* The *ματαιολογία* of the Heretics is that which either directly and expressly, or by prone and proximate consequence, denies and impugns one or several articles of faith necessary to salvation to be believed, and defends and champions other *morbid, pestilent, and mortiferous* dogmas — as **Augustine** puts it (*Of the City of God* XVIII. 51). Heresy therefore is an error contrary to divinely revealed truth, and overthrowing the foundation of salvation.

*Note XII.* The *τερατολογία* or prodigious and thorny **Scholastic Theology** — arisen at Paris of the Parisians with Peter Lombard for father and educated in the Sorbonne — has been at last disseminated through the whole Western Church since the thirteenth century from the birth of the Saviour, and dispersed. What is it (as **Luther** *pathetically* [i.e. with pathos] calls it) but *the sect of Robbers, the ignorance of Truth, and the utter emptiness of the Word*?¹¹ For in the highest mysteries of faith the Scholastics summon their conclusions from the principles of Logic, Physics, and Metaphysics — with the passages of Scripture touched pragmatically and with a light hand.

*Note XIII.* **Syncretistic Theology** is the confusion of religions and worships, or the commingling of diverse religions arisen from carnal reason and the untimely study of peace — laying it down that a fraternal, and that spiritual, communion in Christ is to be entered upon and cultivated with Papists and Calvinists, even though they still retain, defend, and champion their errors, and reprobate, hound down, and impugn the celestial truth. Syncretism, which is the very soul of Arminianism, places it as a foundation that "the dissent does not concern the foundation of faith, and therefore Reformed and Papists can be tolerated with faith and salvation safe."¹²

*Note XIV.* **Sceptic** or Doubting Theology is the Theology of the *Neutrals* or *Libertines*, who determine nothing and set up nothing — now nothing, now everything they believe; now this, now that they embrace, as occasion or necessity requires. This Theology is also called *Placentine*, *versatile*, *worldly*, *political*, *πρόσκαιρος* (*temporary*), *hourly*, or (better) *cothurnate* [i.e. actor-like, switching sides].¹³

##### *Θέσις ΙΙΙ — Thesis III*

According to that homonymy which we called *analogical*, true Theology is distinguished into **archetypal** (*ἀρχέτυπον*, or *πρωτότυπον* — that is, *exemplary*), and **ectypal** (*ἔκτυπον*, that is, *expressed and effigied*).

- **Archetypal Theology** is essentially in God, and is that same infinite Wisdom of God by which God knows himself in himself (for the omniscient GOD cannot be ignorant of himself in any way) and all things outside himself by himself — with an actual, indivisible, and immutable act of knowing.
- **Ectypal Theology** is nothing other than a certain expressed and rather adumbrated image and effigy of that infinite and essential Theology, graciously communicated by GOD out of mere goodness to intelligent creatures according to their measure, either in this age communicated, or in the future to be communicated.

*Note I.* This distinction is not that of a univocal thing in its univocates, but of an analogical thing in its analogates. For *ἀρχέτυπος* Theology is *first and primarily* called Theology (*πρώτως*): for it is that very wisdom existing in the divine mind, essential to the nature of God, uncreated, infinite. *Ectypal* Theology, however, is *secondarily and second-place* called Theology (*δευτέρως*), namely as expressed from that Wisdom, and as formed toward it as toward a divine and immutable exemplar; and it is secondary, created, finite. *Archetypal* Theology is substance, and as to the thing itself, the infinite essence of God itself; *ectypal* is a *habit* (*habitus*), and is contained in the genus of quality.

*Note II.* *Archetypal* Theology is the origin and principle of all wisdom; *ectypal* is *its outflowing and out-shining* (*ἀπόρροια καὶ ἀπαύγασμα*), or effigy. That is the *undoctored Wisdom* (*σοφία ἀδίδακτος*); this is *the word of taught Wisdom* (*διδακτῆς σοφίας λόγος*), which is taught and learned (1 Cor. 2:13). That is most perfect, wholly at once, and pure act (*actus purissimus*); this is imperfect, partial, discrete, habitual.

## From Chapter I

⁹ *λόγος* / *λόγιον* / *λόγια θεοῦ* — Quenstedt's philological argument turns on the distinction between the *speech of God* (*λόγος θεοῦ*, that which God utters — namely Scripture) and *speech concerning God* (*λόγος περὶ τοῦ θεοῦ*, which is theology). The distinction is Aristotelian in shape (subjective vs. objective genitive) but late-orthodox in application: it grounds the priority of Scripture over the theological discipline. The *principiate* (*principiatum*) is Quenstedt's term of art for anything that flows from a principle; hence theology is the principiate, Scripture the principle. Cf. the discussion of *principium theologiae* at Chapter III, and the entries in the working glossary.

¹⁰ *homonymy* — the ancient rhetorical-logical term for the case where a single name is used of two things that are not the same in substance (as in Aristotle, *Categories* 1a1). *Pure* homonymy (in Quenstedt's use) is bare equivocation — the two things share only a name; *analogical* homonymy is where the name is transferred by some real proportion or relation, and it is this latter that grounds the *archetypa* / *ectypa* distinction in Thesis III. Quenstedt's typology in the ten *Notae* of Thesis II — pagan, Talmudic, Muhammedan, Samaritan, pseudo-Christian, heretical, scholastic, syncretistic, sceptic — is the standard late-orthodox taxonomy of *false theologies* against which the Lutheran *theologia vera* stands.

¹¹ Luther's *pathetice loquens* — Quenstedt is likely paraphrasing rather than citing verbatim; the *sect of Robbers, ignorance of Truth, and emptiness of the Word* combines several standard Lutheran invectives against the *Sententiarii* into a single phrase. The base is Luther's early *Disputatio contra Scholasticam Theologiam* (1517, WA 1:224–228) and the recurring polemic of the Table Talk. That Lombard is treated as "father" and the Sorbonne as "educator" of the whole scholastic enterprise is a genealogical simplification standard in Lutheran anti-scholastic polemic.

¹² *Syncretism* — the technical term for the Helmstedt-Königsberg project of doctrinal reconciliation with the Reformed and (in some versions) with Rome, associated with **Georg Calixt** (1586–1656). The clause "the dissent does not concern the foundation of faith" is a fair summary of Calixt's *consensus quinquesaecularis* argument — that the first five centuries of the Church's teaching define the *fundamental articles* and that later divergences are non-fundamental. Quenstedt's father-in-law Calov led the anti-Calixtine polemic, and Quenstedt inherited it; the *Consensus Repetitus Fidei Vere Lutheranae* (Wittenberg 1655) — of which Calov was the principal architect — is the confessional-orthodox response. See the parallel [[calixt-apparatus-1628]] work in this program for Calixt's own architectonic; the polemical response is the [[dannhauer-mysterium-syncretismi-1648]].

¹³ *Cothurnate Theology* — from *cothurnus*, the high boot worn by actors in classical tragedy and, by metonymy, the actor himself. Voetius (see *Selectae Disputationes Theologicae* III, p. 829 following) uses the metaphor of the actor who wears now this shoe, now that — the Sceptic theologian who takes now one side, now another, as pragmatic necessity dictates. Quenstedt's placement of Sceptic theology at the end of his taxonomy is deliberate: it is the ultimate degeneration of the very notion of theology as *habitus*, because it will not commit to any *habitus* at all.
